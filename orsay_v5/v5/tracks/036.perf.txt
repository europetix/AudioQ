[lightly] In the same spring that Renoir was painting his dance, the Seine walked into Port-Marly and stayed for a while.

Sisley had moved to Marly-le-Roi two years earlier. When the river flooded Port-Marly in the spring of 1876, he went out to paint it. Not once. He made six pictures of that flood. This is one of them.

[curious] Look at how he splits the work in two. The sky and the water take up most of the canvas, and he paints them in light, broad strokes. Then the house. It stands at an angle, and its pink and yellow walls are painted with real precision. Everything that moves is loose. The one thing that stays put is firm. And the boat of the title sits where the street should be.

[pause] There's a small story in who owned it first. Its first owner was a man named Hoschedé. His wife was called Alice. Years later, Alice Hoschedé married Claude Monet. So this quiet flood scene began life in a household that would end up joined to Monet's.

After that, it passed through the dealer Durand-Ruel to the collector Isaac de Camondo, who left it to the nation in his will. You'll meet his name again in the next room.
