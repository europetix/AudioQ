[wry] When Paris first saw this painting, it laughed at the dog.

[pause] If this wall is empty — Arearea travels to Tokyo this winter — look in these rooms for Femmes de Tahiti, Tahitian Women, if it is on display, and listen there. You'll know it by its heavy, still women, the one on the right in a strict mission dress.

Gauguin showed Arearea in Paris in November 1893, with the pictures from his first stay in Tahiti. What people noticed was a red dog, and it drew a good deal of sarcasm.

The jokes didn't change his mind, for he thought Arearea one of his best works. In 1895, before he left Europe for good, he bought it back himself.

[curious] So picture what he couldn't let go of. Two women seated at the centre, and a tree cutting across the whole scene. The red dog, right in the foreground. And behind them, women worshipping a great statue.

That statue is invented. Gauguin took a small Maori motif, enlarged it to the size of a great Buddha, and imagined a sacred rite around it.

Keep that in mind with everything Gauguin told about Tahiti. His book Noa Noa reads like a memoir, but it's a romanticised account, reworked with Charles Morice. Even the ages he gave for the young Tahitian girls in his life come from his own telling. They should be heard that way, and not as fact.

[warmly] If you're standing at Femmes de Tahiti, it comes from the start of that same first stay. Look at the women at their simple daily tasks, heavy and still, each in her own space. Their faces are like masks, touched with melancholy. In a later version he swapped the strict mission dress of the woman on the right for a flowered pareo.

He had come, he said, "to live there in ecstasy, calm and art". [quietly] Arearea is that wish, painted. Perhaps that's why he wanted it back.

Now walk on towards the Nabi rooms and find Paul Sérusier's very small painting on wood, Le Talisman. It's easy to walk past, so look closely, and play its track there.
