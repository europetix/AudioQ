[reverent] Two faces, both with their eyes closed. One belongs to a living girl, and the other to a dead poet. And they look strangely alike.

This is Gustave Moreau's Orphée, Orpheus, painted in 1865 on a wooden panel. In the myth, the poet Orpheus was killed by the Maenads. Moreau doesn't show the killing, only what comes after. A young Thracian girl, dressed in Oriental finery, holds the poet's head, resting on his lyre. She gazes down at it with a melancholy air.

[quietly] Look at the two faces together. Strangely similar, with their closed eyes, they seem absorbed in infinite contemplation. It's a gruesome subject, and yet the scene is calm, mysteriously free of morbidity.

Then let your eye travel past them. Behind the figures there's a twilight glow, over a fantastic landscape that the museum calls worthy of Leonardo da Vinci. Moreau gives you a long way to look, and very little noise.

He showed the painting at the Salon of 1866. That July, the State bought it from him, straight from the Salon. [pause] And it did more than sell. It established Moreau as a decisive figure in Symbolism.

[warmly] In other hands, a severed head and a lyre would make a horror story. Moreau turned them into a silence.
