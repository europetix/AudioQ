[amused] The man who ordered these little heads is one of them. His nickname is the Toothless Laugher.

He was Charles Philipon, and in the early eighteen thirties he ran satirical papers, La Caricature and Le Charivari, against the government of the July Monarchy. That's the same regime Couture would needle with his Roman orgy, fifteen years later. Philipon asked Honoré Daumier to model the public men of the day.

Daumier made them in clay and never fired them in a kiln. Instead, he painted the raw clay in oil, and used the heads as models for his lithographs. Of the forty or so that were made, [pause] thirty-six have survived, and every one of them is in this museum.

[conspiratorial] They are called Celebrities of the Juste Milieu, and not simply parliamentarians, for a good reason. Only twenty-six of these men actually held a seat. The rest were simply famous enough to mock.

Look closely, because they are small: Philipon's head is about sixteen centimetres high, and Guizot's about twenty-two. Among the nicknames the museum records, Guizot is the Bore and Dupin is the Preacher, so look for them if they are on show. Daumier didn't need captions, because the faces do the work.

Unbaked clay is fragile, and these heads have needed care. In the nineteen twenties a sculptor repaired them, before bronze casts were made. The museum bought several of them in nineteen eighty.
