[wry] A toad in a sack. A block of salt caught in a shower. A menhir. A snowman. Those are some of the things critics called this statue when Paris first saw it.

The story starts well. In 1891, Émile Zola, the writer whose portrait by Manet you may have seen downstairs, had just been elected president of the Société des gens de lettres, the writers' society. The society wanted a monument to Honoré de Balzac, almost half a century after his death. Zola chose Rodin.

Seven years later, Rodin showed this plaster at the Salon of 1898, and the critics poured scorn on what they called a formless block. [pause] The society refused it. It had asked for a realistic likeness, in the usual commemorative style, and Rodin had given it neither. The commission went to another sculptor, Falguière.

[quietly] What Rodin did next tells you how much he believed in it. He kept the statue. He gave back the money. And he refused every offer to buy it.

Now look up at the whole figure, almost three metres of plaster. You can see what upset the critics. There's no tidy portrait of an author at his desk. There's a single great mass, as if the statue were still wrapped, and Balzac's head at the top of it.

[conspiratorial] And how did Rodin get that mass? François Pompon, the sculptor of the polar bear, left an account. According to him, Rodin dipped his dressing gown in a big tub of plaster, and used it to clothe his study for the figure. That is Pompon's memory of it. But stand here for a minute, and it's not hard to imagine.

[warmly] The monument did reach the street in the end. A bronze was unveiled on the boulevard Raspail in 1939, forty-one years after the Salon. The snowman, it turns out, never melted.

On this terrace, look for a seated woman in marble by Maillol, and Bourdelle's gilded bronze archer drawing his bow. Play their track there.
