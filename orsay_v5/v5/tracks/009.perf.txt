[warmly] When Millet explained this painting, he didn't talk about faith. He talked about his grandmother.

Years after he painted it, he put it like this, in the museum's translation. [pause] "L'Angélus is a painting I created thinking about how, when working in the fields long ago, my grandmother never failed, upon hearing the church bell ring, to make us stop our work to recite the Angelus for those poor souls."

The Angelus is a prayer that recalls the Annunciation. The museum is careful to say this is a childhood memory, not religious fervour. Millet himself was not a practising Catholic.

Look how little he gives you. It's a small picture, smaller than most people expect. A man and a woman have stopped in the middle of the potato harvest. Around them are their tools: a fork, a basket, sacks, a wheelbarrow. Nothing is happening. That's the point. The work has stopped because a bell rang.

[curious] And then this quiet picture had a very noisy life. In eighteen eighty-nine it was sold at auction to the American Art Association. Soon after, the collector Alfred Chauchard owned it, and he left it to the Louvre. In the twentieth century it became an icon around the world. Salvador Dalí venerated it. And in nineteen thirty-two, a disturbed man slashed it.

[quietly] All of that, for two people standing still in a field, because an old woman had taught a boy to stop and listen.
