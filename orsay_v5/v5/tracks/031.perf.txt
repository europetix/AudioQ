[conspiratorial] This class never took place, not like this anyway. Degas invented it, the way a stage designer invents a set.

The teacher, though, is real: Jules Perrot, a famous ballet master, and Degas painted him as a portrait. But the scene around him is imaginary. It's set in the rehearsal room of the old Opéra on the rue Le Peletier. That's not Garnier's building, the one you saw in model form downstairs, but the older house. And by the time Degas was at work on this, that opera house had already burned down.

[pause] So a real man is teaching a class that never happened, in a room that no longer existed.

Who wanted it? A singer, as it happens. The painting was commissioned by Jean-Baptiste Faure, the opera baritone, who also owned Manet's Déjeuner sur l'herbe.

[amused] Now, the best part is the dancers. The class is ending, and they are exhausted. Look along the room and you'll find them stretching, fixing their hair, tugging at their clothes. One of them twists round to scratch her back. See if you can find her. It's the least balletic gesture in the room, and Degas put it in on purpose.

That's his trick: he gives you the grand old rehearsal room and the famous teacher. And then he shows you what the end of a long class really feels like. Tired girls, and an itch.

Next door, in room 32, a real dancer is waiting: Degas's sculpture, the Petite danseuse de quatorze ans. Start her track when you reach her.
