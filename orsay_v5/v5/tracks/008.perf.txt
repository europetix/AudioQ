[quietly] These three women are working for leftovers.

[pause] If the wall in front of you is empty, the painting is away on loan. Walk on to the Millet rooms and find his small painting of a man and a woman pausing in a potato field, L'Angélus. Play this story there. It belongs to both pictures.

Once the harvest was in, women like these were allowed into the fields at sunset. They picked up, one stalk at a time, the wheat the harvesters had missed. That is gleaning.

Jean-François Millet didn't arrive at this picture quickly. The museum calls it the crowning of ten years of research on the gleaners. Ten years, for one moment of three women at work. He showed it at the Salon of eighteen fifty-seven.

[curious] Now look past them. Behind the women, the harvest itself is going on. That's where the wealth of this field is, pushed back into the distance. In front, close to you, there are only the gleaners and the ground. Millet doesn't make a speech about it. He simply lets you notice where he has put the plenty, and where he has put the women.

It isn't a huge canvas, about a metre wide. It passed through several collections. Then, in eighteen ninety, Madame Pommery gave it to the nation, keeping the right to enjoy it for the rest of her life.

If you are hearing this in front of L'Angélus instead, you're with a close relative. Millet began that painting in the same years. Another field, a man and a woman, another pause in the work. But there, the pause is for a prayer.

[warmly] Three women, one field, ten years of looking. Millet asks for nothing more than your attention.

Now find L'Angélus in the Millet rooms, the small canvas of a man and a woman pausing in a field. If you're already there, play its track now.
