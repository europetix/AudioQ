[lightly] When this railway station became a museum, the architects made a choice. They would keep some of the station showing. The metal structure. One of the seven domes. And the clocks.

The station was built for the World's Fair of 1900, and its great clocks look out over the quai and the river. In the museum's archive there are photographs of one of them being restored and repaired from the quai below, using a telescopic arm. A station clock, treated like a work of art.

Behind one of those clocks, right here at the exit of the gallery, is the Café Campana. It opened in 2011, when this gallery was renovated. [amused] And it's a surprise. Its designers, the Brazilian brothers Campana, imagined it as an underwater world, a homage to the glassmaker Émile Gallé. Look in at the curving, organic seats, the golden chandeliers like fish scales, the hints of orange coral. A sea floor, at the top of a railway station.

[warmly] It's also a good moment to breathe. Since the clock at the start of this level, you've walked from the first Impressionists to Van Gogh, Seurat, Gauguin and the Nabis. That's a great deal of looking. Gallé himself is waiting for you downstairs, along with the Nabis' big decorations.

[pause] When you're ready, take the lift or the stairs down to level two. Find room 68 and Toulouse-Lautrec's tall, narrow painting of a woman dancing alone. Play its track there.
