[lightly] This jungle was grown in Paris, in a greenhouse.

The picture travels, though. If it isn't on the wall, go straight on to room 45 and Sérusier's Le Talisman.

Henri Rousseau did not make his jungles from travel. He made them from studies at the Natural History Museum, and in the hothouses of the Jardin des Plantes. Out of those, he built this.

[curious] It began as a commission. Robert Delaunay's mother ordered it after a trip to India. Delaunay was one of Rousseau's most fervent admirers. So were Alfred Jarry, Apollinaire, Picasso and André Breton.

Now look. A dark-skinned figure, neither clearly man nor woman, plays a flute. The snakes answer, and so does the whole natural world around. The museum's own commentary calls the figure "a black Eve in a disquieting Garden of Eden, charming a snake as terrifying as the serpent in Genesis was seductive."

[pause] See how the colour is lit from behind, bright and dense. One part of the picture is very bright, the other very dark. The drawing is precise and naïve at once. And notice how figure, animals and fantastic plants all get exactly the same treatment.

That strangeness carried a long way. The backlit colours seem to look ahead to Magritte. The painting went on to inspire whole generations of artists, the Surrealists above all.

[warmly] The collector Jacques Doucet owned it, alongside Picasso's Demoiselles d'Avignon. It came to the national collections as his bequest, in nineteen thirty-six.

Now walk on into room 45, Redon and the Nabis, and find Paul Sérusier's very small painting on wood, Le Talisman. Play its track there.
