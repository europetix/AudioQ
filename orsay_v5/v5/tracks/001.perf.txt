[warmly] In nineteen seventy-one, someone wanted to put a big international hotel on this spot. The building permit was refused. Without that refusal, there would be no museum.

It began as a railway station, built for the World's Fair of nineteen hundred. The Orléans railway company chose the architect Victor Laloux, and in just two years he raised a station and a hotel. They opened on the fourteenth of July, nineteen hundred.

[amused] Laloux had a delicate problem. His station had to sit beside the Louvre and the Tuileries without embarrassing anyone. So he built it of metal, some twelve thousand tonnes of it, and then hid all that metal behind cut stone. The disguise worked. The painter Édouard Detaille wrote, "The station is superb and looks like a Palais des beaux-arts."

Its life as a great terminus was short. Electric traction and longer trains made its platforms too short, and from nineteen thirty-nine it served only the suburbs.

[quietly] The war gave it sadder work. Parcels for prisoners were sent out from here. And at the Liberation, this was where prisoners and deportees arrived home from Germany.

After that, it took whatever work it could find. Orson Welles filmed The Trial here. A theatre company moved in, and so did auctioneers. The big hotel plan was turned down, and the station's own hotel closed. Paris had just lost Baltard's market halls at Les Halles, and that loss changed how people saw buildings like this. The station was protected as a historic monument. And at the initiative of Valéry Giscard d'Estaing, the government decided to make it a museum.

A team of architects adapted Laloux's building, and Gae Aulenti led the design of the interiors. [pause] President Mitterrand inaugurated the museum on the first of December, nineteen eighty-six. Eight days later, the public walked in.

[lightly] Forty years on, the station still hasn't quite left. The architects kept its metal structure and its great clocks in plain view. You'll meet both on the way.
