[amused] François Pompon became famous at sixty-seven. Until then, he had spent years carving marble for other sculptors.

He was one of the most sought-after practitioners in Paris, carving marble for other sculptors. Among them were Rodin, and Camille Claudel, whose bronze you've just seen. Then, from 1905, he turned against Rodin's expressive style, and away from the human figure altogether. He went to the Jardin des Plantes in Paris and watched the animals.

His polar bear was first shown in plaster at the Salon d'Automne of 1922. It gave him his first public success. This version is in stone, about two and a half metres long. The State commissioned it in 1927, and his own practitioner, Jean-Joachim Supéry, carved it at Malakoff. [pause] So the man who had carved for Rodin now had someone carving for him.

[curious] Pompon described his method like this. "I keep a large number of details destined to disappear. I do the animal with almost all its trappings. And then little by little, I eliminate…"

Take a slow walk along the bear with that in mind. Ask yourself what he took away, and how much bear is still left.

[warmly] Pompon has one more part to play on this terrace. He was the source of a famous story about Rodin's Balzac, and it's a good one.

On the same terrace, find Rodin's Balzac, a towering plaster figure of the writer. Play its track there.
