[curious] In the spring of eighteen sixty-five, the young Claude Monet started the biggest painting of his youth. The canvas measured more than four metres by six. Its subject was a picnic on the grass. He had Manet's Déjeuner sur l'herbe, the painting by Manet you have already met, very much in mind. Monet meant his picnic as both a tribute and a challenge.

He never finished it. A year later, just before the Salon, he gave up. [pause] And then the canvas had a very unglamorous life.

[wry] Monet left it with his landlord, as security for the rent. When he finally got it back, in eighteen eighty-four, it was mouldy. So he cut it up, and kept three pieces.

Two of those pieces are here. The large one is the centre of the composition, almost two and a half metres tall. Hanging to its left is the left-hand fragment. They reached the nation by different roads, thirty years apart, one as a gift and one in payment of tax. The third piece is not in this museum.

Stand back and picture the rest around them, the whole canvas at full size. That's the scale a young painter thought he needed to take on Manet.

[quietly] It's strange to admire a painting its own artist took apart. But these pieces catch a young painter at the very moment he dared to answer Manet at full size. He'd find other ways to make his point.

In this same room, look for a much smaller canvas, a field of red poppies with a woman holding a sunshade. That's Monet's Coquelicots. Press play when you're in front of it.
