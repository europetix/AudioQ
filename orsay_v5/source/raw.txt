Track 01

Welcome and Navigation Guide
Before you go in · How to use this tour
4 min

◆ Stop & Look

Anywhere convenient before you reach the museum doors — on the metro to Solférino, walking
along the Seine, or already at the Esplanade Valéry Giscard d’Estaing in front of the building.
Take a moment with this welcome track before you cross into the entrance queue. The first
content track begins inside, under the glass roof.

Welcome to Paris. And welcome to the Musée d’Orsay — the train station that became
the museum of the modern eye.
What you’re about to walk through is the world’s most concentrated collection of art
from 1848 to 1914. Sixty-six years. The period when European painting tore itself apart
and rebuilt itself into something that could carry the modern century. Courbet, Manet,
Whistler, Monet, Renoir, Van Gogh, Cézanne, Gauguin, Seurat. All of them. Under one
glass roof.
The cover of this script carries a line from Émile Zola, written in 1866 when he was
defending Manet against critics who wanted to destroy him. Notre devoir est d’aller au
fond des choses. Our duty is to go to the bottom of things. That’s the spirit of the whole
museum. The painters in this building stopped painting what was expected of them and
started painting what was actually in front of them — in a railway station, in a Paris café,
on a harvest field, on a body that wasn’t a goddess. Whatever the subject, the impulse
was the same. Go to the bottom of things.

What you’re visiting in 2026-2028
Important first thing to know: the museum is in the middle of a major renovation that
runs from 2026 to 2028. The chief architect leading the work is Marie-Suzanne de
Ponthaud, chief architect of historic monuments. The forecourt is being rebuilt — the
original 1980s polychrome floor by the French artist Guy de Rougemont, in Vosges
granite and coloured marble, is being restored to its first finish. The glass canopy over
the building is being restored. The entry and exit have been separated, after studies of
visitor flow concluded the old single-point system wasn’t working. The famous animal
sculptures that used to greet visitors on the esplanade — the rhinoceros by Jacquemart,
the elephant by Frémiet, the horse by Rouillard — have been temporarily removed for
restoration. They’re on long-term loan at the Jardin des Plantes on the other side of the

Getting in
The easiest way is metro line 12 to Solférino, then a five-minute walk to the Seine. RER
C stops directly at Musée d’Orsay. Two entrances under the renovation: Entrance 1 (the
Seine quay side) for timed-ticket holders, Entrance 2 (the forecourt) for priority visitors.
Booking online beats showing up cold every day of the week. The queue at the door
without a timed ticket can swallow an hour or more in summer.

Hours
Tuesday through Sunday, 9:30 in the morning to 6:00 in the evening. Closed Monday.
Late opening Thursday evenings until 9:45 p.m., which is the quietest time to see the
museum if your schedule allows it. Last entry one hour before closing. The rooms start
clearing thirty minutes before closing. Closed May 1st and December 25th.

How long this tour takes
Thirty-two tracks. About two and a half hours of narration. Add the walking, the standing
in front of paintings, the time you spend just looking — you’re looking at three to four
hours minimum. Most people end up staying longer than they planned. Bring water.
Wear shoes that can handle marble floors. There are benches in almost every room —
use them.

How the tour is shaped
Three phases, by floor. Phase one on the ground floor — the old train platform level —
covers the building itself and the painters who broke open the nineteenth century:
Couture, Millet, Courbet, Manet, Whistler, Toulouse-Lautrec, and the Symbolists. Phase
two on level two covers the sculpture terraces, the Pavillon Amont, the new
Nazi-restitution gallery, Van Gogh, and Gauguin. Phase three on level five is the
Impressionist heart of the museum — Monet, Renoir, Pissarro, Degas, Cézanne, Seurat
— ending in the Café Campana behind the second great clock face.
When you’re ready, walk into the museum, present your ticket, and step through into
the central nave. Press play on Track Two once you’re standing under the glass roof and
you can see the great clock at the far end.

Laloux’s Station
Under the glass roof · The building that nearly wasn’t a museum
5 min

◆ Stop & Look

You’re inside the museum, in the central nave. The huge vaulted glass ceiling is above you.
Sculptures line the central walkway. Walk to a position where you can see down the full length of
the nave — from the entrance side to the far wall with the great clock. Then start the track.

Look up. Take a moment with the ceiling before you take a moment with anything else.
What you’re standing in is not a building that was designed as a museum. It’s a building
that was designed as a train station, rescued from demolition in the 1970s, and
converted into a museum in the 1980s. The conversion is one of the best decisions Paris
has made in the last fifty years.

Victor Laloux’s problem
The architect was Victor Laloux. The year was 1898. The Compagnie du Chemin de fer
de Paris à Orléans, the railway company for the southwest of France, needed a new
terminal station in the centre of Paris in time for the 1900 World’s Fair. The site they
chose was directly across the Seine from the Louvre and the Tuileries gardens — prime
real estate, surrounded by classical stone buildings and the Beaux-Arts establishment of
the Third Republic.
Laloux had a problem. A train station in 1900 meant steam, smoke, soot, iron, and the
kind of industrial architecture that had been raised in Paris around the Gare du Nord and
the Gare Saint-Lazare. He could not put a soot-stained iron warehouse directly opposite
the Louvre. The neighbourhood would not allow it, the Beaux-Arts academy would not
allow it, the state would not allow it.
So Laloux did something clever. He built two buildings in one. On the outside, a
Beaux-Arts palace — pale limestone, classical ornament, the kind of facade that
belonged on the Seine quay across from the Louvre. On the inside, the most advanced
train station of its era — a single vast glass-and-iron vault, 138 metres long, 40 metres
wide, electrified from the start. The first electrified urban rail terminal in Europe. No
steam to stain the stone. No smoke to choke the platforms.
Look up again. That barrel vault above you is the original 1900 structure. The glass roof
is a listed historic monument — it’s being restored as we speak, as part of the current
renovation. The decorative coffered rosettes you see in the ceiling are plaster, masking
the iron. The iron weighs about twelve thousand tons. The whole canopy is essentially

an Eiffel-Tower-scale industrial structure dressed up as a ballroom ceiling.

Forty years as a working station
The station opened on the 14th of July 1900. For forty years it was a working terminal —
trains from the southwest of France arriving at platforms right where you’re standing. In
1939 the platforms became too short for the longer electric trains then coming into
service, and most of the long-distance traffic was diverted to Austerlitz. The building
drifted out of railway use.
What happened to it next is a strange list. During the Second World War, it was a
mailing centre for parcels sent to French prisoners of war in Germany. After the
Liberation, it was a reception centre for returning French prisoners and deportees. In
1962, Orson Welles filmed parts of The Trial here, using the empty station hall as the
warped Kafkaesque court. By the 1970s, the building was a candidate for demolition. A
luxury hotel chain wanted to knock it down and put up a glass tower.

Saved
It was Georges Pompidou — the president of France from 1969 to 1974 — who first
proposed turning the station into a museum. Pompidou died before the plan was
realised, but Valéry Giscard d’Estaing, the next president, kept the project alive.
François Mitterrand, the next president after that, inaugurated the museum on the 9th
of December 1986. Three French presidents touched this project. The Italian architect
Gae Aulenti designed the interior — the stone terraces, the sculpture nave, the way the
light falls. That intervention is also now a listed heritage element. When the museum
talks about “blending heritage and modernity” in the current renovation, what they
mean is preserving Laloux’s 1900 bones and Aulenti’s 1986 conversion.
Now look down the nave toward the far wall. You can see the shape of the old train
platforms in the stepped stone terraces either side of the central walkway. The
sculptures are placed along what used to be the track bed. The far end of the nave leads
up to the great clock. We’ll get there at the start of Half B. First — the first painting on
this tour, a twenty-five-foot-wide canvas hanging here on level zero that taught a whole
generation of French painters what to rebel against. Walk to the central wall of the nave,
find Thomas Couture’s Romans of the Decadence, and stand about ten metres back.
Track Three begins there.

Track 03

Couture’s Romans of the Decadence
—7—

ORSAY

Musée d’Orsay · Paris

Central nave, level 0 · The peak of academic painting
5 min

◆ Stop & Look

Walk back into the central nave on level zero (the ground floor). Find the very large painting on
the central wall — about seven metres wide, a debauched Roman banquet scene with marble
columns, sprawled bodies, and tall white statues looking down on them. This is Thomas Couture’s
Romans of the Decadence. Stand about ten metres back so you can see the whole canvas.

This painting is enormous. Stand back. Let your eye take it in as one composition before
you start picking out the details.
What you’re looking at is Thomas Couture’s The Romans of the Decadence, painted
between 1844 and 1847. It is one of the few paintings in this museum that everyone in
1847 Paris agreed was a masterpiece. It won the gold medal at the 1847 Salon. The
French state bought it immediately. Théophile Gautier called it the most important
painting of the decade. Couture became one of the most famous painters in Europe
overnight.

What it shows
The scene is a debauched Roman feast, set inside a grand classical hall. In the centre,
exhausted aristocrats are draped across couches in various states of undress,
surrounded by spilled wine cups and discarded flower wreaths. The party is ending. The
bodies are slumped. The faces are blank or asleep or staring at nothing.
Now look at the columns. Between each column stands a tall white statue of one of the
founding heroes of the Roman Republic — the ancestors of these very Romans. The
statues are upright, noble, austere. They look down at the orgy below them with cold
stone faces. The contrast is the whole point: the great ancestors stand sober and
unmoving while the descendants collapse in their shadow.
On the far right, two figures stand apart from the party — soberly dressed philosophers,
looking at the scene with what Couture called “deep sadness.” They’re the moral
observers. They’re also, secretly, the painter’s view of his own century. Couture was
using ancient Rome as a coded warning about the France of King Louis-Philippe — the
wealthy bourgeoisie of the 1840s, getting fat on industrial money while the republic’s
founding ideals were forgotten. The 1848 revolution — which overthrew Louis-Philippe —
broke out within a year of this painting being unveiled.

Why this painting matters here

ORSAY

This is the painting that the Impressionists rebelled against. Look at how it’s made:
every figure is carefully posed, every fold of drapery is meticulously rendered, every
brushstroke is polished invisible. The lighting is theatrical. The composition is
symmetrical, classical, balanced. The subject is borrowed from history. The technique is
the technique you learned at the École des Beaux-Arts — the state academy where
French painters were trained, judged, and ranked.
Couture was the model. Hundreds of younger painters tried to paint like him. One of
those younger painters, briefly, was Édouard Manet — who studied in Couture’s studio
from 1850 to 1856 and then walked out, because he couldn’t stand the academic
training another year. We’ll see Manet’s response to this kind of painting later in this
tour, on the same floor, in two canvases that broke the Academy open.
I want to be careful here. The standard story of nineteenth-century French art tells you
that Couture and his Academy were the villains and the Impressionists were the heroes
who saved painting from them. That story is too simple. Couture was not a fraud. He
was a brilliant technician working inside a tradition that genuinely valued moral
seriousness, narrative depth, and craftsmanship. Many of the painters who later became
Impressionists trained inside that same tradition and continued to submit to the official
Salon for years. The break didn’t happen overnight. What changed between Couture and
Monet was not that one was good and the other was bad. It was what painters thought
painting was for. We’ll watch that change happen, painting by painting, all the way
through this tour.
Walk down the Lille side of the nave — the inland side, away from the Seine. Find the
gallery dedicated to academic painters of the 1850s and 60s, and beyond it the
Symbolist room — the Salle du Bord de l’eau. Track Four takes us into that world, briefly,
before we move into Realism and Impressionism.

Ingres and the Symbolists
Level 0, Lille side · The Source and the Salle du Bord de l’eau
5 min

—9—

ORSAY

Musée d’Orsay · Paris

◆ Stop & Look

Walk to the Lille side of the central nave on level zero — the side opposite the Seine. Look for the
academic painting room (Ingres, Cabanel, Bouguereau) and the adjoining Symbolist room called
the Salle du Bord de l’eau. We’re going to look at two works in two rooms — Ingres’ The Source
and Gustave Moreau’s L’Apparition.

Two rooms, two paintings. We’re going to move fast through both, because they’re both
side-trips from the main story of this museum — but they’re both essential context for
understanding what the Impressionists thought they were fighting against, and what
was happening parallel to Impressionism that was not the same thing at all.

Ingres’ The Source
First, find the standing nude with a water jar tipped over her shoulder. This is La Source
— The Source — by Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres. He started it in 1820 and finished it
in 1856. Thirty-six years on one painting. He was eighty-six years old when it was
unveiled.
Ingres was the most powerful painter in France for most of the 19th century. He led the
Neoclassical movement — the tradition that believed painting should imitate Greek and
Roman marble: cold, smooth, idealised, drawn before it’s coloured. Look at the surface
of The Source. The skin is impossibly polished. The contour line is razor-sharp. The
whole figure looks like she’s carved out of porcelain. Ingres said that drawing was “the
probity of art” — by which he meant the honesty, the spine, the discipline. Colour was
secondary. The Impressionists, half a century later, would reverse this completely.
Colour would be everything, and the contour line would dissolve into light. But while
Ingres was alive, his view was the orthodoxy.
Look at her face. It’s expressionless. That blankness is deliberate — Ingres wasn’t
painting a person, he was painting the idea of a nymph, the platonic form of a young
water spirit. The Impressionists would walk away from this kind of idealisation. They
wanted painted faces to be people. The Source is the wall they were pushing off from.

The Symbolist room
Now walk into the adjoining room — the Salle du Bord de l’eau, the Riverside Room,
dedicated to the Symbolist movement. You’ll see darker walls, smaller pictures, a
different atmosphere. The two key works in here are Gustave Moreau’s L’Apparition and
Odilon Redon’s Les Yeux clos — The Apparition and Closed Eyes.
Find Moreau’s L’Apparition first — the watercolour with Salome and a floating head. The
biblical story is brief: Salome dances for King Herod at his birthday feast, and as a
reward she asks for the head of John the Baptist on a platter. Moreau paints the moment

the head appears — a glowing severed head, haloed in light, floating in mid-air above
the dance floor. Salome stands frozen with her arm raised, jewelled and barely dressed,
transfixed by the vision.
This watercolour was unveiled at the 1876 Salon. It became instantly famous and
inspired a generation of late-19th-century writers and painters — most famously
Joris-Karl Huysmans, who wrote a long ecstatic passage about this painting in his 1884
novel À Rebours (Against Nature), and Oscar Wilde, who saw it in Paris in 1884 and went
home and wrote his play Salomé based on it. Moreau was running parallel to
Impressionism, not in opposition to it. While Monet was painting light on water, Moreau
was painting dreams, severed heads, femmes fatales, and biblical visions. Symbolism is
the other half of the late 19th century — the side that fed surrealism and decadence and
Freudian dream-imagery.
Look at Redon’s Les Yeux clos next to it — a single face with the eyes closed, floating in
a misty grey-blue field. Redon spent the first half of his career making dark charcoal
drawings of nightmares, then in his sixties switched suddenly to brilliant colour. Both are
here at Orsay. Closed Eyes is from 1890. The closed eyes are not death; they’re
inwardness. The painter is asking you to look at someone who is looking at something
inside themselves that you cannot see.
Walk back toward the Seine side of the nave. We’re going to Jean-François Millet next,
into the Barbizon room. The pivot from Ingres and Moreau to Millet is the pivot from the
idealised body and the dream to the dirty body and the harvest field.

Millet’s The Gleaners
Level 0, Seine side · Three women in a wheat field
5 min

◆ Stop & Look

Cross to the Seine side of the central nave on level zero. Find the Barbizon school room — the
painters who left Paris to paint rural life. The painting you’re looking for is Jean-François Millet’s
The Gleaners: three peasant women bent in a wheat field at the end of harvest, with golden
haystacks behind them.

Three women. A wheat field. Late afternoon light. Look at the painting before you read
the wall text.

ORSAY

Musée d’Orsay · Paris

This is Des Glaneuses — The Gleaners — painted by Jean-François Millet in 1857 and
shown at the Salon that year. It is now one of the most famous paintings in this
museum. It was, when it was first shown, one of the most hated paintings of the year.

What gleaning was
Look at what the women are actually doing. The wheat harvest is over — you can see
the haystacks rising behind them, the wagon loaded with grain, the supervisor on
horseback in the distance. The three women in the foreground are bent double, picking
up the leftover stalks that the harvesters dropped on the ground. This was called
gleaning — a legal right under French law that let the poorest people in a village walk a
harvested field after the landowner was done, and keep whatever scraps they could
find. A handful of broken stalks at the end of a day of bent-back work.
Look at their hands. Coarse. Sunburned. Look at their clothes — rough fabric, patched,
faded. Look at their faces — you can’t really see them, because their heads are bent.
They’re not individuals here. They’re a class. The rural poor of mid-19th-century France,
doing the work that fed Paris.

Why this painting scandalised people
Millet did something that the Paris art establishment found deeply uncomfortable. He
took the lowest agricultural workers in France and he painted them at the scale, with the
dignity, with the compositional weight that academic painters reserved for biblical
scenes, royal portraits, and ancient heroes. The three figures are arranged like a Greek
frieze. Their bent bodies form a slow rhythm across the canvas. The light is the warm,
gold-soaked light of late-day rural France — light Millet had been studying for years from
his cottage in the village of Barbizon. He made the gleaners monumental. He made
looking at them feel like an act of seeing something serious.
Conservative critics in 1857 called Millet a socialist agitator. They said the painting was
an indictment of French society. They pointed out the obvious contrast inside the frame
— the abundance of the harvest in the background, the desperation in the foreground —
and asked why anyone would want to hang such a thing on their wall.
Millet always denied that he was making a political statement. He said he was just
painting the work he’d grown up watching, in his own village, by people he knew. “I was
born a peasant,” he wrote, “and I will die a peasant.” But denying the politics didn’t
mean the politics weren’t in the painting. By taking the scale and the seriousness of
academic art and applying it to the people the academy ignored, he changed what
painting could be about.

What he opened up

Every painter we’re going to look at for the rest of this floor owes something to Millet.
Courbet, in the next room, will take this even further — he’ll paint a small-town funeral
at the scale of a coronation. Manet, two rooms over, will paint a Parisian sex worker with
the gravity of a Venus. The whole ground floor of this museum is built on the premise
Millet established in 1857: ordinary modern people are a legitimate subject for serious
art. You don’t need a Roman emperor or a biblical hero. The woman bent over picking
up stalks is enough.
Walk into the adjoining Courbet gallery. The two largest paintings in this museum are
next door, both by the same man, both produced in the same five-year window. We’re
going to look at them in sequence. Track Six starts in front of A Burial at Ornans.

Track 06

Courbet’s A Burial at Ornans
Level 0, Courbet gallery · The death of romantic painting
5 min

◆ Stop & Look

Walk into the Courbet gallery on level zero. Two enormous horizontal paintings dominate the
room — we’ll look at both. For this track, stand in front of the funeral scene: a line of townspeople
in black, a priest, an open grave, a grey winter sky over a small French town. This is A Burial at
Ornans.

Stand back. This painting is twenty-two feet wide. It needs distance to be seen as one
composition.
This is Gustave Courbet’s A Burial at Ornans, painted between 1849 and 1850 and
shown at the Salon of 1850-51. Courbet called it “the burial of Romanticism” — by which
he meant the burial of the kind of operatic, theatrical, idealised painting that had
dominated French art for the previous fifty years. He wasn’t exaggerating. With this
canvas, he killed a whole tradition. The Impressionists who followed him never had to
fight that battle, because Courbet had already won it.

What’s in the painting
A funeral in a small French town. The town is Ornans, in the Franche-Comté region of
eastern France — Courbet’s home town. The man being buried is Courbet’s great-uncle,
Claude-Étienne Teste. The mourners along the back are real people from Ornans —
Courbet’s neighbours, relatives, the local priest, the mayor, the local vineyard workers,

ORSAY

Musée d’Orsay · Paris

the women of the parish. Courbet painted them in his studio one by one, from memory
and from sittings, and assembled them into this single horizontal frieze.
Look at the bottom centre of the canvas. The open grave is right there, cutting into the
foreground, the dark rectangular hole inches from your feet. Courbet doesn’t hide it. He
doesn’t put a beam of heavenly light coming down from above. He doesn’t show angels
carrying the soul up. The grave is just an empty hole, waiting.
Look at the dog in the right foreground. A normal village dog, sniffing the dirt. Not
symbolic. Not allegorical. Just a dog at a funeral, the way dogs actually are at funerals in
small towns. Look at the gravedigger kneeling beside the hole. He’s waiting for the
priest to finish. Look at the sky — a grey, flat, mid-afternoon sky over a chalk cliff. No
drama. No weather. Just the weather you actually get in eastern France in October.

What the Salon of 1850 thought
The Salon jury argued for a long time about whether to accept this painting at all. The
scale was the first problem. A canvas of this size was reserved, by tradition, for what the
academy called peinture d’histoire — history painting. A coronation. A battle. The death
of Caesar. The crucifixion. Courbet had taken twenty-two feet of canvas and put a
small-town funeral on it. He had given monumental scale to an event that no one
outside Ornans would have heard of.
The second problem was the people. The mourners in the back row are not idealised.
The men have weathered faces, drinkers’ noses, stubble. The women are stocky and
middle-aged. The priest is fat. The mayor is bald. No one in the painting is beautiful in
the academic sense. They’re just the actual people who happen to live in Ornans,
France, in 1849.
Conservative critics called it “a celebration of ugliness.” One critic said Courbet had
“dragged painting into the mud.” Champfleury, defending Courbet, said he had “simply
painted what he saw.” The painting was both attacked and celebrated. But more
importantly: it could not be ignored. The Academy couldn’t pretend Courbet hadn’t
painted it. After this canvas, the question of what painting could be about was
permanently open.

Realism
Courbet gave his approach a name. He called himself a Realist. He wrote a manifesto: “I
cannot paint an angel because I have never seen one.” He meant it literally. He would
paint only what he could physically observe, at the scale he wanted, with the dignity he
chose to give it. The Realist movement that he started would feed directly into Manet
and the Impressionists, who took the same impulse — paint what you actually see, not
what tradition tells you to paint — and turned it into something even more radical.

Track 07

Courbet’s The Artist’s Studio
Same Courbet gallery · The real allegory of seven years
5 min

◆ Stop & Look

Stay in the same Courbet gallery on level zero. Turn to the companion painting — another huge
canvas, showing Courbet himself in the centre, seated at an easel, with a naked model standing
behind him and a small boy watching, surrounded by dozens of figures on either side. This is The
Artist’s Studio.

Stand back from this one too. It’s nearly twenty feet wide. And it’s one of the strangest
paintings in the museum.
Courbet painted this in 1855 and gave it the longest title in the history of art: The
Painter’s Studio: A Real Allegory Summing Up Seven Years of My Artistic and Moral Life.
“A real allegory” is a contradiction in terms — an allegory is an abstract symbolic image,
the opposite of real. Courbet knew that. He was being provocative. He wanted to make a
painting that was both: rooted in the actual people he knew, and at the same time a
statement about what painting was.

The three parts
The composition has three parts. In the centre: Courbet himself, seated at an easel,
painting a landscape of his home region. Behind him stands a naked female model with
a white sheet draped over her hip and her hand on his shoulder. In front of him, a small
boy in a peasant smock watches him paint.
To the left of Courbet: a crowd of common people. A poacher with his dogs. A canvas
merchant. A priest. A beggar woman sitting on the floor with a child. A worker in a
smock. Look at them — these are the people Courbet considered his real subject. The
rural and urban poor of the France of his lifetime.
To the right of Courbet: a crowd of his Parisian friends and supporters. Charles
Baudelaire — the poet, sitting at the far right reading a book. The wealthy patron Alfred
Bruyas, who had been bankrolling Courbet for years. The art critic Champfleury, sitting
on a stool. A couple of bohemian art lovers admiring a painting on the wall. These are

the people who understood his project.

The naked model and the boy
Now look at the central trio again. The naked model is not posing for the landscape
Courbet is painting. She’s just standing there, watching. Critics have been arguing about
who she’s supposed to represent for a hundred and seventy years. Truth? Nature? The
naked reality that the painter has to look at to do his work? Courbet himself called her
“the Muse” in his letters but never explained further. Whatever she is, she is there as a
kind of standing permission — the right of the painter to see things directly.
The small boy in the peasant smock is even stranger. He’s looking up at Courbet with
absolute attention. He’s not painted from any one model; he’s a generic peasant child.
Some critics have read him as the future viewer — the next generation of ordinary
people coming to see what painting can do. Courbet painting toward his audience, with
the audience already inside the painting watching him.

The 1855 rejection
Courbet submitted this canvas, along with thirteen other paintings, to the great
Universal Exhibition of 1855 — the world’s fair that Paris was hosting that year. The jury
accepted eleven of his paintings and rejected three, including this one and A Burial at
Ornans. Courbet was furious. He did something unprecedented. He built his own pavilion
across the road from the official exhibition, paid for it himself, called it the Pavilion of
Realism, and charged admission. He showed forty paintings in his own pavilion, with this
canvas as the centrepiece.
That move — a painter exhibiting his own work outside the official jury system, charging
the public to see it — is the birth of the independent modern art exhibition. Twenty
years later, the Impressionists would do exactly the same thing in 1874, in a former
photographer’s studio in central Paris, when they organised their own exhibition outside
the Salon and invented the name Impressionism. Courbet showed them how it was
done. The Pavilion of Realism is the prototype.

This is Olympia, painted by Édouard Manet in 1863. He didn’t submit it to the Salon for
two years. When it was finally shown, at the 1865 Salon, it produced what one
contemporary called “the greatest scandal of the century” in Parisian art. The painting
was hung high — above eye level, near the ceiling — partly to protect it from being
attacked by visitors with walking sticks and umbrellas. Two policemen were posted in
the room to keep order. Critics called the model “a female gorilla” and “the Queen of
Spades stepping out of the bath.” Manet went home shaken. His friend Baudelaire wrote
to him: “You are only the first in the decrepitude of your art” — which sounds like an
insult, but was Baudelaire’s way of saying the future belonged to him.

Two dates: 1863 and 1865
I want to clear up a date confusion that runs through a lot of writing about this painting.
Manet painted Olympia in 1863. The same year, he submitted a different painting to the
Salon — Le Déjeuner sur l’herbe, the picnic with the naked woman, which we’ll look at
next. Déjeuner was rejected by the jury and shown at the special Salon des Refusés —
the Salon of Rejected Works — that Napoleon III set up in 1863 for paintings the jury had
thrown out. Déjeuner caused the first Manet scandal. Then Manet held Olympia in his
studio for two years before showing it. The 1865 Salon is where Olympia exploded. Two
paintings, two scandals, two years apart. People often blur them. The dates matter.

Why she was scandalous
Manet was working in a tradition. Nude women on beds, looking at the viewer, had been
a subject in Western painting since the Renaissance. Titian’s Venus of Urbino, painted in
1538, is the famous prototype — and Olympia is directly modelled on it. Manet had been
to Florence, he had stood in front of the Titian, he had taken notes. So why did one
painting (the Titian) become a Renaissance masterpiece and the other (the Manet)
cause a riot in Paris?

The difference is the details. Titian’s figure is a goddess: Venus, classical, mythological,
set in a Renaissance interior, softly lit. Manet’s figure is a contemporary woman. The
black ribbon around her neck was a Parisian fashion accessory. The orchid in her hair,
the gold bracelet, the satin slippers — these were the accessories of a high-class
Parisian courtesan. The name “Olympia” was a known pseudonym for a sex worker in
Paris in the 1860s. Her left hand isn’t modestly placed — it’s firmly held, in control. She
isn’t inviting you. She’s weighing you up. The black cat at her feet, instead of the
sleeping dog at Venus’s feet, was the final detail. The implications didn’t need to be
spelled out.
Look at the maid behind her, holding the bouquet wrapped in paper. The maid was
painted from a model named Laure, a Black woman living in Paris in the 1860s — Manet
recorded her name in his sketchbook. Modern scholarship has rediscovered her, and
Orsay itself held a major exhibition in 2019 called Le modèle noir — The Black Model —
specifically to put Laure back into the painting’s history. The flowers she’s holding are a
gift from a client — wrapped in paper, casual, the sort of thing that gets delivered
between appointments.

What Manet was actually doing
The painting is not pornographic. It’s not even particularly erotic by the standards of its
century — the Salons were full of nudes far more sexually displayed than Olympia. What
scandalised people was the transparency. Manet refused the alibi. He wouldn’t pretend
his nude was a goddess. He wouldn’t even pretend she was beautiful in the academic
sense — her skin is flat-lit, almost greenish in places, with none of the soft rounded
modelling the Salon demanded. He painted her as the thing she actually was: a working
Parisian woman whose body was her trade, looking directly at the wealthy man who
would be the Salon’s typical viewer, and pricing him in real time. The wealthy Parisian
men who visited brothels at night and demanded purity in art by day found themselves
looking at their own client relationship, hung on a wall, in public, framed as art.
Manet wasn’t trying to start a revolution. He thought of himself as a serious painter
working in the great tradition of Velázquez and Goya. He wanted Salon recognition. He
wanted official acceptance. He didn’t get it. What he got, instead, was a generation of
younger painters — Monet, Renoir, Degas, Pissarro, Cézanne, all the people we’re going
to meet on level five — who looked at Olympia and understood that the rules were over.
After 1865, you could paint anything. The painters of light who came after Manet were
possible because Manet had done this first.

The Salon des Refusés
Manet submitted this canvas to the 1863 Salon. The jury rejected it. They rejected so
many paintings that year, in fact, that the Emperor Napoleon III intervened personally.
He decreed that the rejected works would be shown in a separate exhibition, in the
same building, at the same time — the Salon des Refusés, the Salon of Rejected Works.
Napoleon’s intention was conciliatory — a way to defuse the artists’ anger. The effect
was the opposite. Crowds came to the Salon des Refusés specifically to laugh at what
the jury had thrown out, and what they laughed at hardest was Manet’s picnic.

What people couldn’t accept
Look at the composition. Two men in fashionable Parisian suits — frock coats, beards,
soft hats — sitting on the grass in what appears to be a forest clearing. Between them
sits a naked woman, looking directly out at the viewer with an expression somewhere
between amusement and challenge. She has kicked off her clothes — they’re in a pile
next to her, with the picnic basket. Behind them, half-undressed, another woman bathes
in a stream.
The men are talking to each other. The woman is looking at you. None of them seem
bothered by her nakedness. The men are gesturing as if continuing a philosophical
conversation. The scene reads as a kind of relaxed afternoon at an artists’ outing in the
Bois de Boulogne — except the woman is naked and the men are not.

This was the offence. The Academy was perfectly comfortable with female nudity — as
long as the nude was a mythological figure (Venus, Diana, a nymph) in a clearly
imaginary setting. Manet refused both. The setting is a contemporary picnic. The woman
is a contemporary Parisian. She has no excuse for being naked — no swan
transformation, no bath of the goddess, no ancient myth. She’s just a woman who has
taken her clothes off in the woods with two clothed men. The scene reads, to a Parisian
in 1863, as either a brothel scene transplanted outdoors, or an art-school model session
pretending to be a picnic. Either way, the viewer is implicated. The viewer is the third
man, the one whose place at the picnic she’s waiting to fill.

The technical scandal
But the moral scandal was only half of it. The technical scandal was just as big. Look at
the lighting on the woman’s body. It’s flat. There are no soft transitions from light to
shadow — her body is lit from the front almost like a studio photograph, with the contour
line of her skin standing out hard against the dark forest behind her. The Academy
demanded smooth chiaroscuro, the gradual modelling of form, the polished surface that
made painted skin feel three-dimensional. Manet refused that too.
Look at the woman bathing in the background. She’s too big. Spatially, she’s in the
wrong scale for her position in the frame — she should be smaller. Manet did this
deliberately. He was telling the viewer: this is a flat painted surface, not a window into a
real space. The painting refuses the illusion. It says, openly, I am paint on canvas. Look
at me as paint on canvas.
The Impressionists got it. Three or four years later, the younger painters — Monet,
Renoir, Bazille — were already experimenting with the same idea: paintings that didn’t
hide that they were paintings. Surfaces of visible brushstroke. Light that came from the
actual sun, not from theatrical studio spotlights. Subjects taken from the streets and
parks of contemporary Paris. The visual rebellion against the Academy was already
underway. Manet’s Déjeuner was its first public manifesto.

Manet himself
One thing worth saying about Manet personally: he wasn’t a bohemian. He wasn’t a
poor outsider. He came from a wealthy Parisian family, dressed elegantly, drank at the
Café Tortoni, and wanted Salon medals like every other ambitious painter of his
generation. He never thought of himself as a revolutionary. The scandals he caused
embarrassed him. He kept submitting to the Salon for the rest of his life, hoping for
official recognition. He got it briefly, near the end. The Legion of Honour came in 1881,
two years before he died of complications from syphilis at the age of fifty-one. He never
quite understood that the place his paintings had earned was bigger than the Salon —
they had earned the future.

An American in Paris and London
Whistler was born in Massachusetts in 1834. He spent most of his career in London and
Paris. He apprenticed in Paris between 1855 and 1859, knew Courbet, was a friend of
Fantin-Latour and Manet, and showed work at the same 1863 Salon des Refusés where
Manet’s Déjeuner caused its scandal. Whistler’s Symphony in White, No. 1: The White
Girl hung in that same rejected exhibition and drew almost as much attention as the
Manet.
By 1871 Whistler was living in Chelsea, London, at 96 Cheyne Walk. His mother, Anna
McNeill Whistler, had moved over from America to live with him. The story —
unverifiable but durable — is that the model he had booked for a portrait failed to show
up one day, and his mother stepped in. She sat for the portrait standing at first, until her
age forced her to sit. Whistler painted her in profile, in the dress she happened to be

What he was actually making
Whistler was a fanatic about painting as composition rather than painting as narrative.
He gave his works titles borrowed from music — Symphony in White, Nocturne in Black
and Gold, Arrangement in Grey and Black. The titles were a deliberate signal: don’t look
at this painting and ask what story it tells. Look at it and ask how it’s balanced. He was
moving toward what would later be called abstraction — the idea that a painting is first
and foremost a flat surface with shapes and colours on it, and that the subject is
secondary to the formal arrangement.
Look at how the painting is composed. A vertical strip of grey wall on the left. A
horizontal grey curtain across the top. A framed etching (one of Whistler’s own prints,
called Black Lion Wharf) hanging on the wall in the upper-left quadrant. The seated
figure in absolute profile, almost sculptural, occupying the lower-right two-thirds. The
whole canvas is built out of horizontal and vertical lines — a grid — with the curve of her
bonnet and folded hands as the only soft notes. The palette is grey, black, off-white,
with a single touch of warmth on her face.
If you cover the face with your thumb, you can see what Whistler actually painted: a
still-life arrangement of textures and rectangles. If you remove your thumb, the human
face reasserts itself and the painting becomes something else — a portrait of an old
woman, alone, formidable, somehow holding her own private thoughts. That double
reading is the whole point. The arrangement and the mother are both there. Neither
cancels the other.

How it got to Paris
Whistler submitted the painting to the Royal Academy in London in 1872. The Academy
nearly rejected it, then accepted it under pressure but hung it badly. It was not a
success in London. Whistler hated England’s reception of his work and eventually
pawned the painting. In 1891 the French state bought it directly from him — the first
time France had ever bought a painting by a living American artist for the national
collections. Whistler wrote to a friend: “To have one’s own picture hanging in the
Luxembourg, treated everywhere with deference and respect, is like a dream.” He had
been rescued from English indifference by the French state. The painting moved to the
Louvre in 1925, then here in 1986.
There’s a quiet political moment in this painting being where it is. America’s most
famous portrait, of an American mother, hangs not in any American museum but here in
Paris. It went on loan to American museums twice in the twentieth century — to Chicago
in 1933, to Pasadena in 2015 — but it always came home to the Musée d’Orsay. Anna

Who Lautrec was
Toulouse-Lautrec was a French aristocrat — his full name was Henri Marie Raymond de
Toulouse-Lautrec-Monfa — born in 1864 into one of the oldest noble families in southern
France. He had a genetic bone disease, made worse by two broken legs in childhood
that healed badly. As an adult he stood about four feet eight inches tall, with adult-sized
torso and head on child-sized legs. He moved with crutches and a cane. His family was
wealthy enough that he never had to work, but his condition and his physical
appearance closed off the noble military and political careers his father had wanted for
him.

What he did instead was move to Paris, set up a studio in Montmartre — the bohemian

Jane Avril
Jane Avril was born Jeanne Beaudon in 1868, in a working-class Paris neighbourhood.
Her mother was a prostitute, her father an Italian aristocrat who had separated from her
mother when Jane was two. She had a violent childhood, ran away repeatedly,
attempted suicide as a teenager, and found her way into Paris dance halls through a
circle of women who took her in. By the late 1880s she was performing at the newly
opened Moulin Rouge in Montmartre. She had a particular style — thin, angular, almost
mournful-looking offstage, but on stage capable of kicking her leg over her head with
what one contemporary called “the incarnation of dance.” Her stage nickname was La
Mélinite — melinite, a French military explosive.
Lautrec watched her dance dozens of times and drew her dozens more. The poster he
made of her in 1893, advertising the Jardin de Paris, became one of the most famous
lithographs of the century and helped invent the visual language of modern poster art.
They were lifelong friends. After Lautrec’s death in 1901, Avril stayed close to his family,
retired from dancing in 1905, married a print-maker, lost everything in the 1929 crash,
and died alone in poverty in occupied Paris in 1943.

How the painting is made
Look at the surface. The painting is on cardboard, not canvas — you can see places in

on purpose. He used the warm brown of the cardboard as one of his colours. He diluted
his oil paint with turpentine until it ran almost like watercolour, brushed it on fast, and
left whole areas of the support uncovered. The look is sketch-like, immediate,
caught-in-the-moment — but every line is deliberate.

Why this gallery matters
This room — the Galerie Françoise Cachin — is named after the first director of the

1980s. She fought to make sure the museum showed the full range of late-19th-century

managed to imitate.

in a sanatorium by his family. He died at his mother’s estate in 1901, at the age of
thirty-six. He never got to forty.

The Paris Opera commission

— 27 —

ORSAY

Musée d’Orsay · Paris

In the early 1860s, Napoleon III commissioned a new opera house to be the cultural
centerpiece of Haussmann’s rebuilt Paris. Charles Garnier was the architect. Garnier
wanted four monumental sculpture groups on the front facade, each representing one of
the performing arts: Lyric Drama, Instrumental Music, Lyric Poetry, and the Dance. He
gave each commission to a different sculptor. The Dance went to Carpeaux.
Carpeaux had a problem. The three other sculptors handled their commissions in the
calm, balanced, neoclassical mode the Beaux-Arts academy expected. Carpeaux
refused. He wanted movement. He wanted the dance to actually look like dancing —
bodies in motion, off-balance, caught mid-step, twisting around each other. He spent
three years working it out. The result was a ring of seven dancing female figures circling
a tall winged male genius in the centre, who holds a tambourine high above his head.
The figures are linked arm to arm. They lean back, kick forward, throw their heads
sideways. The whole group looks as if it could spin if you pushed it.

The 1869 scandal
The sculpture was unveiled on the Opera House facade in July 1869. The Parisian public
found it scandalous, and not for the movement. For the nakedness. Carpeaux’s dancing
women are nude. Their bodies are visibly real — their breasts, hips, and thighs rendered
with the kind of soft modelling that read, to a Second Empire audience, as more erotic
than mythological. Critics wrote letters to the newspapers complaining that the women
looked like “prostitutes” dancing on the facade of the city’s premier cultural institution.
Mothers refused to walk their daughters past the opera house. There were calls for the
sculpture to be removed.
On the night of the 26th of August 1869, someone threw a bottle of ink at the sculpture.
The black stain dripped down the stomach of one of the dancers and pooled at her feet.
The police didn’t find who did it. The stain was eventually cleaned, but the political
pressure on the Opera house to remove the sculpture grew. In April 1870 Garnier
commissioned a different sculptor, Charles Guméry, to make a more decorous
replacement. Then the Franco-Prussian War broke out in July 1870, France lost
catastrophically, Napoleon III’s Second Empire collapsed in September, and the whole
question of replacing the sculpture was forgotten. By the time the Opera house finally
opened in 1875, the regime that had been offended was gone. Carpeaux’s Danse stayed
on the facade.
Carpeaux didn’t live to see his work vindicated. He died of cancer in October 1875, three
months before the opera house opening. He was forty-eight years old.

What MNR means
The letters MNR stand for Musées Nationaux Récupération — the National Museums
Recovery designation. The story is this. During the Second World War, the German
occupation of France involved the systematic looting of art collections belonging to
Jewish families. Roughly one hundred thousand artworks were taken from French Jews
between 1940 and 1944 — paintings, drawings, sculptures, books, furniture, ritual
objects. Some were taken at gunpoint. Some were sold under coercion. Some were
confiscated by the Vichy regime as their owners were deported to the camps.
After the Liberation, the Allied recovery teams — the so-called Monuments Men, but also
French recovery officers — found about sixty thousand of these works in Germany and
Austria. Most were returned to surviving owners or their heirs. About fifteen thousand
could not be returned because the original owners and their families had been killed and
no heirs could be traced. Most of those were eventually sold off by the French state in
the 1950s. But about two thousand two hundred were kept back for safekeeping under
the MNR designation, held in trust by the French national museums until rightful heirs
could be found.
Two hundred and twenty-five of those MNR works are held by this museum. Most have
been in storage. This room is the first time they have been put on permanent public
display — not as a temporary exhibition, but as a permanent rotating exhibition from
this museum’s holdings, with the explicit aim of bringing the works back into public view
so that descendants of the original owners might recognise them.

Saint-Rémy
He painted this inside the asylum of Saint-Paul-de-Mausole, in the village of
Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, in the south of France. He had voluntarily admitted himself in
May 1889, after a series of mental crises in Arles — the most dramatic of which was the
night in December 1888 when he cut off part of his left ear and carried it to a brothel as
a gift. He spent the next year in the asylum. He was allowed to paint. He was given a
small studio. He produced about 150 paintings during that single year of treatment,
including this self-portrait, several versions of The Starry Night, the wheat-field
paintings, and Irises.
I want to be careful here, because Van Gogh has been mythologised for over a century
as the tormented madman who painted with his feelings. That story is partial. He was a
profoundly deliberate and technically articulate painter. He wrote nearly nine hundred
letters in his lifetime, most of them to his younger brother Theo, and the letters are full
of detailed analyses of colour theory, brushwork, composition, light. He read art
criticism. He studied prints. He copied other painters carefully. The swirling brushstrokes
you’re looking at on this canvas are not the symptoms of his illness. They are a colour
and movement system he had worked out over years. The illness is part of his story; it
doesn’t explain the paintings.

What he’s wearing
Look at the jacket. A working-man’s blue cotton jacket, the kind a peasant farmer or
labourer wore in 19th-century France. Van Gogh wore this jacket habitually. He chose it

The technique
Look at the background. A swirling, rhythmic field of pale blue-green brushstrokes,
applied in short curved dashes that move around his head in waves. Now look at the
jacket: the same kind of dashes, but in a different blue. And the face: the same dashes

colours and directions. It’s a structural decision. Van Gogh wanted unity between the
figure and the space around it — wanted them to feel like they belonged to the same
material universe. He wrote to Theo about this canvas: he was trying for a portrait that
was “simpler, more truthful than a photograph.” Not more dramatic. More truthful.
Look at the eyes. They’re slightly different colours — one more grey-green, one more
blue. The asymmetry is small but it’s there. Look at his mouth. Set hard, not quite a
frown. Look at the line of his temple where his jacket meets his hair. The edge is a single
dark stroke. He could draw.

What he was trying to do
He painted between twenty and thirty self-portraits in his ten-year career as a painter.

free. They were partly a record of his own changing condition. They were also, near the
end, a way of seeing whether he was still himself. After the December 1888 ear incident,
he was no longer sure. He wrote to Theo: “I need above all to look at myself in the
mirror, in order to paint myself, because if the figure I see in the mirror is mine, then I
will be able to paint other people too.” He was checking.

closer to a doctor named Paul Gachet. He was thirty-seven.

Why I want to be careful here
The standard interpretation of this painting is that the tilting walls and the dark windows

was buckling, that the dark windows show his spiritual isolation from organised religion,

Japanese prints for years and had absorbed their habit of subordinating realistic

way of using colour and brushstroke that compressed an enormous amount of
psychological pressure into ordinary subjects. The painting is genuinely doing what
people say it’s doing — it does feel like a building under spiritual strain — but it’s doing
it through deliberate technical means, not through unmediated emotional outpouring.

How he painted it

There is a famous legend about how Van Gogh painted this. The legend says that he
wore a straw hat with a ring of lit candles stuck around the brim, so that he could see his
palette in the dark while painting outdoors. The image is wonderful. It’s in every popular
biography. There’s a problem with it: it’s almost certainly not true. The candle-hat story
comes from a 1923 biography by Gustave Coquiot, published thirty-three years after

Modern Van Gogh scholars — including the team at the Van Gogh Museum in
Amsterdam who control the archive of his letters — consider it folklore. A hat with
burning candles around the brim would have been impractical and dangerous to wear,
and Van Gogh himself never mentions it.
What Van Gogh did write to his brother Theo, in a letter dated September 29, 1888 —
the same week he painted this canvas — was that he painted it “actually at night, under
a gas lamp.” He probably started the painting outdoors on the riverbank, lit by one of
the city’s gas streetlamps, and finished it the next day in his studio. A local newspaper,
L’Homme de Bronze, published a small notice on September 30th saying: “Mr Vincent,
an impressionist painter, works, we are told, in the evening, by the light of the
gaslamps, in one of our squares.” That’s contemporary evidence. It’s a gas-lamp, not a
candle hat.

What he wrote about the colours
In the same letter to Theo, Van Gogh described the painting in extraordinary technical

to green-bronze. On the aquamarine field of the sky the Great Bear is a sparkling green
and pink, whose discreet paleness contrasts with the brutal gold of the gas.”
Now look at the painting and check. The sky is aquamarine — a blue-green, not pure
blue. The water is royal blue, darker than the sky. The reflections of the gaslamps in the
river are russet-gold falling into green-bronze. He saw all of that, named all of that, and
put all of that on the canvas. There is nothing imprecise about how he was working. He
was making careful, observed colour decisions in a state of intense calm.

been visible above the southwest horizon where Van Gogh was looking. He had to turn
his head to the north to see it. He included it anyway. The constellation mattered to him

What you see
The ground is divided into flat ribbons of saturated colour — a yellow-orange band in the
foreground, a green band behind it, and pink and lavender bands beyond. Gauguin has
thrown out naturalistic landscape colour. There is no attempt to make the grass look like
grass. He uses the ground colour the way a stained-glass artist uses colour — as a flat
decorative field, chosen for emotional intensity, not realistic resemblance.
In the foreground, two seated Tahitian women in long printed dresses. The woman on
the right is playing a small wooden flute. The woman on the left looks straight out at the
viewer. Between them, a small bright red dog — a real red, not a stylised redness.
Behind them, in the middle distance, a procession of smaller figures dances around
what appears to be a tall carved wooden idol or god-figure. Gauguin was inventing this
idol — the Polynesian religious imagery he painted was largely imaginary, since the
French colonial administration and Christian missionaries had suppressed traditional
Tahitian religion decades before he arrived.

Who Gauguin was
Paul Gauguin was born in Paris in 1848. He spent his childhood partly in Peru, with his
mother’s family. As an adult he worked for years as a successful stockbroker in Paris,
married a Danish woman named Mette Gad, and had five children with her. He painted
on the weekends. After the 1882 stock-market crash, he lost most of his income, and in
his late thirties he decided to leave his job and family and become a full-time painter.
He spent the next decade wandering — Brittany, Martinique, Provence (where he stayed
with Van Gogh in Arles for two months in 1888, the disastrous collaboration that ended
in Van Gogh’s ear-cutting), and eventually French Polynesia. He arrived in Tahiti in June
1891, returned to France briefly in 1893, and went back to the Pacific for good in 1895,
this time eventually moving to the more remote Marquesas Islands, where he died in
1903 of syphilis at the age of fifty-four.

What he was looking for, and what he found
Gauguin’s public story about why he went to Tahiti was about art. He wrote that Europe
had become artistically dead, that Western painting had run out of fresh material, and
that he needed to find what he called a “primitive” culture untouched by Western
civilisation in order to renew his work. He talked about searching for a paradise. He
wrote his autobiography Noa Noa, in which he described his Tahitian life in romantic,
mystical terms.

The reality on the ground was different. By the time Gauguin arrived in 1891, Tahiti had
been a French colony for nearly fifty years. The capital, Papeete, was a colonial port with
a Catholic cathedral, a French governor, French shops, and a European-dressed mixed
population. Traditional Polynesian religion had been largely outlawed. The local
population had been devastated by introduced diseases — from an estimated forty
thousand before European contact to about six thousand when Gauguin arrived. The
“primitive paradise” he claimed to find was largely a fiction he constructed for himself
and for his Paris market.

The part that has to be said
I want to say this directly, because it is now part of how this painter is officially
discussed at the Musée d’Orsay and at every major museum that holds Gauguin’s work.
Within a few weeks of arriving in Tahiti, Gauguin entered into a relationship with a
Tahitian girl named Teha’amana, whom he described as his “wife.” She was thirteen
years old. He was forty-three. The relationship lasted for parts of his first stay on the
island. He took at least two other Polynesian girls as partners over the next decade —
Pahura, also thirteen when their relationship began, and Vaeoho, fourteen. He fathered
several children. He had syphilis, which he had contracted in France, and which he
transmitted to multiple sexual partners.
Gauguin himself wrote about these relationships openly in letters and in Noa Noa. He
saw nothing wrong with them. The French colonial administration in Tahiti saw nothing
wrong with them. By the standards of his time and his colonial context, they were
unremarkable. By the standards now applied to him by Pacific scholars, by survivors’
descendants in Polynesia, and increasingly by museum curators in Europe — they were
the sexual exploitation of children by a powerful European man inside a colonial
structure that had stripped them of protection.

I am not telling you not to look at Arearea. The painting is here, it changed the course of

emotional carrier rather than as description was a genuine invention. Gauguin gave
painters permission to use colour the way composers use a key change.
What I’m asking you to do is hold two things at once. The first: this canvas is a genuine
artistic achievement. The second: it was made by a forty-four-year-old French colonist

his market. The two seated women in the foreground are not generic figures. They are
likely people he knew personally. They had names. They had lives. We mostly don’t

Pissarro himself
Camille Pissarro was born in 1830 on the island of St Thomas, in what was then the
Danish West Indies and is now the U.S. Virgin Islands. His father was a Sephardic Jewish
merchant. Pissarro moved to Paris in 1855 to study painting, never went home, and
spent the rest of his life in France. He was the oldest member of the Impressionist circle
by nearly a decade, and the most generous — he was the painter who mentored
Cézanne in the early 1870s, taught Gauguin to paint in the early 1880s, and quietly held
the group together through years of poverty, ridicule, and infighting. Without Pissarro
the Impressionists might have stayed a loose set of friends. With him, they became a
movement.

What he’s painting
The scene is a winter morning in the countryside near Pontoise, the town northwest of

How Monet worked
In February 1892, Monet rented a room across the street from Rouen Cathedral, on the
second floor of a small commercial building. The room’s window looked directly at the
west facade of the cathedral, about thirty metres away. He set up a row of easels along
the window, with a different canvas on each. As the sun moved across the sky during
the day, he switched canvases every thirty or forty minutes, working on whichever one
matched the current light. When the light changed beyond the canvas he was on, he
moved to the next.
He worked on this scheme for two consecutive winters, 1892 and 1893. He returned to
his home studio at Giverny in the spring and continued working on the canvases from
memory, refining them, repainting, building up the surface. The paint on the finished
canvases is thick — in some places almost three millimetres deep — because he kept
adding layers.
He suffered nightmares about the cathedral throughout the project. He wrote to his
second wife Alice that he dreamed the cathedral was collapsing on top of him in colours
— pink, blue, yellow — and that he woke up convinced he would never finish the
paintings. He wrote: “I work with intense fatigue. Everything changes, even stone.” The
project nearly broke him. It also produced his most famous statement on what he was
trying to do in painting: that an object has no fixed colour, no fixed form, no fixed
identity. An object is only its appearance at one moment in one light, and the
appearance is what painting is for.

Where the painting is set
The Moulin de la Galette was a working-class dance hall on the hill of Montmartre, then

metre and a half wide — and he wanted to paint it on location, in the open air, capturing
the actual moving light. He couldn’t carry a canvas that size up and down the hill every

walked the canvas back and forth on his back, with his friends helping him. He worked
on it through the summer of 1876.

Montmartre circle disguised as a public dance.

Who Degas was
I want to be honest about Degas, because he was a difficult man. He was born into a

married and lived alone.

motion that the nineteenth century produced. The contradiction is the contradiction. His
work and his politics existed in the same person.

What the painting shows

The Opéra system
The Paris Opéra of the 1870s was a complicated institution. It produced great ballet at
the highest technical level. It also operated a system in which wealthy male subscribers,
called abonnés, paid for access to backstage areas where they could approach the
dancers directly. Many of the corps de ballet had patrons. Some of the patronage was
respectful. Much of it was exploitative. The dancers, most of whom came from
working-class families with no other income, had limited ability to refuse. Degas knew
this system. He painted both the dancers and the abonnés repeatedly, often in the same
frame. He was a witness inside it.

What the original was made of
The original 1881 sculpture was made of pigmented beeswax over a metal armature,

Visitors in 1881 didn’t know what they were seeing. Sculpture, by every
nineteenth-century convention, was made of marble or bronze. It depicted gods, heroes,
allegorical figures — the permanent and the elevated. Degas had produced something
that looked like a wax-museum exhibit. Worse, he had not idealised the model. The wax
girl was thin, plain-featured, with the slightly forward-jutting jaw of a working-class child.
The critics were appalled.

third called for the sculpture to be removed from the exhibition. The objections were
partly about technique — the use of wax and fabric — but they were also unmistakably
about class. The critics were reading the girl’s working-class face as a moral defect.

Who Morisot was
Berthe Morisot was born in 1841 into a wealthy bourgeois Parisian family. Her sister,

the only one of her gender in the Impressionist circle.
The obstacles she worked under were ones the male Impressionists never had to think
about. A woman of her class could not walk alone through working-class Paris. She could
not enter cafés unaccompanied. She could not visit brothels to draw the women there,
the way Degas and Lautrec did. She could not sit in the wings of the Opéra at rehearsal.
She could not paint the dance halls of Montmartre or the modern leisure of the Bois de
Boulogne without a male chaperone. The whole external world that the male
Impressionists treated as their natural subject was, for Morisot, structurally inaccessible.

transparent without being insubstantial. It catches light. It feels like fabric you could
touch. The whole composition is built around this veil of white — it separates the
sleeping baby from the world the way the woman’s contemplation separates her from
us.

cradle make one continuous shape, with the baby at the centre. The composition is a
kind of nest.

What the painting opened

Who Cézanne was
Paul Cézanne was born in 1839 in the southern French city of Aix-en-Provence, where
his father was a successful banker. He moved to Paris in the 1860s to study painting,
became friends with Pissarro, exhibited at the first Impressionist exhibition in 1874, and
was savaged by the critics so badly that he withdrew from Paris and returned to Aix. He
spent most of the rest of his life working alone in Provence, painting the landscape
around his father’s estate, the local labourers, still lifes of apples and oranges in his
studio, and the mountain of Sainte-Victoire visible from his windows.
He was a difficult, anxious man, prone to destroying his own canvases. He worked slowly
— sometimes a single still life took him six months. He had almost no public success
during his lifetime. He died in 1906 at the age of sixty-seven, after collapsing while
painting outdoors in a thunderstorm. He thought of himself as a failure.
Then everything changed. Within five years of his death, younger painters — Matisse,
Picasso, Braque — had identified Cézanne as the most important precursor of the new
century. Picasso said, repeatedly, that Cézanne was “the father of us all.” Matisse said
Cézanne was “the master of us all.” Cubism is unimaginable without him. So is most of
20th-century abstract painting. The man who died thinking he had failed had in fact built
the foundation for modern art.

What he was trying to do
Cézanne wrote, in a letter near the end of his life, what he thought he was trying to do.
He said: “I want to make of Impressionism something solid and durable, like the art of
the museums.” He admired what the Impressionists had done — the broken brushwork,
the capture of fleeting light — but he thought it was unstable. He wanted to build
paintings that would last the way Poussin’s paintings had lasted, the way Velázquez’s
paintings had lasted. He wanted Impressionist perception with classical structure
underneath it.
Look at how he’s built this painting. The two men are essentially geometric forms —
their bodies are cylinders, their hats are domes, their shoulders are wedges. The table
between them is a flat plane. The wine bottle on the table stands as a vertical axis right
down the middle of the canvas, splitting the composition into two near-mirror halves.
The two men are not portraits of two individuals so much as two weighted masses on
either side of a single architectural beam.
Look at the brushwork. The strokes are short and rectangular, applied in patches.
Cézanne called these petites sensations — little sensations — each one a single

What this opened
Now look at how the table tilts. The plane of the tabletop is not in correct perspective.

has decided that painterly truth matters more than optical accuracy. If the tabletop has
to tilt a few degrees to make the composition balance, then the tabletop tilts.
This is the move that opens twentieth-century painting. Cézanne has given the painter
permission to manipulate perspective, anatomy, and proportion in service of the
painting itself — not in service of the depicted scene. Picasso and Braque took this
insight and ran with it. Within fifteen years of Cézanne’s death, they had broken the
figure into facets and invented Cubism. Mondrian and Malevich took Cézanne’s
geometric impulse further and arrived at pure abstraction. All of it came out of this kind
of painting — the small, obsessive, structural work that a lonely man in Aix did for thirty
years without an audience.

Seurat’s Le Cirque
First, find the large circus scene. This is Le Cirque — The Circus — by Georges Seurat.
He started it in late 1890 and was still working on it when he died of a sudden illness —
either diphtheria or meningitis, the diagnosis was never confirmed — on the 29th of
March 1891. He was thirty-one years old. The painting is technically unfinished. Seurat
had intended to add more figures and resolve the foreground, but he ran out of time.
Walk close to the canvas. Almost touch it with your nose. Look at how the paint is
applied. The whole surface is built out of millions of tiny separate dots of pure colour.
There are no blended areas. There is no brushwork in the traditional sense. Seurat
applied each dot by tapping the brush onto the canvas. The technique is called
pointillism, or sometimes — in Seurat’s own preferred term — divisionnisme,
divisionism.
He invented this technique from a careful study of nineteenth-century colour theory. He
read Michel-Eugène Chevreul and Ogden Rood, two scientists who had worked out the
optical principle that two adjacent dots of pure colour are mixed not on the canvas but
in the viewer’s eye. A dot of pure blue next to a dot of pure yellow does not look like
blue and yellow at viewing distance — it looks like green, but a green more luminous
than any green you could mix on a palette. Seurat decided that this optical mixing was
the future of painting. He spent the last seven years of his life applying it.
Now step back five metres. The dots become forms. The acrobat is balanced on the back
of a white horse. A clown leaps in front. The crowd in the amphitheatre rises behind. The
colour vibrates — actually vibrates, in a way that earlier painting doesn’t. Seurat had
proved his theory. He would not live to develop it further. He left about seven major
canvases. His early death was one of the great losses of nineteenth-century painting.

Sérusier asked Gauguin for a lesson. Gauguin walked him out into the Bois d’Amour, a
small wood outside Pont-Aven, set up a wooden board, and watched Sérusier paint.
As Sérusier started to paint the landscape in normal naturalistic colours, Gauguin
interrupted him. The exact exchange has been recorded many times, with slight
variations. The version most commonly told goes like this. Gauguin asked: “How do you
see these trees? They are yellow? Well then, put down yellow. And that shadow, rather
blue? Don’t be afraid to paint it as blue as possible. And those red leaves? Use
vermilion.”
Sérusier painted what Gauguin told him to paint. The result — the small board you’re
looking at — wasn’t a representation of a forest. It was an arrangement of pure flat
colour patches. Yellow path, blue shadow, red foliage, green water. The landscape was
barely identifiable. Sérusier brought the small board back to Paris and showed it to a
group of his fellow art students — Maurice Denis, Pierre Bonnard, Édouard Vuillard. They
were astonished. They called the board le Talisman, the talisman, because it seemed to
contain a magical principle. Around this small painting they formed the group called the
Nabis — the prophets — dedicated to the principle that colour is freed from description
and free to be used for its own emotional power.

What these two paintings opened
These two paintings are the two main routes out of Impressionism. Seurat took the
Impressionist observation of light and made it scientific — broken into measured dots,
applied by a system, derived from optical theory. That route fed into systematic
modernism — the rigorous, theory-driven art of the early twentieth century. Sérusier
and Gauguin took the Impressionist freedom of colour and pushed it further — dropped
the observation entirely, freed colour from any reference, made painting into pure
emotional structure. That route fed into Matisse, into the Fauves, into German
Expressionism, into all of the colour-driven modernism that followed.
Two paintings, made within three years of each other, by two young men who knew
each other through the Paris art world. Between them, they invented the next century.
Seurat died at thirty-one without finishing his great circus painting. Sérusier lived
another forty years and spent most of them as a quiet professor of decorative arts,
painting religious subjects — he never produced another work as important as the small
board he made under Gauguin’s direction in one afternoon in Brittany when he was
twenty-four.

Walk to the far end of level five — the upstream end of the building, opposite the clock you stood
behind at the start of Half B. Find the Café Campana, just past the exit of the Impressionist
Gallery. Step inside. You’ll see a strange aquatic interior — orange coral-shaped lamps, gold
scale-pattern chandeliers, organic chair shapes — set against the back of the second great clock
face. Find a seat or a place to stand near the clock window.

You’ve arrived. The end of the tour. Sit if you can. The Café Campana is one of the great
pleasures of this museum, and the right place to end.
This is the Café Campana. It opened in its current form in 2011, as part of the same
renovation that added the bridge to the Pavillon Amont we crossed earlier. The interior
was designed by the Brazilian brothers Fernando and Humberto Campana, the two most
celebrated industrial designers in South America. Their commission was to make a
space that would echo the Art Nouveau decorative arts collection of the museum
without imitating it — a contemporary tribute to the spirit of the 1900 Universal
Exposition, in which Laloux’s station had opened.

Look around
Look at the lamps. They are orange and red glass pieces shaped like coral or sea
anemones, hanging in clusters from the ceiling. The wall behind the bar is panelled in
hammered copper, blue methacrylate, and shimmering scale-patterned metal. The
chairs are organic pebble-shapes. The whole room is meant to feel like the inside of a
strange aquarium — a dream-like underwater chamber suspended at the top of an
iron-and-glass train station.
The Campana brothers were drawn to the work of Émile Gallé, the great French glass
artist of the 1890s whose work fills the Art Nouveau decorative arts rooms downstairs.
Gallé made glass vases shaped like deep-sea creatures, glass lamps shaped like
jellyfish, dragonflies, orchids, fish. The Café Campana is the Campana brothers’ homage

The second clock
And there it is, the second great clock. Look at it from inside the café. The Roman
numerals are visible from this side too, in mirror image — the same iron-and-glass face
as the clock at the other end of the floor, set into the downstream pediment of Laloux’s
station. The whole museum is hung between these two clocks. The art is the body. The
clocks are the bookends.
If you came on a Thursday evening, the café is open until a quarter to nine. The lighting
changes through the afternoon — the sun moves across the western sky, dropping
toward the Seine, and the gold light pours through the clock face into the room. Late
afternoon in this café, in late summer, is one of the great quiet pleasures in Paris.

What you’ve walked through
Now, before you leave, hold the whole tour in your mind for a moment. You started on
the empty forecourt where the rhinoceros should have been. You stood under Laloux’s
glass canopy. You looked at Couture’s twenty-five-foot warning to the bourgeoisie of
1847. You stood in front of Millet’s gleaners and Courbet’s funeral and Manet’s Olympia,
painted 1863, scandalous 1865. You stood in front of Whistler’s mother in her
arrangement of grey and black, and Toulouse-Lautrec’s Jane Avril kicking on a piece of
bare cardboard.
You crossed the bridge to the Pavillon Amont and met the Carpeaux Danse from the
Opéra facade. You stood quietly in the MNR Gallery in front of works whose first owners
were murdered in Auschwitz. You met Van Gogh in three paintings — the asylum
self-portrait, the Auvers church, the Rhône under stars — and you met Gauguin with the
full picture, both his radical use of colour and the children he took as partners.
You came up here, to level five, and stood behind the first great clock. You looked
through it at the Louvre and the Tuileries and Montmartre, and then you turned around
and walked through the Impressionist heart of the museum — Pissarro’s frost, Monet’s
poppy field and steaming station and dissolving cathedral, Renoir’s sunlit Sunday dance,
Degas’s ballet class and his fourteen-year-old in fourth position, Morisot’s cradle,
Caillebotte’s floor scrapers, Cézanne’s card players, and Seurat’s dotted circus next to
Sérusier’s tiny talisman that opened the twentieth century.
Thirty-one tracks. Sixty-six years of European painting. One former train station. Two
clocks. You came in through one of them. You’re leaving through the other.

When you’re ready to leave, the museum exit is back through the central nave. Walk
back the way you came — down through the Post-Impressionist galleries, past the great
clock, down the escalators to level zero, and out through the exit. The exit and entrance
have been separated as part of the current renovation, so the exit is no longer where
you came in.
If you have an extra hour and the day is bright, walk across the Seine to the Tuileries
Garden. The Musée de l’Orangerie, at the western end of the garden, holds Monet’s
great Water Lilies cycle — eight enormous oval panels installed in two oval rooms
shaped like the infinity symbol, designed by Monet himself in the last years of his life.
The Orangerie and the Musée d’Orsay are administered together — you can buy a
combined ticket. The two museums together hold the great closing chord of Monet’s
career: the Impressionist paintings you just walked through, and the late masterworks
that grew out of them.

Goodbye
Thank you for walking the Musée d’Orsay with me. Sixty-six years of painting, two great
clocks, one converted train station saved from demolition by three French presidents —
the building that became the museum of the modern eye.
Zola, defending Manet in 1866, wrote that our duty is to go to the bottom of things.
Every painter on these walls did that, in their own way. So, in your way, do you.
Au revoir, et merci.
