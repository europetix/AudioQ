Track 27

Berthe Morisot’s The Cradle
Impressionist Gallery · The first Impressionist exhibition, 1874
5 min

◆ Stop & Look

Stay on level five in the Impressionist Gallery. Find the small painting of a young woman seated in
profile beside a white cradle, looking down at a sleeping baby through a sheer white netting that
covers the cradle like a tent. The woman wears a dark dress; the painting is delicate and quiet.
This is Berthe Morisot’s Le Berceau — The Cradle.

Stand quietly with this painting for a moment. After the noise of the dance hall and the
harshness of the dance studio, this is the first room of the museum that asks you to be
still.
This is Le Berceau — The Cradle — painted by Berthe Morisot in 1872. It was one of nine
works she showed at the first Impressionist exhibition in April 1874. She was the only
woman to exhibit in that founding show, and one of the founders of the Impressionist
movement — a position that almost every history book obscures.

Who Morisot was
Berthe Morisot was born in 1841 into a wealthy bourgeois Parisian family. Her sister,
Edma, was also a serious painter — the two of them trained together as girls and
continued working side by side into their twenties. Berthe exhibited at the official Salon
for the first time in 1864, when she was twenty-three. She knew Manet, who became
one of her closest friends and her sister-in-law’s husband. She painted in oil and
watercolour and pastel. She was, by 1870, a fully professional exhibiting painter — and
the only one of her gender in the Impressionist circle.
The obstacles she worked under were ones the male Impressionists never had to think
about. A woman of her class could not walk alone through working-class Paris. She could
not enter cafés unaccompanied. She could not visit brothels to draw the women there,
the way Degas and Lautrec did. She could not sit in the wings of the Opéra at rehearsal.
She could not paint the dance halls of Montmartre or the modern leisure of the Bois de
Boulogne without a male chaperone. The whole external world that the male
Impressionists treated as their natural subject was, for Morisot, structurally inaccessible.

What she did instead was turn her attention to the world she did have access to: the
interiors of bourgeois homes, the domestic lives of women and children, the gardens of
summer houses. She painted what was around her with the same technical seriousness
and the same visual intensity that the men brought to streets and railway stations and
dance floors. She made the case, painting by painting, that the domestic was as worth
painting as the public.

The painting
The young woman in Le Berceau is Morisot’s older sister, Edma Pontillon. The sleeping
infant is Edma’s daughter Blanche, who was a few months old when this was painted.
Edma had given up her own painting career to marry, which was the expected path.
Berthe had decided not to. The two sisters’ different choices about marriage and work
were a constant subject of letters between them. The painting is partly about this: a
sister looking at the sister who became a mother, and at the child who is the reason the
mother stopped painting.

Look at the netting
The technical centre of the painting is the white netting draped over the cradle. Morisot
has painted it with extraordinary delicacy — long pale curving strokes of cool white over
a pinker underlayer, with the contour of the cradle visible underneath. The netting is
transparent without being insubstantial. It catches light. It feels like fabric you could
touch. The whole composition is built around this veil of white — it separates the
sleeping baby from the world the way the woman’s contemplation separates her from
us.
Look at how Edma’s left arm forms a diagonal line that mirrors the diagonal line of the
netting above her. Morisot has built a geometry of two triangles — the protective tent
over the baby, and the curve of the mother bending toward it. The mother and the
cradle make one continuous shape, with the baby at the centre. The composition is a
kind of nest.

What the painting opened
Morisot painted in this domestic register for the next twenty years, producing about four
hundred works — paintings of her daughter Julie growing up, of friends visiting, of
women reading and sewing and walking in gardens. She continued to exhibit in seven of
the eight Impressionist shows. She was respected within the circle but largely ignored
by the wider art world during her lifetime. She died of pneumonia in 1895 at the age of
fifty-four — caught from her daughter Julie, whom she nursed through the same illness.
Julie survived. Her death certificate, written by a clerk who did not know her, listed her
profession as “without profession.”

It took the better part of the twentieth century for art history to bring her fully back into
view. The Musée d’Orsay now holds nine of her paintings. The Cradle is the one
everyone comes for. It is one of the quietest masterpieces in this museum.
Walk on. The next painting is also about labour, but a different kind — three shirtless
men on their hands and knees, scraping the varnish off a wooden floor in a Paris
apartment, with sunlight coming in through tall windows behind them. Track
Twenty-Eight is Caillebotte’s The Floor Scrapers.
