Track 06

Courbet’s A Burial at Ornans
Level 0, Courbet gallery · The death of romantic painting
5 min

◆ Stop & Look

Walk into the Courbet gallery on level zero. Two enormous horizontal paintings dominate the
room — we’ll look at both. For this track, stand in front of the funeral scene: a line of townspeople
in black, a priest, an open grave, a grey winter sky over a small French town. This is A Burial at
Ornans.

Stand back. This painting is twenty-two feet wide. It needs distance to be seen as one
composition.
This is Gustave Courbet’s A Burial at Ornans, painted between 1849 and 1850 and
shown at the Salon of 1850-51. Courbet called it “the burial of Romanticism” — by which
he meant the burial of the kind of operatic, theatrical, idealised painting that had
dominated French art for the previous fifty years. He wasn’t exaggerating. With this
canvas, he killed a whole tradition. The Impressionists who followed him never had to
fight that battle, because Courbet had already won it.

What’s in the painting
A funeral in a small French town. The town is Ornans, in the Franche-Comté region of
eastern France — Courbet’s home town. The man being buried is Courbet’s great-uncle,
Claude-Étienne Teste. The mourners along the back are real people from Ornans —
Courbet’s neighbours, relatives, the local priest, the mayor, the local vineyard workers,

the women of the parish. Courbet painted them in his studio one by one, from memory
and from sittings, and assembled them into this single horizontal frieze.
Look at the bottom centre of the canvas. The open grave is right there, cutting into the
foreground, the dark rectangular hole inches from your feet. Courbet doesn’t hide it. He
doesn’t put a beam of heavenly light coming down from above. He doesn’t show angels
carrying the soul up. The grave is just an empty hole, waiting.
Look at the dog in the right foreground. A normal village dog, sniffing the dirt. Not
symbolic. Not allegorical. Just a dog at a funeral, the way dogs actually are at funerals in
small towns. Look at the gravedigger kneeling beside the hole. He’s waiting for the
priest to finish. Look at the sky — a grey, flat, mid-afternoon sky over a chalk cliff. No
drama. No weather. Just the weather you actually get in eastern France in October.

What the Salon of 1850 thought
The Salon jury argued for a long time about whether to accept this painting at all. The
scale was the first problem. A canvas of this size was reserved, by tradition, for what the
academy called peinture d’histoire — history painting. A coronation. A battle. The death
of Caesar. The crucifixion. Courbet had taken twenty-two feet of canvas and put a
small-town funeral on it. He had given monumental scale to an event that no one
outside Ornans would have heard of.
The second problem was the people. The mourners in the back row are not idealised.
The men have weathered faces, drinkers’ noses, stubble. The women are stocky and
middle-aged. The priest is fat. The mayor is bald. No one in the painting is beautiful in
the academic sense. They’re just the actual people who happen to live in Ornans,
France, in 1849.
Conservative critics called it “a celebration of ugliness.” One critic said Courbet had
“dragged painting into the mud.” Champfleury, defending Courbet, said he had “simply
painted what he saw.” The painting was both attacked and celebrated. But more
importantly: it could not be ignored. The Academy couldn’t pretend Courbet hadn’t
painted it. After this canvas, the question of what painting could be about was
permanently open.

Realism
Courbet gave his approach a name. He called himself a Realist. He wrote a manifesto: “I
cannot paint an angel because I have never seen one.” He meant it literally. He would
paint only what he could physically observe, at the scale he wanted, with the dignity he
chose to give it. The Realist movement that he started would feed directly into Manet
and the Impressionists, who took the same impulse — paint what you actually see, not
what tradition tells you to paint — and turned it into something even more radical.
