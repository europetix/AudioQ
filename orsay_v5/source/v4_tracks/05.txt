Musée d’Orsay · Paris

This is Des Glaneuses — The Gleaners — painted by Jean-François Millet in 1857 and
shown at the Salon that year. It is now one of the most famous paintings in this
museum. It was, when it was first shown, one of the most hated paintings of the year.

What gleaning was
Look at what the women are actually doing. The wheat harvest is over — you can see
the haystacks rising behind them, the wagon loaded with grain, the supervisor on
horseback in the distance. The three women in the foreground are bent double, picking
up the leftover stalks that the harvesters dropped on the ground. This was called
gleaning — a legal right under French law that let the poorest people in a village walk a
harvested field after the landowner was done, and keep whatever scraps they could
find. A handful of broken stalks at the end of a day of bent-back work.
Look at their hands. Coarse. Sunburned. Look at their clothes — rough fabric, patched,
faded. Look at their faces — you can’t really see them, because their heads are bent.
They’re not individuals here. They’re a class. The rural poor of mid-19th-century France,
doing the work that fed Paris.

Why this painting scandalised people
Millet did something that the Paris art establishment found deeply uncomfortable. He
took the lowest agricultural workers in France and he painted them at the scale, with the
dignity, with the compositional weight that academic painters reserved for biblical
scenes, royal portraits, and ancient heroes. The three figures are arranged like a Greek
frieze. Their bent bodies form a slow rhythm across the canvas. The light is the warm,
gold-soaked light of late-day rural France — light Millet had been studying for years from
his cottage in the village of Barbizon. He made the gleaners monumental. He made
looking at them feel like an act of seeing something serious.
Conservative critics in 1857 called Millet a socialist agitator. They said the painting was
an indictment of French society. They pointed out the obvious contrast inside the frame
— the abundance of the harvest in the background, the desperation in the foreground —
and asked why anyone would want to hang such a thing on their wall.
Millet always denied that he was making a political statement. He said he was just
painting the work he’d grown up watching, in his own village, by people he knew. “I was
born a peasant,” he wrote, “and I will die a peasant.” But denying the politics didn’t
mean the politics weren’t in the painting. By taking the scale and the seriousness of
academic art and applying it to the people the academy ignored, he changed what
painting could be about.

Every painter we’re going to look at for the rest of this floor owes something to Millet.
Courbet, in the next room, will take this even further — he’ll paint a small-town funeral
at the scale of a coronation. Manet, two rooms over, will paint a Parisian sex worker with
the gravity of a Venus. The whole ground floor of this museum is built on the premise
Millet established in 1857: ordinary modern people are a legitimate subject for serious
art. You don’t need a Roman emperor or a biblical hero. The woman bent over picking
up stalks is enough.
