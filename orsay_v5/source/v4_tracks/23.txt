Monet’s Rouen Cathedral Series
Impressionist Gallery · The cathedral dissolving in light
5 min

◆ Stop & Look

Stay in the Impressionist Gallery. Find a wall — or several adjacent walls — with multiple
canvases of the same building: the front facade of a Gothic cathedral, all the same composition,
each one in different colours: blue, gold, pink, grey, white. These are Monet’s Rouen Cathedral
paintings.

Stand back and look at the wall as a whole. The same building, painted four or five
times, hung in a row. The composition is almost identical. Only the light changes.
This is the Musée d’Orsay’s holding of Claude Monet’s Rouen Cathedral series — a
project he worked on between 1892 and 1894, producing thirty paintings of the west
facade of Rouen Cathedral, the great Gothic church in the Norman city of Rouen. The full

series was exhibited together in Paris in May 1895, in a single exhibition of twenty

How Monet worked
In February 1892, Monet rented a room across the street from Rouen Cathedral, on the
second floor of a small commercial building. The room’s window looked directly at the
west facade of the cathedral, about thirty metres away. He set up a row of easels along
the window, with a different canvas on each. As the sun moved across the sky during
the day, he switched canvases every thirty or forty minutes, working on whichever one
matched the current light. When the light changed beyond the canvas he was on, he
moved to the next.
He worked on this scheme for two consecutive winters, 1892 and 1893. He returned to
his home studio at Giverny in the spring and continued working on the canvases from
memory, refining them, repainting, building up the surface. The paint on the finished
canvases is thick — in some places almost three millimetres deep — because he kept
adding layers.
He suffered nightmares about the cathedral throughout the project. He wrote to his
second wife Alice that he dreamed the cathedral was collapsing on top of him in colours
— pink, blue, yellow — and that he woke up convinced he would never finish the
paintings. He wrote: “I work with intense fatigue. Everything changes, even stone.” The
project nearly broke him. It also produced his most famous statement on what he was
trying to do in painting: that an object has no fixed colour, no fixed form, no fixed
identity. An object is only its appearance at one moment in one light, and the
appearance is what painting is for.

What you can see on these canvases
Look at the surfaces. The Rouen Cathedrals on this wall are hung in the order of times of
day: early morning blue light, midday white sun, afternoon gold, late afternoon
orange-pink, evening violet. The cathedral facade is the same on all of them — the same
Gothic portals, the same rose window, the same towers — but the stone has been
almost completely dissolved by the light at every moment. In the blue morning canvas,
the stone looks like underwater rock. In the gold midday canvas, the stone looks like it’s
melting. In the violet evening canvas, the cathedral is almost a ghost.
Walk close to one of them. From three metres back, the canvas reads as a Gothic
cathedral. From thirty centimetres, it reads as a thick relief of impasto paint — ridged,

What he proved
Monet had been working toward this project his whole career. The studies in his
Argenteuil years, the steam paintings in 1877, the Belle-Île cliffs in the 1880s, the
Haystacks in 1890-91 — all of them were rehearsals for what the Cathedrals would
finally make completely clear. A subject doesn’t exist outside the light that strikes it.
Painting is not the representation of the world. Painting is the representation of the
moment of looking. The Cathedrals showed this not as an argument but as a fact: thirty
paintings of one building, no two alike, all of them true.
