Cross back to the Lille side of the central nave on level zero, or look for the Galerie Françoise
Cachin area where international 19th-century painters and the Toulouse-Lautrec works are
shown. Find a small-to-medium painting of an elderly woman seated in profile against a grey wall,
wearing a long black dress, hands folded in her lap. This is Whistler’s Mother.

Walk up to this painting and just stand with it for a moment. After the colour and theatre
of Manet, this one asks you to slow down.
This is Arrangement in Grey and Black No. 1, painted by James McNeill Whistler in 1871.
It is known to almost everyone in the world by another name: Whistler’s Mother. The
second title is the one Whistler hated. He didn’t paint a mother. He painted, as he
insisted his whole life, an arrangement of grey and black.

An American in Paris and London
Whistler was born in Massachusetts in 1834. He spent most of his career in London and
Paris. He apprenticed in Paris between 1855 and 1859, knew Courbet, was a friend of
Fantin-Latour and Manet, and showed work at the same 1863 Salon des Refusés where
Manet’s Déjeuner caused its scandal. Whistler’s Symphony in White, No. 1: The White
Girl hung in that same rejected exhibition and drew almost as much attention as the
Manet.
By 1871 Whistler was living in Chelsea, London, at 96 Cheyne Walk. His mother, Anna
McNeill Whistler, had moved over from America to live with him. The story —
unverifiable but durable — is that the model he had booked for a portrait failed to show
up one day, and his mother stepped in. She sat for the portrait standing at first, until her
age forced her to sit. Whistler painted her in profile, in the dress she happened to be

What he was actually making
Whistler was a fanatic about painting as composition rather than painting as narrative.
He gave his works titles borrowed from music — Symphony in White, Nocturne in Black
and Gold, Arrangement in Grey and Black. The titles were a deliberate signal: don’t look
at this painting and ask what story it tells. Look at it and ask how it’s balanced. He was
moving toward what would later be called abstraction — the idea that a painting is first
and foremost a flat surface with shapes and colours on it, and that the subject is
secondary to the formal arrangement.
Look at how the painting is composed. A vertical strip of grey wall on the left. A
horizontal grey curtain across the top. A framed etching (one of Whistler’s own prints,
called Black Lion Wharf) hanging on the wall in the upper-left quadrant. The seated
figure in absolute profile, almost sculptural, occupying the lower-right two-thirds. The
whole canvas is built out of horizontal and vertical lines — a grid — with the curve of her
bonnet and folded hands as the only soft notes. The palette is grey, black, off-white,
with a single touch of warmth on her face.
If you cover the face with your thumb, you can see what Whistler actually painted: a
still-life arrangement of textures and rectangles. If you remove your thumb, the human
face reasserts itself and the painting becomes something else — a portrait of an old
woman, alone, formidable, somehow holding her own private thoughts. That double
reading is the whole point. The arrangement and the mother are both there. Neither
cancels the other.

How it got to Paris
Whistler submitted the painting to the Royal Academy in London in 1872. The Academy
nearly rejected it, then accepted it under pressure but hung it badly. It was not a
success in London. Whistler hated England’s reception of his work and eventually
pawned the painting. In 1891 the French state bought it directly from him — the first
time France had ever bought a painting by a living American artist for the national
collections. Whistler wrote to a friend: “To have one’s own picture hanging in the
Luxembourg, treated everywhere with deference and respect, is like a dream.” He had
been rescued from English indifference by the French state. The painting moved to the
Louvre in 1925, then here in 1986.
There’s a quiet political moment in this painting being where it is. America’s most
famous portrait, of an American mother, hangs not in any American museum but here in
Paris. It went on loan to American museums twice in the twentieth century — to Chicago
in 1933, to Pasadena in 2015 — but it always came home to the Musée d’Orsay. Anna
