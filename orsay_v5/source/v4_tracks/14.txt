Find the room on level two titled À qui appartiennent ces œuvres ? in French, Who Owns These
Works? in English. It opened in May 2026 and is signposted in multiple languages. Before you go
in, slow your pace. This is a different kind of gallery from anything else in the museum.

Walk in slowly. Notice that the room is quieter than the rooms around it. Notice that
some of the paintings are hung so that you can see their backs. Notice the way the light
is set lower.

What MNR means
The letters MNR stand for Musées Nationaux Récupération — the National Museums
Recovery designation. The story is this. During the Second World War, the German
occupation of France involved the systematic looting of art collections belonging to
Jewish families. Roughly one hundred thousand artworks were taken from French Jews
between 1940 and 1944 — paintings, drawings, sculptures, books, furniture, ritual
objects. Some were taken at gunpoint. Some were sold under coercion. Some were
confiscated by the Vichy regime as their owners were deported to the camps.
After the Liberation, the Allied recovery teams — the so-called Monuments Men, but also
French recovery officers — found about sixty thousand of these works in Germany and
Austria. Most were returned to surviving owners or their heirs. About fifteen thousand
could not be returned because the original owners and their families had been killed and
no heirs could be traced. Most of those were eventually sold off by the French state in
the 1950s. But about two thousand two hundred were kept back for safekeeping under
the MNR designation, held in trust by the French national museums until rightful heirs
could be found.
Two hundred and twenty-five of those MNR works are held by this museum. Most have
been in storage. This room is the first time they have been put on permanent public
display — not as a temporary exhibition, but as a permanent rotating exhibition from
this museum’s holdings, with the explicit aim of bringing the works back into public view
so that descendants of the original owners might recognise them.

What you can see right now
Twelve paintings and one sculpture are on display when this tour goes out. The selection
will rotate over time as more works are brought up from storage. The works currently in
the room include paintings by Renoir, Degas, Rodin, Cézanne, and Alfred Stevens.
One painting I want you to find is by Edgar Degas. The title is Souper au bal — Supper at
the Ball. It’s a ballroom scene from 1879, with elegant figures seated around a dinner
table under chandeliers. Look at it. Now read the wall text next to it. The painting was
acquired in 1919 by Fernand Ochsé, a French Jewish art collector. Ochsé was arrested
by the German occupation forces with his wife Mathilde and deported to Auschwitz in
1944. They were both murdered there. Their art collection was looted. Souper au bal
was recovered after the war. The Musée d’Orsay has held it in trust for over eighty
years, waiting for an heir to come forward. None has been confirmed.

Look at another work nearby — a small Renoir titled Madame Alphonse Daudet, a
portrait of the wife of the novelist Alphonse Daudet. Look at the painting by the Belgian
artist Alfred Stevens of two children: the wall text records that it was acquired “for
Hitler” — those are the exact words on the transaction record — by a German art dealer
at a public auction in Paris in 1942. The original owners have not been traced.

Why the paintings are sometimes hung backwards
Some of the works in this room are hung so that you can see their reverse side. This is
unusual in any museum. The reason is that the backs of these paintings carry their
provenance — the trail of stamps, inventory numbers, dealer labels, transport tags, and
Nazi-era markings that document the path the work took from a Jewish home into
German hands and eventually back to France. The fronts are the art. The backs are the
evidence. The museum is telling you, by hanging them this way, that both sides matter.

Annick Lemoine’s words
The current president of the Musée d’Orsay is Annick Lemoine. When this gallery
opened, she made a statement that I want to read to you, because it explains what this
room is for better than I can. She said: “Behind each painting, each object, often lie
shattered lives — lives disrupted, even destroyed, by the violence of the Nazi regime.
Today, by dedicating a room to these works, the museum hopes both to highlight the
specific issues related to them, and to convey to the public the memory of this dark
period.”
Over the past thirty years, the Musée d’Orsay has returned fifteen MNR works from its
holdings to identified heirs of their original owners. That is fifteen out of two hundred
and forty. The other two hundred and twenty-five are still here, waiting. This room is
part of the search.

Stand for a moment
Stand in this room for a moment longer than you think you need to. Read one wall text
in full. Notice that the people whose paintings these were had names, addresses,
professions, children. Notice that you are standing in front of objects that outlived the
people who loved them. The museum is not asking you to be sad. It is asking you to
know.
When you’re ready, walk on into the Van Gogh suite next door. Track Fifteen is in front
of his 1889 self-portrait. The two rooms speak to each other: the room you’re leaving is
about people whose presence has been erased; the room you’re entering is about a man
who put himself onto a canvas with everything he had, knowing he would not last.
