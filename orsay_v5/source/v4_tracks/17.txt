Rhône
Van Gogh suite · September 1888 · The earlier starry night

◆ Stop & Look

Stay in the Van Gogh suite. Find the painting of a wide dark river under a star-filled night sky, with
golden lights reflected across the water and a couple walking arm-in-arm along the foreground
bank. This is Starry Night Over the Rhône.

Look at the water for a moment before you look at anything else. The reflections of the
gaslamps come down across the river toward you in long broken columns of gold.
This is La Nuit étoilée — Starry Night Over the Rhône — painted by Vincent Van Gogh in
September 1888, on the banks of the river Rhône in the southern French town of Arles.
It was the first of his great night paintings. It is not to be confused with the more famous
Starry Night in the Museum of Modern Art in New York, which Van Gogh painted nine
months later, in June 1889, inside the asylum at Saint-Rémy. This one came first. He was
free, happy, productive, and looking at the actual night sky.

Where he was
Van Gogh had moved to Arles in February 1888, fleeing the cold and the grey of Paris in
search of the southern light he had been reading about. He rented a small house near
the railway station, in the Place Lamartine, and painted it yellow on the outside. He
called it the Yellow House. It was meant to be the headquarters of an artists’ community
he hoped to start with Gauguin and other friends. (Gauguin would come and stay for two
months in the autumn of 1888. The collaboration collapsed in the December ear-cutting
crisis.)
In September 1888, before Gauguin arrived, Van Gogh painted three night paintings in
quick succession. The Café Terrace at Night — the yellow café against a blue starry sky,
currently at the Kröller-Müller Museum in the Netherlands. The Night Café — the green
and red interior with the billiard table, now at Yale. And this one, Starry Night Over the
Rhône, painted from the riverbank a two-minute walk from his front door.

How he painted it

There is a famous legend about how Van Gogh painted this. The legend says that he
wore a straw hat with a ring of lit candles stuck around the brim, so that he could see his
palette in the dark while painting outdoors. The image is wonderful. It’s in every popular
biography. There’s a problem with it: it’s almost certainly not true. The candle-hat story
comes from a 1923 biography by Gustave Coquiot, published thirty-three years after
Van Gogh’s death. Coquiot heard the story from someone who heard it from someone.
Modern Van Gogh scholars — including the team at the Van Gogh Museum in
Amsterdam who control the archive of his letters — consider it folklore. A hat with
burning candles around the brim would have been impractical and dangerous to wear,
and Van Gogh himself never mentions it.
What Van Gogh did write to his brother Theo, in a letter dated September 29, 1888 —
the same week he painted this canvas — was that he painted it “actually at night, under
a gas lamp.” He probably started the painting outdoors on the riverbank, lit by one of
the city’s gas streetlamps, and finished it the next day in his studio. A local newspaper,
L’Homme de Bronze, published a small notice on September 30th saying: “Mr Vincent,
an impressionist painter, works, we are told, in the evening, by the light of the
gaslamps, in one of our squares.” That’s contemporary evidence. It’s a gas-lamp, not a
candle hat.

What he wrote about the colours
In the same letter to Theo, Van Gogh described the painting in extraordinary technical
detail: “The sky is aquamarine, the water is royal blue, the ground is mauve. The town is
blue and purple. The gas is yellow and the reflections are russet gold descending down
to green-bronze. On the aquamarine field of the sky the Great Bear is a sparkling green
and pink, whose discreet paleness contrasts with the brutal gold of the gas.”
Now look at the painting and check. The sky is aquamarine — a blue-green, not pure
blue. The water is royal blue, darker than the sky. The reflections of the gaslamps in the
river are russet-gold falling into green-bronze. He saw all of that, named all of that, and
put all of that on the canvas. There is nothing imprecise about how he was working. He
was making careful, observed colour decisions in a state of intense calm.

The constellation
Look at the stars. Most of them are scattered, but in the upper portion of the sky, just
left of centre, you can find a small pattern that resembles the Big Dipper — the part of
the constellation Ursa Major, the Great Bear, that Van Gogh specifically mentions in his
letter. A modern astronomer has actually pointed out that the Big Dipper would not have
been visible above the southwest horizon where Van Gogh was looking. He had to turn
his head to the north to see it. He included it anyway. The constellation mattered to him
— he had painted it earlier the same month in The Café Terrace at Night. The accuracy

The couple
Now look at the bottom right of the canvas. Two figures, small, walking arm in arm along
the riverbank. A man in a darker coat, a woman in a lighter dress. Van Gogh called
them, in his letter to Theo, simply “two colourful figurines of lovers in the foreground.”
He doesn’t name them. They’re anyone — any two people walking a riverbank at night
together under a starry sky. They’re also the human reason the painting exists. The
whole composition — the gaslamps, the stars, the water — is staged around their walk.
Van Gogh hoped to exhibit this painting publicly. In the summer of 1889, when he was
offered the chance to send two paintings to the Salon des Indépendants in Paris, he
chose this one and Irises. He wrote to Theo: “I don’t want to exhibit anything too mad.”
He thought Starry Night Over the Rhône was the calmer of his recent works. The critic
Félix Fénéon saw it and called Van Gogh “a diverting colourist even in eccentricities like
his Starry Night.” It was one of the few occasions Van Gogh was publicly noticed during
his lifetime.
