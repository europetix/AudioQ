Central nave, level 0 · The peak of academic painting

◆ Stop & Look

Walk back into the central nave on level zero (the ground floor). Find the very large painting on
the central wall — about seven metres wide, a debauched Roman banquet scene with marble
columns, sprawled bodies, and tall white statues looking down on them. This is Thomas Couture’s
Romans of the Decadence. Stand about ten metres back so you can see the whole canvas.

This painting is enormous. Stand back. Let your eye take it in as one composition before
you start picking out the details.
What you’re looking at is Thomas Couture’s The Romans of the Decadence, painted
between 1844 and 1847. It is one of the few paintings in this museum that everyone in
1847 Paris agreed was a masterpiece. It won the gold medal at the 1847 Salon. The
French state bought it immediately. Théophile Gautier called it the most important
painting of the decade. Couture became one of the most famous painters in Europe
overnight.

What it shows
The scene is a debauched Roman feast, set inside a grand classical hall. In the centre,
exhausted aristocrats are draped across couches in various states of undress,
surrounded by spilled wine cups and discarded flower wreaths. The party is ending. The
bodies are slumped. The faces are blank or asleep or staring at nothing.
Now look at the columns. Between each column stands a tall white statue of one of the
founding heroes of the Roman Republic — the ancestors of these very Romans. The
statues are upright, noble, austere. They look down at the orgy below them with cold
stone faces. The contrast is the whole point: the great ancestors stand sober and
unmoving while the descendants collapse in their shadow.
On the far right, two figures stand apart from the party — soberly dressed philosophers,
looking at the scene with what Couture called “deep sadness.” They’re the moral
observers. They’re also, secretly, the painter’s view of his own century. Couture was
using ancient Rome as a coded warning about the France of King Louis-Philippe — the
wealthy bourgeoisie of the 1840s, getting fat on industrial money while the republic’s
founding ideals were forgotten. The 1848 revolution — which overthrew Louis-Philippe —
broke out within a year of this painting being unveiled.

Why this painting matters here

This is the painting that the Impressionists rebelled against. Look at how it’s made:
every figure is carefully posed, every fold of drapery is meticulously rendered, every
brushstroke is polished invisible. The lighting is theatrical. The composition is
symmetrical, classical, balanced. The subject is borrowed from history. The technique is
the technique you learned at the École des Beaux-Arts — the state academy where
French painters were trained, judged, and ranked.
Couture was the model. Hundreds of younger painters tried to paint like him. One of
those younger painters, briefly, was Édouard Manet — who studied in Couture’s studio
from 1850 to 1856 and then walked out, because he couldn’t stand the academic
training another year. We’ll see Manet’s response to this kind of painting later in this
tour, on the same floor, in two canvases that broke the Academy open.
I want to be careful here. The standard story of nineteenth-century French art tells you
that Couture and his Academy were the villains and the Impressionists were the heroes
who saved painting from them. That story is too simple. Couture was not a fraud. He
was a brilliant technician working inside a tradition that genuinely valued moral
seriousness, narrative depth, and craftsmanship. Many of the painters who later became
Impressionists trained inside that same tradition and continued to submit to the official
Salon for years. The break didn’t happen overnight. What changed between Couture and
Monet was not that one was good and the other was bad. It was what painters thought
painting was for. We’ll watch that change happen, painting by painting, all the way
through this tour.
Walk down the Lille side of the nave — the inland side, away from the Seine. Find the
gallery dedicated to academic painters of the 1850s and 60s, and beyond it the
Symbolist room — the Salle du Bord de l’eau. Track Four takes us into that world, briefly,
before we move into Realism and Impressionism.
