Track 16

Van Gogh’s The Church at
Auvers-sur-Oise
Van Gogh suite · June 1890, one month before
5 min

◆ Stop & Look

Stay in the Van Gogh suite. Find the painting of a stone village church with a high pointed roof,
twisting walls, dark windows, and two paths in front of it that fork around the building. The sky
behind is a deep cobalt blue.

Look at the church. It’s a building, but it doesn’t feel like a building. Notice that the walls
bend slightly, as if the stone were moving.
This is The Church at Auvers-sur-Oise, painted by Vincent Van Gogh in June 1890. It is
one of about seventy paintings he made in the last two months of his life. He had been
released from the asylum at Saint-Rémy in May, moved north to be near his brother
Theo in Paris, and settled in the village of Auvers-sur-Oise, about thirty kilometres
northwest of the city, under the loose care of Doctor Paul Gachet. Auvers was a village
of about two thousand people. The church in this painting was — and still is — the main
parish church of the village, a 13th-century Gothic building called Notre-Dame d’Auvers.
You can visit it today; it’s a thirty-minute train ride from Paris.

What’s in the painting
The church fills most of the canvas. Behind it, a deep cobalt blue sky — not a night sky,
but a daytime sky painted as if it were almost solid colour. In the foreground, two paths
fork around the church on either side, meeting again behind it. A single figure — a
peasant woman in a white bonnet — walks along the left-hand path, small and alone.
Now look at the building itself. The walls are not straight. They tilt slightly to one side.
The roof line is not quite horizontal. The pointed apse at the back leans. The Gothic
windows are dark — black holes, not lit. There is no light coming from inside the church.
The whole building has a kind of low pulse to it, as if you were seeing a slightly trembled
photograph.
Van Gogh wrote to his sister Wilhelmina about this painting shortly after he finished it.
He said the colour effect he was after was “more expressive and more sonorous in its

Why I want to be careful here
The standard interpretation of this painting is that the tilting walls and the dark windows
show Van Gogh’s mental state — that the church is buckling because Van Gogh’s mind
was buckling, that the dark windows show his spiritual isolation from organised religion,
that the lone peasant figure represents his loneliness. Those readings are not wrong.
They’re also too easy.
Look at the painting technically. The walls bend because Van Gogh has used short
curved brushstrokes following the contours of the building. He had been studying
Japanese prints for years and had absorbed their habit of subordinating realistic
perspective to expressive line. The dark windows are dark because the actual interior of
a stone Gothic church at midday, seen from outside in bright sun, is dark — the contrast
between the lit stone and the unlit interior is exactly what you see if you stand in front of
Notre-Dame d’Auvers on a summer afternoon. The two forking paths are actual forking
paths around the actual church. The figure of the peasant woman is the kind of figure
Van Gogh added to many of his landscapes to give human scale.
So why does the painting feel charged? Because Van Gogh, by 1890, had developed a
way of using colour and brushstroke that compressed an enormous amount of
psychological pressure into ordinary subjects. The painting is genuinely doing what
people say it’s doing — it does feel like a building under spiritual strain — but it’s doing
it through deliberate technical means, not through unmediated emotional outpouring.
Van Gogh chose every stroke.

The last summer
He had about six weeks left when he finished this painting. He wrote to his mother in
early July saying he was painting daily, well, and was glad to be in the countryside. He
was, by all indications, working harder than he had ever worked. He produced the
portrait of Doctor Gachet, the great late wheat field paintings, the room interiors, the
village landscapes. On the 27th of July 1890, he walked out into a wheat field at the
edge of Auvers, shot himself in the chest with a revolver, walked back to his room at the
Auberge Ravoux, and died two days later, with Theo at his bedside. He was thirty-seven.
Theo died six months later. They are buried next to each other in the village cemetery of
Auvers-sur-Oise. The cemetery is a ten-minute walk from the church in this painting.
