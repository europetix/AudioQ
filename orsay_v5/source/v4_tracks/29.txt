Cézanne’s The Card Players
Post-Impressionist room · The architecture of looking
5 min

◆ Stop & Look

Move into the Post-Impressionist section of level five. Find the painting of two men in dark hats
and dark jackets seated on either side of a wooden table, each holding a hand of playing cards,
with a wine bottle standing in the centre of the table between them. This is Paul Cézanne’s Les
Joueurs de cartes.

Look at the two men first. They’re completely still. They’re not looking at each other.
They’re looking down at their cards. The whole painting feels held in a long silent breath.
This is Les Joueurs de cartes — The Card Players — painted by Paul Cézanne in the early
1890s. Cézanne painted five versions of this scene over about five years, with different
numbers of figures — some with three or four men, some with this stripped-down
two-figure composition. The Orsay version is the smallest and the most concentrated of
the five. The other four are in New York, London, the Barnes Foundation in Philadelphia,

Who Cézanne was
Paul Cézanne was born in 1839 in the southern French city of Aix-en-Provence, where
his father was a successful banker. He moved to Paris in the 1860s to study painting,
became friends with Pissarro, exhibited at the first Impressionist exhibition in 1874, and
was savaged by the critics so badly that he withdrew from Paris and returned to Aix. He
spent most of the rest of his life working alone in Provence, painting the landscape
around his father’s estate, the local labourers, still lifes of apples and oranges in his
studio, and the mountain of Sainte-Victoire visible from his windows.
He was a difficult, anxious man, prone to destroying his own canvases. He worked slowly
— sometimes a single still life took him six months. He had almost no public success
during his lifetime. He died in 1906 at the age of sixty-seven, after collapsing while
painting outdoors in a thunderstorm. He thought of himself as a failure.
Then everything changed. Within five years of his death, younger painters — Matisse,
Picasso, Braque — had identified Cézanne as the most important precursor of the new
century. Picasso said, repeatedly, that Cézanne was “the father of us all.” Matisse said
Cézanne was “the master of us all.” Cubism is unimaginable without him. So is most of
20th-century abstract painting. The man who died thinking he had failed had in fact built
the foundation for modern art.

What he was trying to do
Cézanne wrote, in a letter near the end of his life, what he thought he was trying to do.
He said: “I want to make of Impressionism something solid and durable, like the art of
the museums.” He admired what the Impressionists had done — the broken brushwork,
the capture of fleeting light — but he thought it was unstable. He wanted to build
paintings that would last the way Poussin’s paintings had lasted, the way Velázquez’s
paintings had lasted. He wanted Impressionist perception with classical structure
underneath it.
Look at how he’s built this painting. The two men are essentially geometric forms —
their bodies are cylinders, their hats are domes, their shoulders are wedges. The table
between them is a flat plane. The wine bottle on the table stands as a vertical axis right
down the middle of the canvas, splitting the composition into two near-mirror halves.
The two men are not portraits of two individuals so much as two weighted masses on
either side of a single architectural beam.
Look at the brushwork. The strokes are short and rectangular, applied in patches.
Cézanne called these petites sensations — little sensations — each one a single

recorded perception of colour at a single point. He built the entire visible surface out of

built out of structure, not out of smooth illusion.

What this opened
Now look at how the table tilts. The plane of the tabletop is not in correct perspective.
The wine bottle stands as if it were leaning slightly to one side. The hands of the men
are subtly misshapen — they don’t quite resemble actual anatomy. Cézanne is
deliberately distorting these elements in order to give the painting structural unity. He
has decided that painterly truth matters more than optical accuracy. If the tabletop has
to tilt a few degrees to make the composition balance, then the tabletop tilts.
This is the move that opens twentieth-century painting. Cézanne has given the painter
permission to manipulate perspective, anatomy, and proportion in service of the
painting itself — not in service of the depicted scene. Picasso and Braque took this
insight and ran with it. Within fifteen years of Cézanne’s death, they had broken the
figure into facets and invented Cubism. Mondrian and Malevich took Cézanne’s
geometric impulse further and arrived at pure abstraction. All of it came out of this kind
of painting — the small, obsessive, structural work that a lonely man in Aix did for thirty
years without an audience.
