Track 28

Caillebotte’s The Floor Scrapers
Impressionist Gallery · The labour that built modern Paris
5 min

◆ Stop & Look

Stay on level five in the Impressionist Gallery. Find the horizontal painting of three shirtless men
on their hands and knees on a bare wooden floor, working with hand-held scraper blades, with tall
windows behind them filling the room with cool morning light. This is Gustave Caillebotte’s Les
Raboteurs de parquet — The Floor Scrapers.

Look at the three men working. Notice that the painting takes their labour seriously — it
doesn’t pity them, doesn’t romanticise them, doesn’t turn them into peasants. They’re
working urban tradesmen, and the painting treats them as that.
This is Les Raboteurs de parquet — The Floor Scrapers — painted by Gustave Caillebotte
in 1875. He submitted it to the Salon of 1875. The jury rejected it. He showed it instead
at the second Impressionist exhibition in April 1876, where it became one of the most
discussed paintings in the show. He was twenty-seven years old.

Who Caillebotte was
Gustave Caillebotte is one of the strangest careers in the Impressionist circle. He was
born in 1848 into a fabulously wealthy Parisian family — his father had made a fortune
in military textiles. Gustave inherited enough money in his twenties that he never
needed to work and never needed to sell a painting. He used his money for two parallel
projects. He painted constantly — producing about five hundred canvases over the next
twenty years. And he bought the work of his Impressionist friends, paying them when no
one else would, and built a personal collection of about seventy paintings by Monet,
Renoir, Pissarro, Degas, Manet, Cézanne, and Sisley.

When he died in 1894 at the age of forty-five, of a stroke while pruning roses in his
garden, he left his entire collection to the French state with the condition that it be
exhibited publicly. The state argued for years about whether to accept. Eventually it
took thirty-eight of the paintings. Those thirty-eight paintings became the founding
nucleus of the French national Impressionist collection — the works that moved from the
Musée du Luxembourg to the Louvre to the Jeu de Paume to this museum. The Musée
d’Orsay you are standing in, in a real sense, exists because Caillebotte made it possible.
He was the patron without whom the Impressionists might have gone broke and stopped
working, and he was the donor whose bequest made the public collection possible.

What he was painting
The scene is the interior of an apartment in the new Haussmann Paris of the 1870s.
Three professional floor scrapers are at work refinishing a wooden parquet floor —
stripping the old varnish with hand-held metal blades to prepare the wood for a new
finish. This was a common urban trade in the 1870s. The Haussmann renovations had
built thousands of new apartment buildings across central Paris, and the wealthy
tenants who moved into them required teams of tradesmen to maintain the interiors.
The apartment in this painting was Caillebotte’s own. The windows in the background
look out onto the Rue de Miromesnil in the 8th arrondissement, where his family kept a
townhouse. He had hired the floor scrapers to refinish a room in the house, watched
them work, and decided to paint them. He paid them to keep working while he set up his
easel.

What’s in the painting
Look at how the scene is composed. Caillebotte stands above and behind the workers,
looking down at their bare muscular backs. The wooden floorboards rush away from us
in a steep exaggerated perspective — a vanishing point so aggressive that the floor
seems to tilt up the back wall. The men are arranged in a diagonal line, evenly spaced.
The light comes from the tall iron-railed window in the upper background, falling in long
bands of cool white across the bare wood.
Look at the curled shavings of wood scattered around the men — small, intricate,
painted with the same care Caillebotte gave to skin and fabric. Look at the half-empty
bottle of wine on the floor in the upper left. Look at the men’s thin arms and their bare
feet. Caillebotte has not idealised their bodies — they are wiry, not heroic. They are
working-class men doing skilled physical labour. They are completely absorbed in the
work and barely aware of being painted.

What the critics couldn’t see

The Salon jury rejected this painting in 1875 because they considered the subject
“vulgar.” It was, by the academic categories of the time, beneath the dignity of serious
painting. Floor scrapers were not biblical heroes, not Roman senators, not nymphs in a
forest. They were tradesmen in an apartment. The jury could not accept that the
rebuilding of Paris itself — the actual physical labour of preparing the floors that the
Parisian bourgeoisie walked on — was a legitimate subject for a large oil painting.
Caillebotte’s answer was to put the painting on the wall anyway, at the Impressionist
exhibition, where everyone who came in had to walk past it. The critics in 1876 mostly
praised it. Even the conservative critics conceded that the technique was astonishing.
Within two years, the painting was being talked about as one of the masterpieces of the
new movement. By the time Caillebotte died, it was recognised as his most important
work.
