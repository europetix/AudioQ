Track 31

Café Campana — the closing chord
Level 5, behind the second great clock · The end of the tour
5 min

◆ Stop & Look

Walk to the far end of level five — the upstream end of the building, opposite the clock you stood
behind at the start of Half B. Find the Café Campana, just past the exit of the Impressionist
Gallery. Step inside. You’ll see a strange aquatic interior — orange coral-shaped lamps, gold
scale-pattern chandeliers, organic chair shapes — set against the back of the second great clock
face. Find a seat or a place to stand near the clock window.

You’ve arrived. The end of the tour. Sit if you can. The Café Campana is one of the great
pleasures of this museum, and the right place to end.
This is the Café Campana. It opened in its current form in 2011, as part of the same
renovation that added the bridge to the Pavillon Amont we crossed earlier. The interior
was designed by the Brazilian brothers Fernando and Humberto Campana, the two most
celebrated industrial designers in South America. Their commission was to make a
space that would echo the Art Nouveau decorative arts collection of the museum
without imitating it — a contemporary tribute to the spirit of the 1900 Universal
Exposition, in which Laloux’s station had opened.

Look around
Look at the lamps. They are orange and red glass pieces shaped like coral or sea
anemones, hanging in clusters from the ceiling. The wall behind the bar is panelled in
hammered copper, blue methacrylate, and shimmering scale-patterned metal. The
chairs are organic pebble-shapes. The whole room is meant to feel like the inside of a
strange aquarium — a dream-like underwater chamber suspended at the top of an
iron-and-glass train station.
The Campana brothers were drawn to the work of Émile Gallé, the great French glass
artist of the 1890s whose work fills the Art Nouveau decorative arts rooms downstairs.
Gallé made glass vases shaped like deep-sea creatures, glass lamps shaped like
jellyfish, dragonflies, orchids, fish. The Café Campana is the Campana brothers’ homage

The second clock
And there it is, the second great clock. Look at it from inside the café. The Roman
numerals are visible from this side too, in mirror image — the same iron-and-glass face
as the clock at the other end of the floor, set into the downstream pediment of Laloux’s
station. The whole museum is hung between these two clocks. The art is the body. The
clocks are the bookends.
If you came on a Thursday evening, the café is open until a quarter to nine. The lighting
changes through the afternoon — the sun moves across the western sky, dropping
toward the Seine, and the gold light pours through the clock face into the room. Late
afternoon in this café, in late summer, is one of the great quiet pleasures in Paris.

What you’ve walked through
Now, before you leave, hold the whole tour in your mind for a moment. You started on
the empty forecourt where the rhinoceros should have been. You stood under Laloux’s
glass canopy. You looked at Couture’s twenty-five-foot warning to the bourgeoisie of
1847. You stood in front of Millet’s gleaners and Courbet’s funeral and Manet’s Olympia,
painted 1863, scandalous 1865. You stood in front of Whistler’s mother in her
arrangement of grey and black, and Toulouse-Lautrec’s Jane Avril kicking on a piece of
bare cardboard.
You crossed the bridge to the Pavillon Amont and met the Carpeaux Danse from the
Opéra facade. You stood quietly in the MNR Gallery in front of works whose first owners
were murdered in Auschwitz. You met Van Gogh in three paintings — the asylum
self-portrait, the Auvers church, the Rhône under stars — and you met Gauguin with the
full picture, both his radical use of colour and the children he took as partners.
You came up here, to level five, and stood behind the first great clock. You looked
through it at the Louvre and the Tuileries and Montmartre, and then you turned around
and walked through the Impressionist heart of the museum — Pissarro’s frost, Monet’s
poppy field and steaming station and dissolving cathedral, Renoir’s sunlit Sunday dance,
Degas’s ballet class and his fourteen-year-old in fourth position, Morisot’s cradle,
Caillebotte’s floor scrapers, Cézanne’s card players, and Seurat’s dotted circus next to
Sérusier’s tiny talisman that opened the twentieth century.
Thirty-one tracks. Sixty-six years of European painting. One former train station. Two
clocks. You came in through one of them. You’re leaving through the other.

Where to go next

When you’re ready to leave, the museum exit is back through the central nave. Walk
back the way you came — down through the Post-Impressionist galleries, past the great
clock, down the escalators to level zero, and out through the exit. The exit and entrance
have been separated as part of the current renovation, so the exit is no longer where
you came in.
If you have an extra hour and the day is bright, walk across the Seine to the Tuileries
Garden. The Musée de l’Orangerie, at the western end of the garden, holds Monet’s
great Water Lilies cycle — eight enormous oval panels installed in two oval rooms
shaped like the infinity symbol, designed by Monet himself in the last years of his life.
The Orangerie and the Musée d’Orsay are administered together — you can buy a
combined ticket. The two museums together hold the great closing chord of Monet’s
career: the Impressionist paintings you just walked through, and the late masterworks
that grew out of them.

Goodbye
Thank you for walking the Musée d’Orsay with me. Sixty-six years of painting, two great
clocks, one converted train station saved from demolition by three French presidents —
the building that became the museum of the modern eye.
Zola, defending Manet in 1866, wrote that our duty is to go to the bottom of things.
Every painter on these walls did that, in their own way. So, in your way, do you.
Au revoir, et merci.
