◆ Stop & Look

In the Degas section of the Impressionist Gallery on level five, find the painting of a ballet studio
interior — a wooden-floored room with mirrors, an older male instructor in a dark suit leaning on a
tall walking cane, surrounded by a group of young ballerinas in white tutus practising and resting.
This is La Classe de danse.

Look at the dancers first. Notice that almost none of them are dancing. Most of them are
resting, scratching, adjusting their costumes, leaning against a piano.
This is La Classe de danse — The Ballet Class — painted by Edgar Degas between 1871
and 1874. Degas was the great chronicler of the Paris Opéra ballet — he produced about
fifteen hundred works on the subject across his career, more than half of his total
output. He had a season pass to the Opéra, attended performances and rehearsals for
over forty years, and knew the dancers, the choreographers, and the patrons by name.
He was never interested in the glamour of the stage. He was interested in the work
behind it.

Who Degas was
I want to be honest about Degas, because he was a difficult man. He was born into a
wealthy banking family, was financially independent his whole life, and never had to sell
his paintings to live. He was politically reactionary, deeply antisemitic, and broke with
several of his closest friends — including Pissarro, who was Jewish — over the Dreyfus
Affair in the late 1890s. He was misogynistic in his recorded statements. He never
married and lived alone.
And he was one of the most observant and humane painters of the human body in
motion that the nineteenth century produced. The contradiction is the contradiction. His
work and his politics existed in the same person.

What the painting shows
The man in the dark suit leaning on the cane is Jules Perrot, a real person — a retired
star dancer who became one of the great ballet masters of nineteenth-century Europe.
Perrot ran the Opéra ballet school in the 1860s and 70s. Degas painted him from sittings
in the studio.
The girls around Perrot are les petits rats — the “little rats” — the youngest students of
the Opéra ballet school. They were typically between eight and fourteen years old when
they began. Almost all of them came from poor working-class Parisian families. Their
mothers brought them to the school in hopes of a place in the corps de ballet, which
paid a salary and provided housing. The training was brutal — six days a week, twelve

What Degas did and didn’t see
Look at how Degas has framed the composition. The viewpoint is high and off-centre,
looking down into the studio from an elevated angle, like a balcony seat. The floor takes
up nearly half the canvas. The dancers are crowded into the right side. There’s a large
empty space on the left, with a single dancer scratching her back, oblivious. The whole
composition feels like a snapshot — like a glimpse caught through a doorway. Degas
learned this kind of framing from Japanese woodblock prints and from contemporary
photography. Both were new influences in 1870s Paris. He absorbed them faster than
any other painter of his generation.
Look at the individual dancers. One adjusts her shoulder strap. One stretches her aching
foot. One leans on the piano with her chin in her hands, exhausted. One scratches the
small of her back. None of them look at Perrot or at the viewer. None of them are
performing. Degas has caught them in the gaps between exercises — the unwatched
moments when the dancer is just a young girl in a tutu, tired.

The Opéra system
The Paris Opéra of the 1870s was a complicated institution. It produced great ballet at
the highest technical level. It also operated a system in which wealthy male subscribers,
called abonnés, paid for access to backstage areas where they could approach the
dancers directly. Many of the corps de ballet had patrons. Some of the patronage was
respectful. Much of it was exploitative. The dancers, most of whom came from
working-class families with no other income, had limited ability to refuse. Degas knew
this system. He painted both the dancers and the abonnés repeatedly, often in the same
frame. He was a witness inside it.
Now turn to the small bronze sculpture in this room — a young ballerina, fourteen years
old, standing in fourth position with her chin lifted and her hands clasped behind her
back. Track Twenty-Six is Degas’s Little Fourteen-Year-Old Dancer.
