Track 19

The Great Clock Face
Level 5 · The view through the iron timepiece
4 min

◆ Stop & Look

Take the escalators or elevator up to level five — the top floor of the museum, where the
Impressionist galleries are. Before you enter the Impressionist Gallery, look for the alcove behind
one of the great station clocks. The clock face is a circle of glass and iron set into the wall, with
Roman numerals visible from inside the building. Stand in the alcove behind the clock so you’re
looking out through it.

Stand here for a moment before the paintings. This is the single best view in the
museum.
You’re standing inside one of the original 1900 railway clocks of the Gare d’Orsay. The
clock face is a circle of iron and glass, about six metres across, set into the upstream
pediment of Laloux’s station. The Roman numerals you can see from this side, in mirror
image, are the numerals that visitors approaching the building from the Seine still read
as the time. The clock still works. The gears are kept running.

What you can see through it
Look through the glass. You’re facing northeast, across the Seine. On the far bank,
directly opposite, are the long stone wings of the Louvre and the green of the Tuileries
Garden. Beyond them, in the middle distance, the dome of the Opéra Garnier and the
rooftops of the 9th arrondissement. On a clear day, on the horizon to the north, the

Everything you’ve been looking at downstairs was made in or near the streets you can
see through this glass. The Impressionist movement was a movement of one city. From
this clock, you can see most of it.

Why this view matters
When Victor Laloux designed this station in the 1890s, the clocks were practical objects.
Trains ran on minutes. The platforms below had to be synchronised to the second with
the rest of the French rail network. The clock you’re standing behind was, for forty
years, an industrial instrument — a machine for telling time to travellers.
Then the trains stopped. The platforms emptied. The clock kept running, but for no one
in particular. When Gae Aulenti designed the conversion in the 1980s, she made the
decision to treat the clock not as a leftover but as a viewpoint. She built the alcove
you’re standing in. She made the clock face into a window. The decision turned an
obsolete piece of railway machinery into the most photographed spot in the museum.
There’s a second great clock at the other end of this floor, behind the Café Campana,
where we’ll end this tour. Two clocks, one at each end of the building, framing
everything in between. Notice the symmetry. The whole museum is hung between these
two timepieces.

Looking the other way
Now turn around. Walk into the Impressionist Gallery itself. The lights are lower than in
the rooms downstairs. The walls are painted a deep grey-blue — the recent rehang
chose dark wall colours specifically to make the Impressionist canvases feel as if they’re
generating their own light. You’ll see why in the next track.
Walk into the first room of the gallery, on the Seine side, and find Camille Pissarro’s
Hoarfrost — a small canvas of a peasant carrying a bundle of firewood across a frozen
field with long blue shadows. Track Twenty starts there.
