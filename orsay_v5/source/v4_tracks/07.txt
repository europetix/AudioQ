Track 07

Courbet’s The Artist’s Studio
Same Courbet gallery · The real allegory of seven years
5 min

◆ Stop & Look

Stay in the same Courbet gallery on level zero. Turn to the companion painting — another huge
canvas, showing Courbet himself in the centre, seated at an easel, with a naked model standing
behind him and a small boy watching, surrounded by dozens of figures on either side. This is The
Artist’s Studio.

Stand back from this one too. It’s nearly twenty feet wide. And it’s one of the strangest
paintings in the museum.
Courbet painted this in 1855 and gave it the longest title in the history of art: The
Painter’s Studio: A Real Allegory Summing Up Seven Years of My Artistic and Moral Life.
“A real allegory” is a contradiction in terms — an allegory is an abstract symbolic image,
the opposite of real. Courbet knew that. He was being provocative. He wanted to make a
painting that was both: rooted in the actual people he knew, and at the same time a
statement about what painting was.

The three parts
The composition has three parts. In the centre: Courbet himself, seated at an easel,
painting a landscape of his home region. Behind him stands a naked female model with
a white sheet draped over her hip and her hand on his shoulder. In front of him, a small
boy in a peasant smock watches him paint.
To the left of Courbet: a crowd of common people. A poacher with his dogs. A canvas
merchant. A priest. A beggar woman sitting on the floor with a child. A worker in a
smock. Look at them — these are the people Courbet considered his real subject. The
rural and urban poor of the France of his lifetime.
To the right of Courbet: a crowd of his Parisian friends and supporters. Charles
Baudelaire — the poet, sitting at the far right reading a book. The wealthy patron Alfred
Bruyas, who had been bankrolling Courbet for years. The art critic Champfleury, sitting
on a stool. A couple of bohemian art lovers admiring a painting on the wall. These are

The naked model and the boy
Now look at the central trio again. The naked model is not posing for the landscape
Courbet is painting. She’s just standing there, watching. Critics have been arguing about
who she’s supposed to represent for a hundred and seventy years. Truth? Nature? The
naked reality that the painter has to look at to do his work? Courbet himself called her
“the Muse” in his letters but never explained further. Whatever she is, she is there as a
kind of standing permission — the right of the painter to see things directly.
The small boy in the peasant smock is even stranger. He’s looking up at Courbet with
absolute attention. He’s not painted from any one model; he’s a generic peasant child.
Some critics have read him as the future viewer — the next generation of ordinary
people coming to see what painting can do. Courbet painting toward his audience, with
the audience already inside the painting watching him.

The 1855 rejection
Courbet submitted this canvas, along with thirteen other paintings, to the great
Universal Exhibition of 1855 — the world’s fair that Paris was hosting that year. The jury
accepted eleven of his paintings and rejected three, including this one and A Burial at
Ornans. Courbet was furious. He did something unprecedented. He built his own pavilion
across the road from the official exhibition, paid for it himself, called it the Pavilion of
Realism, and charged admission. He showed forty paintings in his own pavilion, with this
canvas as the centrepiece.
That move — a painter exhibiting his own work outside the official jury system, charging
the public to see it — is the birth of the independent modern art exhibition. Twenty
years later, the Impressionists would do exactly the same thing in 1874, in a former
photographer’s studio in central Paris, when they organised their own exhibition outside
the Salon and invented the name Impressionism. Courbet showed them how it was
done. The Pavilion of Realism is the prototype.
