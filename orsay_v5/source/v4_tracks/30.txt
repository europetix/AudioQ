Two paintings, two opposite roads out of Impressionism. The Seurat is large, scientific,
and unfinished. The Sérusier is tiny, intuitive, and explosive. They’re here together
because the rest of the twentieth century branches from them.

Seurat’s Le Cirque
First, find the large circus scene. This is Le Cirque — The Circus — by Georges Seurat.
He started it in late 1890 and was still working on it when he died of a sudden illness —
either diphtheria or meningitis, the diagnosis was never confirmed — on the 29th of
March 1891. He was thirty-one years old. The painting is technically unfinished. Seurat
had intended to add more figures and resolve the foreground, but he ran out of time.
Walk close to the canvas. Almost touch it with your nose. Look at how the paint is
applied. The whole surface is built out of millions of tiny separate dots of pure colour.
There are no blended areas. There is no brushwork in the traditional sense. Seurat
applied each dot by tapping the brush onto the canvas. The technique is called
pointillism, or sometimes — in Seurat’s own preferred term — divisionnisme,
divisionism.
He invented this technique from a careful study of nineteenth-century colour theory. He
read Michel-Eugène Chevreul and Ogden Rood, two scientists who had worked out the
optical principle that two adjacent dots of pure colour are mixed not on the canvas but
in the viewer’s eye. A dot of pure blue next to a dot of pure yellow does not look like
blue and yellow at viewing distance — it looks like green, but a green more luminous
than any green you could mix on a palette. Seurat decided that this optical mixing was
the future of painting. He spent the last seven years of his life applying it.
Now step back five metres. The dots become forms. The acrobat is balanced on the back
of a white horse. A clown leaps in front. The crowd in the amphitheatre rises behind. The
colour vibrates — actually vibrates, in a way that earlier painting doesn’t. Seurat had
proved his theory. He would not live to develop it further. He left about seven major
canvases. His early death was one of the great losses of nineteenth-century painting.

Sérusier’s Le Talisman
Now find the small painting nearby. It is barely larger than a book — about twenty-seven
by twenty-one centimetres — painted on a wooden cigar-box lid. From a distance it
looks almost abstract, a patchwork of saturated colour. It shows, if you look carefully, a
landscape with a stream, an autumn forest, and a small mill on the right. This is Le
Talisman — The Talisman — painted by Paul Sérusier in October 1888.
The story of this tiny board is one of the founding moments of modern art. Sérusier was
a twenty-four-year-old painter visiting the village of Pont-Aven in Brittany in the autumn
of 1888. Paul Gauguin was also there. Gauguin had become, in his mid-forties, a kind of

guru for younger painters — he was developing his radical theory that colour did not
need to describe reality but could be used purely for its emotional and decorative effect.
Sérusier asked Gauguin for a lesson. Gauguin walked him out into the Bois d’Amour, a
small wood outside Pont-Aven, set up a wooden board, and watched Sérusier paint.
As Sérusier started to paint the landscape in normal naturalistic colours, Gauguin
interrupted him. The exact exchange has been recorded many times, with slight
variations. The version most commonly told goes like this. Gauguin asked: “How do you
see these trees? They are yellow? Well then, put down yellow. And that shadow, rather
blue? Don’t be afraid to paint it as blue as possible. And those red leaves? Use
vermilion.”
Sérusier painted what Gauguin told him to paint. The result — the small board you’re
looking at — wasn’t a representation of a forest. It was an arrangement of pure flat
colour patches. Yellow path, blue shadow, red foliage, green water. The landscape was
barely identifiable. Sérusier brought the small board back to Paris and showed it to a
group of his fellow art students — Maurice Denis, Pierre Bonnard, Édouard Vuillard. They
were astonished. They called the board le Talisman, the talisman, because it seemed to
contain a magical principle. Around this small painting they formed the group called the
Nabis — the prophets — dedicated to the principle that colour is freed from description
and free to be used for its own emotional power.

What these two paintings opened
These two paintings are the two main routes out of Impressionism. Seurat took the
Impressionist observation of light and made it scientific — broken into measured dots,
applied by a system, derived from optical theory. That route fed into systematic
modernism — the rigorous, theory-driven art of the early twentieth century. Sérusier
and Gauguin took the Impressionist freedom of colour and pushed it further — dropped
the observation entirely, freed colour from any reference, made painting into pure
emotional structure. That route fed into Matisse, into the Fauves, into German
Expressionism, into all of the colour-driven modernism that followed.
Two paintings, made within three years of each other, by two young men who knew
each other through the Paris art world. Between them, they invented the next century.
Seurat died at thirty-one without finishing his great circus painting. Sérusier lived
another forty years and spent most of them as a quiet professor of decorative arts,
painting religious subjects — he never produced another work as important as the small
board he made under Gauguin’s direction in one afternoon in Brittany when he was
twenty-four.
