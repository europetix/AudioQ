Monet’s Gare Saint-Lazare
Impressionist Gallery · Inside the industrial age

◆ Stop & Look

Stay in the Impressionist Gallery. Find the painting showing the inside of a train station — an

dark locomotive shape coming forward through the smoke. This is Monet’s La Gare Saint-Lazare.

ORSAY

Musée d’Orsay · Paris

Look at this painting and notice where you are. You’re standing in an old train station
looking at a painting of a train station. Monet painted this 1877. The Orsay opened as a
station 1900. The painting is twenty-three years older than the building you’re standing
in. That gap is the whole nineteenth century in miniature.
This is La Gare Saint-Lazare, painted by Claude Monet in January 1877. It is one of
twelve canvases he produced that winter, all of the same station from slightly different
angles. Seven of the twelve were shown at the third Impressionist exhibition in April
1877. This was the painting that established, for everyone watching, that Monet could
turn the industrial city itself — not just the countryside — into a subject for serious
painting.

The Gare Saint-Lazare
The Gare Saint-Lazare was, in 1877, the busiest train station in Paris. It still is. It’s the
terminus for trains coming from Normandy and the western suburbs — including the line
that ran out to Argenteuil, where Monet was living. He took this train constantly. He
knew the station from the inside, as a daily commuter. When he decided to paint it, he
had been looking at it for years.
What he did to get permission to paint it from inside is a famous story. He dressed in his
best clothes, walked into the office of the director of the Western Railway, presented his
card, and announced that he had decided to paint the Gare Saint-Lazare and would
require the trains to be held at the platforms, the locomotives ordered to produce extra
steam, and the platforms cleared of passengers during his working hours. The director,
charmed by Monet’s confidence, agreed to all of it. Monet worked for several weeks
inside the station with the entire facility partly at his disposal.

What he was painting
Look at the canvas. The locomotive is barely there. You can see its black mass coming
forward at the bottom of the painting, but Monet hasn’t drawn its mechanical details.
The actual subject of the painting is not the train. The subject is the atmosphere — the
cloud of pale blue and white steam trapped under the iron-and-glass triangular roof, the
cold winter light coming through the smoke, the colour shifts where the steam meets
the sky and the sky meets the iron.
Look at the colours of the steam. Blue, grey, lavender, pale pink, almost-white. The
steam is not white. The steam is everything around the steam, reflected and refracted.
This is what Monet was after for his whole career: not the object, but the light at the
moment it touches the object. The industrial city had made a new kind of atmosphere —
a fusion of nature and machine, where coal smoke and engine steam and winter sun and
glass roofs combined into something that hadn’t existed in nature before. Monet was

Why this matters here
Stand back and think about where you are. The Orsay opened in 1900 as a more
advanced version of the Gare Saint-Lazare — electrified, so it didn’t need the steam.
Laloux looked at the kind of atmosphere Monet had painted twenty-three years earlier
and decided his station would not produce it. The Orsay’s trains pulled in clean and
silent on electric current. The great glass roof above you was never filled with the
blue-grey cloud you see in this painting. The Orsay was, in a real sense, the architectural
answer to Monet’s Gare Saint-Lazare — a station that wanted to be a palace, lit by
daylight, not darkened by industrial smoke.
And then, eighty-six years after the Orsay opened, the building became a museum, and
Monet’s painting of the Saint-Lazare steam came to live here. The painting of the smoky
station hangs inside the clean station. The Orsay’s glass roof is over your head, and the
Saint-Lazare’s glass roof is on the canvas, and they were both designed by the same
generation of French engineers. The frame keeps closing on itself.
