◆ Stop & Look

Walk into the Manet/Monet gallery on level zero, Seine side. Find the painting of a nude woman
lying on a bed, staring directly at you, with a Black maid behind her holding a bouquet of flowers
and a black cat at her feet. This is Olympia. Stand close enough to meet her eyes.

Stop and let her look at you for a moment before anything else. That eye contact is the
painting’s first weapon.
This is Olympia, painted by Édouard Manet in 1863. He didn’t submit it to the Salon for
two years. When it was finally shown, at the 1865 Salon, it produced what one
contemporary called “the greatest scandal of the century” in Parisian art. The painting
was hung high — above eye level, near the ceiling — partly to protect it from being
attacked by visitors with walking sticks and umbrellas. Two policemen were posted in
the room to keep order. Critics called the model “a female gorilla” and “the Queen of
Spades stepping out of the bath.” Manet went home shaken. His friend Baudelaire wrote
to him: “You are only the first in the decrepitude of your art” — which sounds like an
insult, but was Baudelaire’s way of saying the future belonged to him.

Two dates: 1863 and 1865
I want to clear up a date confusion that runs through a lot of writing about this painting.
Manet painted Olympia in 1863. The same year, he submitted a different painting to the
Salon — Le Déjeuner sur l’herbe, the picnic with the naked woman, which we’ll look at
next. Déjeuner was rejected by the jury and shown at the special Salon des Refusés —
the Salon of Rejected Works — that Napoleon III set up in 1863 for paintings the jury had
thrown out. Déjeuner caused the first Manet scandal. Then Manet held Olympia in his
studio for two years before showing it. The 1865 Salon is where Olympia exploded. Two
paintings, two scandals, two years apart. People often blur them. The dates matter.

Why she was scandalous
Manet was working in a tradition. Nude women on beds, looking at the viewer, had been
a subject in Western painting since the Renaissance. Titian’s Venus of Urbino, painted in
1538, is the famous prototype — and Olympia is directly modelled on it. Manet had been
to Florence, he had stood in front of the Titian, he had taken notes. So why did one
painting (the Titian) become a Renaissance masterpiece and the other (the Manet)
cause a riot in Paris?

The difference is the details. Titian’s figure is a goddess: Venus, classical, mythological,
set in a Renaissance interior, softly lit. Manet’s figure is a contemporary woman. The
black ribbon around her neck was a Parisian fashion accessory. The orchid in her hair,
the gold bracelet, the satin slippers — these were the accessories of a high-class
Parisian courtesan. The name “Olympia” was a known pseudonym for a sex worker in
Paris in the 1860s. Her left hand isn’t modestly placed — it’s firmly held, in control. She
isn’t inviting you. She’s weighing you up. The black cat at her feet, instead of the
sleeping dog at Venus’s feet, was the final detail. The implications didn’t need to be
spelled out.
Look at the maid behind her, holding the bouquet wrapped in paper. The maid was
painted from a model named Laure, a Black woman living in Paris in the 1860s — Manet
recorded her name in his sketchbook. Modern scholarship has rediscovered her, and
Orsay itself held a major exhibition in 2019 called Le modèle noir — The Black Model —
specifically to put Laure back into the painting’s history. The flowers she’s holding are a
gift from a client — wrapped in paper, casual, the sort of thing that gets delivered
between appointments.

What Manet was actually doing
The painting is not pornographic. It’s not even particularly erotic by the standards of its
century — the Salons were full of nudes far more sexually displayed than Olympia. What
scandalised people was the transparency. Manet refused the alibi. He wouldn’t pretend
his nude was a goddess. He wouldn’t even pretend she was beautiful in the academic
sense — her skin is flat-lit, almost greenish in places, with none of the soft rounded
modelling the Salon demanded. He painted her as the thing she actually was: a working
Parisian woman whose body was her trade, looking directly at the wealthy man who
would be the Salon’s typical viewer, and pricing him in real time. The wealthy Parisian
men who visited brothels at night and demanded purity in art by day found themselves
looking at their own client relationship, hung on a wall, in public, framed as art.
Manet wasn’t trying to start a revolution. He thought of himself as a serious painter
working in the great tradition of Velázquez and Goya. He wanted Salon recognition. He
wanted official acceptance. He didn’t get it. What he got, instead, was a generation of
younger painters — Monet, Renoir, Degas, Pissarro, Cézanne, all the people we’re going
to meet on level five — who looked at Olympia and understood that the rules were over.
After 1865, you could paint anything. The painters of light who came after Manet were
possible because Manet had done this first.
Look at the wall next to Olympia. The other great early Manet is right there — the picnic
that started the trouble two years earlier. Track Eight is in front of Le Déjeuner sur
l’herbe.
