◆ Stop & Look

Stay on level five in the Impressionist Gallery. Find the bright painting of a poppy field — a slope
of green grass scattered with bright red poppy flowers under a pale summer sky, with a woman in
a white dress and a small child in the foreground. This is Claude Monet’s Coquelicots, sometimes
called Poppy Field near Argenteuil.

Look at the red of the poppies. Now look more closely. The individual flowers are barely
painted at all — they’re short dabs of pure vermilion, almost slashed onto the surface.
This is Les Coquelicots — The Poppies, sometimes translated Poppy Field — painted by
Claude Monet in 1873. Like the Pissarro you just left, it was shown at the first
Impressionist exhibition in April 1874. The two paintings hung in the same rooms in the
photographer Nadar’s old studio on the Boulevard des Capucines. They are, together,
the founding documents of the movement.

Where and when
Monet painted this near the town of Argenteuil, on the Seine northwest of Paris, where
he lived from 1871 to 1878. These were the happiest years of his life. He had just
returned from two years in England — he had fled there during the Franco-Prussian War
of 1870-71 — he had a young wife, Camille Doncieux, and a small son, Jean. He was
poor but he was working constantly. His dealer Paul Durand-Ruel was buying everything
he produced. The countryside of the lower Seine valley, with its meadows and willows
and slow-moving river, gave him the subject matter he had been looking for.
The woman and child in the foreground are Camille and Jean. They appear in many
Monet paintings of these years. The second pair of figures in the background — you can
just make them out, higher on the hillside, near the trees — are also Camille and Jean.
Monet painted them twice, at two distances, to guide your eye through the field.

Look at the poppies
Move closer to the canvas. Look at how Monet has painted the individual flowers. Each
poppy is one short stroke of red paint, or sometimes two strokes, applied wet on wet
without outline. Some are larger, some almost dots. There’s no drawing underneath. The
flowers don’t have stems. They are just red marks scattered across the green slope.
Now back up two metres. The dabs become flowers. Your eye fills in the bodies of the
flowers, the stems, the slight movement in the breeze. The whole field comes alive.
Monet was working from a discovery he and his friends had been testing for years: the
eye builds a coherent image out of broken information faster and more vividly than the
eye reads a smoothly modelled illusion. Roughness, applied with precision, makes the
painting feel more alive than polish.

The 1874 exhibition
The 1874 exhibition was organised by Monet, Pissarro, Degas, Renoir, Cézanne, Berthe
Morisot, Sisley, and a few others, all of them frustrated with the official Salon. They had
been rejected too many times. They formed a society called the Société anonyme des
artistes peintres, sculpteurs et graveurs — the Anonymous Society of Painters,
Sculptors, and Engravers — and put on their own show without official approval. They
rented the photographer Nadar’s studio, hung 165 works on the walls, and opened the
doors to a paying public. Thirty-five-hundred people came over the four weeks. Most of
the reviews were mocking. Almost nothing sold.
But it was the start of the movement that this whole floor is built around. Eight
Impressionist exhibitions followed between 1874 and 1886. Pissarro was at all eight.
Monet was at four. Degas was at seven. Cassatt was at four. Manet, ironically, never
showed at any of them — he kept submitting to the official Salon to the end of his life.

What happened to the painting
Monet sold Coquelicots to the collector Étienne Moreau-Nélaton, who gave it to the
French state in 1906. It has been in this collection ever since. In June 2024, a climate
activist with the group Riposte Alimentaire glued a paper poster of what the field would
look like in the year 2100 over the front of the painting, and superglued her hand to the
wall beside it. The painting was protected by glass and was not damaged. The activist
was arrested. The protest was meant to argue that the world Monet painted is being
made unliveable by climate change. The painting was on the wall again within a week.
