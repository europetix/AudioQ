◆ Stop & Look

Stay in the Degas section of the Impressionist Gallery. Find the small bronze sculpture of a young
ballerina standing in fourth position — chin lifted, hands clasped behind her back, one foot
forward, wearing a real fabric tutu. Walk around it; it rewards being seen from every angle.

Walk around her. Notice that her tutu is made of actual fabric. Notice the satin ribbon in
her hair. Notice that she is not smiling and not posing for you.
This is La Petite Danseuse de quatorze ans — The Little Fourteen-Year-Old Dancer —
first exhibited by Edgar Degas in April 1881 at the sixth Impressionist exhibition. The
version you’re looking at is a bronze cast made after Degas’s death — the museum
holds one of about twenty-eight casts of this sculpture, made in the early 1920s. The
original, which Degas exhibited in 1881, was very different from this bronze.

What the original was made of
The original 1881 sculpture was made of pigmented beeswax over a metal armature,
with real human hair on her head, a real satin ribbon, a real linen bodice, a real fabric
tutu, and real ballet slippers. The wax was tinted to look like skin. The whole figure was
presented inside a glass display case, like a specimen in a natural history museum. She
was about two-thirds life size. She stood at eye level with the people who came to look
at her.
Visitors in 1881 didn’t know what they were seeing. Sculpture, by every
nineteenth-century convention, was made of marble or bronze. It depicted gods, heroes,
allegorical figures — the permanent and the elevated. Degas had produced something
that looked like a wax-museum exhibit. Worse, he had not idealised the model. The wax
girl was thin, plain-featured, with the slightly forward-jutting jaw of a working-class child.
The critics were appalled.

What they said
The reviews from the 1881 show are some of the harshest in the history of sculpture
criticism. One critic called her “a monkey, an Aztec.” Another said she was “a flower of
precocious depravity, with her face marked by the hateful promise of every vice.” A
third called for the sculpture to be removed from the exhibition. The objections were
partly about technique — the use of wax and fabric — but they were also unmistakably
about class. The critics were reading the girl’s working-class face as a moral defect.
They were saying the sculpture had brought a kind of person they didn’t want to see

Degas was so wounded by the reception that he never publicly exhibited a sculpture
again. He continued to model in wax for the next thirty-six years of his life — producing
about a hundred and fifty wax sculptures of dancers, horses, and bathers — but he kept
them in his studio. The whole sculpted side of his career was hidden until after his
death. The bronze casts were made by his heirs from the wax originals starting in 1919.

Who she was
The model for the sculpture was a real fourteen-year-old ballet student at the Opéra
named Marie van Goethem. She was Belgian by birth, lived in poverty in Montmartre
with her two sisters and her mother, and was one of the petits rats we talked about a
moment ago in front of The Ballet Class. She sat for Degas in his studio for the better
part of 1880, in exchange for a small fee. Her family needed the money. Her mother
sent both of her sisters to sit for Degas at different points as well.
What happened to Marie van Goethem after this sculpture was shown is partly
documented and partly lost. Sometime in 1882 she was dismissed from the Opéra ballet
school for missing too many rehearsals. After that she disappears from the official
records. Her older sister, Antoinette, was arrested for theft in 1881. Her younger sister,
Charlotte, became a career dancer and lived until 1945. Marie’s fate is unknown. She is
the most famous teenager in the history of European sculpture, and we don’t know how
she died, or where, or when.

What this sculpture is for
Stand close to her. Look at her face. Her eyes are half-closed — not from sleep, but from
the concentration of holding fourth position. Her chin is lifted slightly, in the way that
ballet teachers tell students to lift the chin. Her hands are clasped behind her back, in
the way that makes the shoulders open. She is doing her job. She is fourteen. She is
good at it.
Degas’s sculpture is not a celebration of ballet, and it is not a denunciation of ballet. It is
a witness statement. He saw her, in the rehearsal studios where most of the public
didn’t go, and he made a record of what she actually looked like. The hostile critics of
1881 understood, correctly, that the sculpture was indicting them — not for what they
had done to Marie van Goethem personally, but for the system that they all participated
in. The sculpture asked them to look at her. They refused, and they punished Degas for
the request. Now, a hundred and forty years later, we can do what they couldn’t. We can
look at her.
