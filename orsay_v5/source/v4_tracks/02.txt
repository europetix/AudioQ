Laloux’s Station
Under the glass roof · The building that nearly wasn’t a museum
5 min

◆ Stop & Look

You’re inside the museum, in the central nave. The huge vaulted glass ceiling is above you.
Sculptures line the central walkway. Walk to a position where you can see down the full length of
the nave — from the entrance side to the far wall with the great clock. Then start the track.

Look up. Take a moment with the ceiling before you take a moment with anything else.
What you’re standing in is not a building that was designed as a museum. It’s a building
that was designed as a train station, rescued from demolition in the 1970s, and
converted into a museum in the 1980s. The conversion is one of the best decisions Paris
has made in the last fifty years.

Victor Laloux’s problem
The architect was Victor Laloux. The year was 1898. The Compagnie du Chemin de fer
de Paris à Orléans, the railway company for the southwest of France, needed a new
terminal station in the centre of Paris in time for the 1900 World’s Fair. The site they
chose was directly across the Seine from the Louvre and the Tuileries gardens — prime
real estate, surrounded by classical stone buildings and the Beaux-Arts establishment of
the Third Republic.
Laloux had a problem. A train station in 1900 meant steam, smoke, soot, iron, and the
kind of industrial architecture that had been raised in Paris around the Gare du Nord and
the Gare Saint-Lazare. He could not put a soot-stained iron warehouse directly opposite
the Louvre. The neighbourhood would not allow it, the Beaux-Arts academy would not
allow it, the state would not allow it.
So Laloux did something clever. He built two buildings in one. On the outside, a
Beaux-Arts palace — pale limestone, classical ornament, the kind of facade that
belonged on the Seine quay across from the Louvre. On the inside, the most advanced
train station of its era — a single vast glass-and-iron vault, 138 metres long, 40 metres
wide, electrified from the start. The first electrified urban rail terminal in Europe. No
steam to stain the stone. No smoke to choke the platforms.
Look up again. That barrel vault above you is the original 1900 structure. The glass roof
is a listed historic monument — it’s being restored as we speak, as part of the current
renovation. The decorative coffered rosettes you see in the ceiling are plaster, masking
the iron. The iron weighs about twelve thousand tons. The whole canopy is essentially

Forty years as a working station
The station opened on the 14th of July 1900. For forty years it was a working terminal —
trains from the southwest of France arriving at platforms right where you’re standing. In
1939 the platforms became too short for the longer electric trains then coming into
service, and most of the long-distance traffic was diverted to Austerlitz. The building
drifted out of railway use.
What happened to it next is a strange list. During the Second World War, it was a
mailing centre for parcels sent to French prisoners of war in Germany. After the
Liberation, it was a reception centre for returning French prisoners and deportees. In
1962, Orson Welles filmed parts of The Trial here, using the empty station hall as the
warped Kafkaesque court. By the 1970s, the building was a candidate for demolition. A
luxury hotel chain wanted to knock it down and put up a glass tower.

Saved
It was Georges Pompidou — the president of France from 1969 to 1974 — who first
proposed turning the station into a museum. Pompidou died before the plan was
realised, but Valéry Giscard d’Estaing, the next president, kept the project alive.
François Mitterrand, the next president after that, inaugurated the museum on the 9th
of December 1986. Three French presidents touched this project. The Italian architect
Gae Aulenti designed the interior — the stone terraces, the sculpture nave, the way the
light falls. That intervention is also now a listed heritage element. When the museum
talks about “blending heritage and modernity” in the current renovation, what they
mean is preserving Laloux’s 1900 bones and Aulenti’s 1986 conversion.
Now look down the nave toward the far wall. You can see the shape of the old train
platforms in the stepped stone terraces either side of the central walkway. The
sculptures are placed along what used to be the track bed. The far end of the nave leads
up to the great clock. We’ll get there at the start of Half B. First — the first painting on
this tour, a twenty-five-foot-wide canvas hanging here on level zero that taught a whole
generation of French painters what to rebel against. Walk to the central wall of the nave,
find Thomas Couture’s Romans of the Decadence, and stand about ten metres back.
