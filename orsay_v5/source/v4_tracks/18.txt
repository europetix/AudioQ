ORSAY

Musée d’Orsay · Paris

This is Arearea — the Tahitian word for Joyousness or Merriment — painted by Paul
Gauguin in 1892, during his first stay on the island of Tahiti. Gauguin was forty-four
years old when he made it. He had been on the island for about a year.

What you see
The ground is divided into flat ribbons of saturated colour — a yellow-orange band in the
foreground, a green band behind it, and pink and lavender bands beyond. Gauguin has
thrown out naturalistic landscape colour. There is no attempt to make the grass look like
grass. He uses the ground colour the way a stained-glass artist uses colour — as a flat
decorative field, chosen for emotional intensity, not realistic resemblance.
In the foreground, two seated Tahitian women in long printed dresses. The woman on
the right is playing a small wooden flute. The woman on the left looks straight out at the
viewer. Between them, a small bright red dog — a real red, not a stylised redness.
Behind them, in the middle distance, a procession of smaller figures dances around
what appears to be a tall carved wooden idol or god-figure. Gauguin was inventing this
idol — the Polynesian religious imagery he painted was largely imaginary, since the
French colonial administration and Christian missionaries had suppressed traditional
Tahitian religion decades before he arrived.

Who Gauguin was
Paul Gauguin was born in Paris in 1848. He spent his childhood partly in Peru, with his
mother’s family. As an adult he worked for years as a successful stockbroker in Paris,
married a Danish woman named Mette Gad, and had five children with her. He painted
on the weekends. After the 1882 stock-market crash, he lost most of his income, and in
his late thirties he decided to leave his job and family and become a full-time painter.
He spent the next decade wandering — Brittany, Martinique, Provence (where he stayed
with Van Gogh in Arles for two months in 1888, the disastrous collaboration that ended
in Van Gogh’s ear-cutting), and eventually French Polynesia. He arrived in Tahiti in June
1891, returned to France briefly in 1893, and went back to the Pacific for good in 1895,
this time eventually moving to the more remote Marquesas Islands, where he died in
1903 of syphilis at the age of fifty-four.

What he was looking for, and what he found
Gauguin’s public story about why he went to Tahiti was about art. He wrote that Europe
had become artistically dead, that Western painting had run out of fresh material, and
that he needed to find what he called a “primitive” culture untouched by Western
civilisation in order to renew his work. He talked about searching for a paradise. He
wrote his autobiography Noa Noa, in which he described his Tahitian life in romantic,
mystical terms.

The reality on the ground was different. By the time Gauguin arrived in 1891, Tahiti had
been a French colony for nearly fifty years. The capital, Papeete, was a colonial port with
a Catholic cathedral, a French governor, French shops, and a European-dressed mixed
population. Traditional Polynesian religion had been largely outlawed. The local
population had been devastated by introduced diseases — from an estimated forty
thousand before European contact to about six thousand when Gauguin arrived. The
“primitive paradise” he claimed to find was largely a fiction he constructed for himself
and for his Paris market.

The part that has to be said
I want to say this directly, because it is now part of how this painter is officially
discussed at the Musée d’Orsay and at every major museum that holds Gauguin’s work.
Within a few weeks of arriving in Tahiti, Gauguin entered into a relationship with a
Tahitian girl named Teha’amana, whom he described as his “wife.” She was thirteen
years old. He was forty-three. The relationship lasted for parts of his first stay on the
island. He took at least two other Polynesian girls as partners over the next decade —
Pahura, also thirteen when their relationship began, and Vaeoho, fourteen. He fathered
several children. He had syphilis, which he had contracted in France, and which he
transmitted to multiple sexual partners.
Gauguin himself wrote about these relationships openly in letters and in Noa Noa. He
saw nothing wrong with them. The French colonial administration in Tahiti saw nothing
wrong with them. By the standards of his time and his colonial context, they were
unremarkable. By the standards now applied to him by Pacific scholars, by survivors’
descendants in Polynesia, and increasingly by museum curators in Europe — they were
the sexual exploitation of children by a powerful European man inside a colonial
structure that had stripped them of protection.

What you do with the paintings
I am not telling you not to look at Arearea. The painting is here, it changed the course of
European art — every Post-Impressionist who followed Gauguin owes him something,
Matisse most of all — and you should look at it carefully. The use of flat colour as
emotional carrier rather than as description was a genuine invention. Gauguin gave
painters permission to use colour the way composers use a key change.
What I’m asking you to do is hold two things at once. The first: this canvas is a genuine
artistic achievement. The second: it was made by a forty-four-year-old French colonist
living with a thirteen-year-old, on an island whose people had been almost annihilated
by European contact, and whose religion he both suppressed and then re-invented for
his market. The two seated women in the foreground are not generic figures. They are
likely people he knew personally. They had names. They had lives. We mostly don’t

know what they thought of him, because they didn’t leave written records and he didn’t

Stand with the painting a moment longer. Notice the small red dog. Notice the
procession in the background. Notice the Tahitian language word that Gauguin gave the
painting as a title. He chose to use the local word — not a French word. That was a kind
of recognition. The painting is full of these moments of partial acknowledgment, side by
side with the larger fact of his presence there. Both are true at once.
