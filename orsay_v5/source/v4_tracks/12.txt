Rodin on the terrace
Walk along the Rodin terrace. The Musée d’Orsay has a substantial Rodin holding even
though most of his work is in the dedicated Musée Rodin a kilometre away. The Orsay
Rodins include casts of The Thinker, The Age of Bronze, and the monumental Gates of
Hell, his unfinished doorway commissioned for a museum of decorative arts that never
got built. Look closely at the surfaces of his bronzes — thumbprints, tool-marks, the
ridges where he pinched the clay with his fingers. Rodin left the marks of making visible.
The Academy wanted bronze that looked like polished stone. Rodin wanted bronze that
looked like a person had wrestled it into being. His surfaces flicker with movement even
when the figures are still.
There’s also a Camille Claudel in this area worth seeking out — L’Âge mûr, The Mature
Age, sometimes called Maturity. Claudel was Rodin’s student, lover, and collaborator for
fifteen years. L’Âge mûr shows three figures: a young woman on her knees, naked,
reaching out to a man being led away by an older woman with wings. Claudel never said
it was autobiographical, but everyone who saw it understood. Rodin was the man,
Rodin’s long-term partner Rose Beuret was the older woman, and Claudel herself was
the kneeling figure. Rodin used his influence to try to suppress the work. Claudel made it
anyway. She was later institutionalised by her family at the age of forty-eight and spent
the last thirty years of her life in a psychiatric hospital. L’Âge mûr is one of the most
direct emotional sculptures of the century.

The new bridge to the Pavillon Amont
Now find the bridge across to the Pavillon Amont. This bridge is one of the most recent
architectural additions to the museum — added during a major reorganisation around
2011-2012 that expanded the upper-level gallery space by about four hundred square
metres. The Pavillon Amont is the pavilion at the upstream end of the old station —
“amont” is French for “upstream”. The Pavillon Aval (“downstream”) is at the other end.
Once you’re across the bridge, you’re in the Pavillon Amont decorative arts section. The
space holds the museum’s collection of modern decorative pieces from 1905 to 1914 —
large Art Nouveau room settings, furniture, glass, ceramics — and at the centre of it, the
great Nabi decorative works: large mural-scale panels by Pierre Bonnard, Édouard
Vuillard, Maurice Denis, and Paul Sérusier.

Who the Nabis were
The Nabis were a group of young painters who met as students in Paris around 1888 and
called themselves les Nabis — the Hebrew word for “prophets.” Their founding
inspiration was a tiny landscape painted by Sérusier under Gauguin’s direct instruction

— a work we’ll meet at the end of this tour on level five. The Nabis took Gauguin’s idea
— that colour could be freed from realistic description and used purely for its own
emotional and decorative power — and applied it to large-scale interior decoration. They
painted folding screens, room panels, stage curtains. They made painting domestic
again, after the Impressionists had made it monumental.
The Pavillon Amont is where Orsay houses these large Nabi panels in a setting that
approximates the kind of bourgeois Parisian interior they were originally made for. It’s a
quieter floor than the Impressionist gallery on level five. Most visitors rush past it on the
way to Van Gogh. I’d encourage you to slow down here for a few minutes. The Nabis
were the bridge between Gauguin and the early twentieth century. Matisse came out of
them.
Walk back across the bridge toward the main building. We’re going to look at one
sculpture in particular before we move on — the original plaster of Carpeaux’s La Danse,
which was carved for the facade of the Paris Opera. Track Thirteen.
