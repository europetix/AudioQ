You’re in the Impressionist Gallery on level five. Find the small landscape by Camille Pissarro of a

Look at the shadows on the field before anything else. Notice what colour they are.
This is Gelée blanche — Hoarfrost — painted by Camille Pissarro in 1873. It was one of
the works he showed at the first Impressionist exhibition in April 1874, the show that
gave the movement its name. We’re going to spend most of the next hour on level five
with the painters who exhibited at that 1874 show and the seven Impressionist
exhibitions that followed it. Pissarro is the right one to start with. He was the only
painter who exhibited in all eight.

Pissarro himself
Camille Pissarro was born in 1830 on the island of St Thomas, in what was then the
Danish West Indies and is now the U.S. Virgin Islands. His father was a Sephardic Jewish
merchant. Pissarro moved to Paris in 1855 to study painting, never went home, and
spent the rest of his life in France. He was the oldest member of the Impressionist circle
by nearly a decade, and the most generous — he was the painter who mentored
Cézanne in the early 1870s, taught Gauguin to paint in the early 1880s, and quietly held
the group together through years of poverty, ridicule, and infighting. Without Pissarro
the Impressionists might have stayed a loose set of friends. With him, they became a
movement.

What he’s painting
The scene is a winter morning in the countryside near Pontoise, the town northwest of
Paris where Pissarro lived. A single peasant walks across a harvested field carrying a
bundle of firewood. The fields have been ploughed in long strips and left for winter. A
thin frost — hoarfrost, the white feathery frost that forms overnight on cold ground —
lies across the earth. The sun is low, coming from the right of the frame, casting long
shadows across the strips.

What you should actually look at
Now look at the shadows. They are not grey. They are not black. They are deep blue,
dark violet, soft purple. The lit strips of earth between the shadows are not white —
they’re a warm yellow-pink, almost the colour of cream and rust. Pissarro is doing
something specific and new. He has stopped using academic shadow colour, which was

always made by adding black or umber to the local colour of the object. Instead, he’s
using a discovery from contemporary optics: shadows in real sunlight contain the
complementary colour of the light source. Warm yellow sunlight casts cool blue
shadows. He’s painting what was actually there — not what the studio convention said
should be there.
Look at the brushstrokes. The whole canvas is built out of thousands of short, separate,
visible marks. The paint sits on the surface in distinct dabs. Pissarro is not trying to
blend anything smoothly. He wants you to see, when you stand at normal viewing
distance, how the eye assembles separate marks into a continuous landscape. The
marks are the painting’s honesty about being made of paint.

What the critics said
When this canvas was shown in the 1874 exhibition, the critic Louis Leroy wrote a
famous review in the magazine Le Charivari. He invented a sarcastic character, a stuffy
academic painter, who walks through the exhibition mocking everything he sees. In
front of Pissarro’s Hoarfrost the character cries: “Those are not shadows, those are
tongue-scrapings on a dirty palette. It has neither head nor tail, neither top nor bottom,
neither front nor back.” The same review is the one that coined the word
impressionniste — intended as an insult, taken up by the painters as a badge.
Pissarro lived another twenty-nine years after this painting. He went on painting,
exhibiting, mentoring younger painters, and watching the movement he had helped
build slowly become the most admired school in Europe. He died in 1903 at the age of
seventy-three. Cézanne, who had been his student and friend for forty years, signed
himself for the rest of his life as “Paul Cézanne, pupil of Pissarro.”
