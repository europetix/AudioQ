Renoir’s Bal du moulin de la Galette
Impressionist Gallery · Sunday afternoon in Montmartre, 1876
6 min

◆ Stop & Look

Find the large painting that dominates one of the main walls of the Impressionist Gallery — a
crowded outdoor dance hall scene under trees, with dozens of figures in summer Parisian dress
talking, drinking, dancing, with bright circular patches of dappled sunlight falling across them.
This is Auguste Renoir’s Bal du moulin de la Galette. Stand back so you can see the whole canvas
at once.

Look at the whole painting first. Don’t focus on any single figure yet. Take in the warm
pulse of the crowd, the trees, the dappled light, the sense of motion. This is a painting
about a feeling first and a subject second.
This is Le Bal du moulin de la Galette — The Dance at the Moulin de la Galette — painted
by Pierre-Auguste Renoir in 1876, shown at the third Impressionist exhibition in April
1877. It is one of the great paintings of the nineteenth century. It is also one of the few
Impressionist paintings that almost everyone, including conservative critics, agreed

Where the painting is set
The Moulin de la Galette was a working-class dance hall on the hill of Montmartre, then
the bohemian village on the edge of Paris. The site got its name from two old windmills
(moulins) that had ground wheat there since the 17th century. By the 1860s, the wheat
trade had collapsed and the windmills had been converted into a small entertainment
business — the owner sold a thin pancake (a galette) and operated an open-air dance
floor under the trees. Every Sunday afternoon, young working-class Parisians —
seamstresses, clerks, students, shop workers, apprentice painters — walked up the hill
to drink, dance, flirt, and watch the sun set over the rooftops of Paris.
Renoir was twenty-five years old, broke, sharing a studio in Montmartre, and going to
the Moulin de la Galette every Sunday. He decided to make a painting of it. The
technical problem was enormous. The canvas he wanted to paint was huge — over a
metre and a half wide — and he wanted to paint it on location, in the open air, capturing
the actual moving light. He couldn’t carry a canvas that size up and down the hill every
day. So he rented a small cottage near the dance hall, set up an easel in its garden, and
walked the canvas back and forth on his back, with his friends helping him. He worked
on it through the summer of 1876.

Who’s in the painting
Almost everyone in the painting is a friend of Renoir’s. The seated group in the
foreground are his fellow painters and their girlfriends — the journalist Georges Rivière
sits at the table with the painters Pierre Lestringuez and Norbert Goeneutte. The girls
are Margot, Estelle, and Jeanne. Margot — the woman sitting at the table in the
foreground in the striped blue-and-pink dress — was one of Renoir’s favorite models in
this period. She died young, of typhoid fever, three years after this painting was made.
Renoir paid for her funeral.
The dancing couple to the left in the middle ground — the young woman in the pink
dress being held by the dark-suited man — are also friends. The young woman is Margot
again. The man is the Spanish painter Pedro Vidal de Solares y Cárdenas, a Cuban-born
art student in Paris. The whole scene is, in a sense, a group portrait of Renoir’s
Montmartre circle disguised as a public dance.

The dappled light
Now look at the bright circular patches of light that fall across the dancers’ clothes, the
tabletops, the gravel underfoot. Look at the back of the young man in the foreground —

his jacket is covered in pale yellow and rose spots. Look at the white dress of the girl
dancing in the middle ground — her skirt is moving through patches of warm yellow and
cool blue. These spots of colour are the painting’s technical centre. They are what
dappled sunlight — sunlight filtering down through the moving leaves of the trees above
the dance floor — actually looks like on cloth and skin. Renoir was the first painter who
captured this effect at scale.
Conservative critics in 1877 were appalled. One critic, Albert Wolff of Le Figaro, wrote
that Renoir had painted his figures as if they were “strewn with patches of mould.” Wolff
thought the dapples looked like a skin disease. He could not accept that the optical
phenomenon Renoir had captured — warm sunlight breaking on cool fabric — was a real
visual experience and not a painting error. Time settled the argument. The dapples are
now what people come to see.

What it captures
Stand back one more time and look at the painting as a whole. Notice how the
brushwork itself moves — swirling, looping, broken — to match the rhythm of the dance.
Notice how the warm yellow of the gas lamps in the upper right balances against the
cool blue of the evening shadows on the left. Notice that the painting has no single focal
point. Your eye is allowed to wander through the crowd the way you would actually
wander through it if you were there. The composition is built to make you a participant,
not a viewer.
Renoir kept the painting in his studio for two years after he made it, then sold it to his
friend and patron Gustave Caillebotte. Caillebotte left his entire art collection to the
French state when he died in 1894 — we’re going to meet Caillebotte himself a few
tracks from now, in front of one of his own paintings. The Bal du moulin de la Galette
entered the national collections in 1894, hung at the Musée du Luxembourg, moved to
the Louvre, and came here in 1986. It has not moved since.
