The two people in this café knew each other well. Degas painted them as if they'd never met.

She is Ellen Andrée, an actress who also modelled for painters. He is Marcellin Desboutin, a painter and engraver. And the café is real, La Nouvelle Athènes on the place Pigalle, a meeting place for the modern artists of Paris. It stood in the same district Monet had moved to before he painted Saint-Lazare. So these are friends from Degas's own circle, cast in a scene of total loneliness.

Look at her first, sitting on the banquette behind a marble table with a glass of absinthe in front of her. Then look at the two faces. They sit side by side, and yet the museum describes each of them as "immersed in silent isolation". Their gazes are empty and sad, and nobody is talking.

Now step back and look at the frame. The whole scene is pushed off to one side. There are empty stretches of table, and the man's pipe and hand are cut off at the edge. That kind of off-centre framing came from Japanese prints. Here it does something unsettling, and the museum's phrase for it is "a certain alcoholic imbalance". The picture itself seems to lean.

It may also be a warning. The museum reads it as a possible denunciation of absinthe, a drink that was later prohibited.

Andrée and Desboutin went back to their lives. The painting kept them at that table.
