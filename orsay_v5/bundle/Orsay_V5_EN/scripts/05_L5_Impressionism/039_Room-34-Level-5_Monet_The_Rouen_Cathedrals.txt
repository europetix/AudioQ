Claude Monet once had a bad night in Rouen. He wrote about it afterwards. "I had nightmares all night: the cathedral was falling on me, it seemed blue or pink or yellow."

You can see why. For two years running, he stood in front of the same stone front of Rouen Cathedral and painted it, again and again, in every light he could catch. He worked on the spot in eighteen ninety-two and ninety-three, and finished the canvases at home in Giverny. In the end there were about thirty of them. In eighteen ninety-five, his dealer Durand-Ruel showed twenty of them together.

Five of them are here, and the trick is to stop seeing one cathedral and start seeing five different days.

The titles help, because each one is named by its colour, like a piece of music. Start with the two morning canvases. One is a harmony in blue, the doorway in morning sun. The other, with the tower of Saint-Romain beside it, is a harmony in white. Then full sun, a harmony in blue and gold. Then a grey day, a harmony in grey. And one seen straight on, from the front, a harmony in brown.

Now let your eye move from one to the next, and forget the stone, because the stone is the same in all five. What changes is the hour and the weather, and with them every colour on its surface. Monet wasn't really painting a building. He was painting the light that happened to be landing on it.

Four of these came to the nation in the bequest of Isaac de Camondo, the collector you met with Sisley's flood. The brown one was bought from Monet himself, while he was still alive.

A phrase of his sums it up. Tout change, quoique pierre. Everything changes, even stone.
