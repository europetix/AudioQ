For a man who painted fruit, Cézanne was remarkably loyal to his props.

In 1899, in his studio in Paris, he set up a still life. Some earthenware dishes, a jug patterned with flowers, a length of drapery. Then he painted it, and then, with the same props, he painted it again. This is one of six still lifes made from those same few objects.

Why keep the props? He didn't leave us a neat answer, so here's a guess you can test with your own eyes. When the objects stay the same, the only things left to change are the arrangement and the painting itself. The subject quietly shifts from the fruit to the way the fruit is put together.

Look at the drapery. The museum points out that it recalls the great Flemish still lifes of the seventeenth century. So Cézanne is borrowing an old, grand way of setting a table, and making it serve a very modern kind of looking.

The museum doesn't hedge on this one. It calls Pommes et oranges "the most important still life produced by the artist in the late 1890s." Like the card players, it came here in the bequest of Isaac de Camondo, in 1911.

Six paintings. One jug. And no hurry at all.
