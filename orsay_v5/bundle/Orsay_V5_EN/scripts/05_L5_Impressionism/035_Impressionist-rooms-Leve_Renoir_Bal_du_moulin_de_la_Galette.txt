This is not a crowd of strangers. Several of the people dancing and talking here were Renoir's own circle, and some of them have names.

Renoir was thirty-five in the spring of 1876, and he painted this mainly on the spot, at the dance itself. Friends helped him carry the big canvas there from his studio on the rue Cortot. He probably finished it back in the studio.

So let's meet a few of them. Find the seated girl in the striped dress. That's Estelle. Her sister Jeanne, a dressmaker of sixteen, posed too. And out on the floor, dancing with the tall painter Solares, is Henriette-Anna Leboeuf, a model very close to Renoir, known as Margot. Rivière, who is in the painting himself, remembered her shaking poor Solares around in a polka, and teaching him Paris slang as they went.

It looks like pure happiness. And it carries a sad fact. Margot died in 1879, only in her early twenties. The young woman dancing at this party had three more years to live.

The painting belonged to Caillebotte, the painter and collector who also bought Monet's station. He left it to the nation, and it was shown with the group the year after it was painted. Rivière wrote that it was "a page of history, a precious monument of Parisian life."

This year, that page turns a hundred and fifty. Everyone on it is gone. The dance is still going.

Nearby in the Impressionist rooms, look for Sisley's view of a flood, a house with pink and yellow walls standing at an angle above the water. Its track picks up the story from that spring.
