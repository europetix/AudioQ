Some of the most famous card players in the history of art may have worked for the painter's father.

The museum chooses its word with care. The men are allegedly peasants Cézanne saw at the Jas de Bouffan, his father's property just outside Aix-en-Provence. Cézanne had shown with the Impressionists in Paris. But after their exhibition of 1877, he worked more and more alone in the south, among the people and places he knew. These players come from that world.

One of them is more than a type. The man smoking the pipe has been identified as père Alexandre, old Alexandre, the gardener at the Jas de Bouffan. So somewhere in this picture, the gardener is taking a break.

In the summer of 2025, this little canvas went home for a while. It was lent to the Musée Granet in Aix for an exhibition about the Jas de Bouffan, close to where its sitters came from.

Cézanne painted it in the mid-eighteen nineties, and he came back to the subject more than once. There are five versions of the card players, and this is the smallest. It's less than sixty centimetres wide. You could carry it under one arm.

And yet it doesn't feel small. Stand back a little and look. There's no story here, no winner, no loser, no joke being shared. Just men at a table and a game, painted with the same weight and attention Cézanne gave to apples or a mountain. The gardener gets the seriousness of a portrait.

Close by in the Cézanne rooms, look for his still life of apples and oranges, with draped cloth, earthenware dishes and a flower-patterned jug. Speaking of apples, play its track there.
