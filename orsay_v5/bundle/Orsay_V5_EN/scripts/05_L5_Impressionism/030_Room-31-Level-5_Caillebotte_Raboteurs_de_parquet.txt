In eighteen seventy-five, the jury of the official Salon looked at this painting and was shocked. Not by a nude or a battle, but by men scraping a floor.

The painter was Gustave Caillebotte, the same man who bought Monet's Gare Saint-Lazare. He was a collector, and a painter too, and he sent this canvas to the Salon. In the museum's own words, the jury was "shocked by its crude realism", and they turned it down.

It's hard to feel the shock now. But this is a big canvas, almost a metre and a half wide, and the whole of it is given to working men, at work. The museum calls it one of the first representations of the urban proletariat. That was the crude part, not the paint but the subject.

So look at it the way the jury didn't. Take in the floor itself, the subject in the title, and the men at work on it. Give them the attention a Salon picture would give a king.

The next year Caillebotte showed it with the Impressionists instead, at their exhibition of eighteen seventy-six, where it divided the critics. Even Zola, who had fought for Manet, found it too exact.

And it isn't part of Caillebotte's famous bequest. After his death, his family gave it separately, through his friend Renoir.

Still in this room, find Degas's La Classe de danse, The Dance Class: a ballet master among his young pupils, and play its track there.
