Courbet painted it in eighteen sixty-six. It is small, about forty-six by fifty-five centimetres. Its first owner, who probably commissioned it, was a Turkish-Egyptian diplomat, Khalil-Bey. He built up a short-lived collection celebrating the female body, and he was ruined by gambling debts.

For most of its history, very few people saw this painting. It stayed in private hands. Among its later owners was the psychoanalyst Jacques Lacan. The museum describes it as the paradox of a famous work that is seldom actually seen.

It came to the nation in nineteen ninety-five, as payment of inheritance tax in kind. That is how it entered this museum, and how a picture made for one private collection came to hang where anyone can see it.

For a long time, the woman who posed had no name. Then, in twenty eighteen, the scholar Claude Schopp found a letter from the writer Alexandre Dumas the younger to George Sand. On the strength of that letter, the model was identified as Constance Quéniaux, a dancer at the Paris Opéra. Sylvie Aubenas, of the French national library, said she was ninety-nine per cent certain.

So a painting that was so seldom seen now hangs in a public room, and the woman in it has a name.
