One of the men in this painting hated the way he looked in it. We'll come to him.

Courbet gave the picture a famously long title. The Painter's Studio, a real allegory determining a phase of seven years of my artistic life. And he summed it up himself, in the museum's translation. "It's the whole world coming to me to be painted."

He even explained the two halves. On the right, he said, are "all the shareholders, by that I mean friends, fellow workers, art lovers." Look for the collector Alfred Bruyas, the philosopher Proudhon, the critic Champfleury on a stool, and the poet Baudelaire, absorbed in a book.

On the left is the other half. In his words, "the other world of everyday life, the masses, wretchedness, poverty, wealth, the exploited and the exploiters, people who make a living from death." There's a priest, a merchant, a beggar girl, an unemployed worker. And a hunter who, as the museum puts it, somewhat resembles Napoleon the Third. Draw your own conclusions.

This was Courbet's world in eighteen fifty-five, and an official jury turned it down. So he showed it himself, with the Burial, in his own Pavilion of Realism.

Now, the man who hated his portrait was Champfleury, the critic on the stool. "I was appalled at this image of myself," he wrote, "which made me look like the Jesuit General." And then, simply: "This damned portrait is monstrous."

The painting has an unusual bond with the public. In nineteen twenty, it was bought for the nation by a public subscription. Then, about ten years before the Burial was restored, it was restored in public, behind glass, and some nine hundred donors raised eighty-six thousand euros to help pay for it. The public bought it once, and then helped to save it.
