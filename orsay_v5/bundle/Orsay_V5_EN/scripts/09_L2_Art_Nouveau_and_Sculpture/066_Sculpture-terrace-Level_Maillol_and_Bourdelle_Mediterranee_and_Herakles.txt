When this seated woman first appeared in public, she didn't have a grand title. At the Salon d'Automne of 1905, she was simply called Woman, and she caused a stir.

Aristide Maillol made her for his German patron, Count Harry Kessler. The name she carries now, Méditerranée, The Mediterranean, came later. The museum owns two versions. The bronze is a recent cast. This marble is a copy made in Maillol's own lifetime, in the nineteen twenties.

Compare her with the Balzac you've just left. Rodin gives you a storm. Maillol turned away from that expressive style, towards formal beauty and simplicity. No story, no drama, no name, just a seated woman and a block of calm.

Now turn to the opposite answer. Antoine Bourdelle's Héraklès, Hercules the Archer, dates from 1909. Its full French title tells you the myth. The hero is shooting the man-eating birds of Lake Stymphalus. It's gilded bronze, nearly two and a half metres high and just as wide, cast by Alexis Rudier. The national museums bought it fifteen years later.

Look at the bow, drawn tight, and then at the open air beyond it, where the arrow is about to fly. All that weight of bronze is gathered into one moment of tension.

Here are two ways to make a body speak in sculpture. One sits perfectly still. The other is a split second from letting go.
