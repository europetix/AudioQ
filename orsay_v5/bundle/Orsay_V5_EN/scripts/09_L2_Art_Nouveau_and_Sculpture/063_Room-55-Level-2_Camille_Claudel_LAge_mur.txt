Her brother Paul said that what you are watching here is a woman having her soul torn out of her.

This bronze was made by Camille Claudel, after her break with Auguste Rodin. You might expect Rodin to have stood in its way. The museum says the opposite. After the break-up, he tried to help her, through an intermediary, and obtained a State commission for this group in 1895.

But the commission went nowhere. The bronze was never ordered, and the plaster was never delivered. In the end, the money came from a private patron, a Captain Tissier. He commissioned the first bronze in 1902. The museum keeps Claudel's letters to him, and in one she reports that the founder, Rudier, was asking six thousand francs to cast it. The group in front of you is that first bronze, cast in her lifetime.

Now look at the three figures. The museum reads the group as Rodin's hesitation between two women. One is his former mistress, the woman who was to prevail. She draws the man away. The other is Claudel herself, bent forward, trying to hold him. Claudel called that kneeling figure l'Implorante, the one who begs.

Follow the man's arm. In the museum's words, the man at the end of his maturity is vertiginously drawn by age, while he extends a useless hand towards youth. And notice the flying draperies. They make the whole group move faster than bronze has any right to.

Paul Claudel put it like this. "My sister Camille, beseeching, humiliated on her knees, this superb, this proud woman, and do you know what is being torn from her, at this very moment, before your eyes, it is her soul."

In 1913, aged forty-eight, Camille Claudel was interned. She never came out. She died thirty years later. But here, in bronze, the kneeling woman is still reaching out.

Now go out onto the sculpture terrace, overlooking the nave, and find François Pompon's great stone polar bear. Play its track there.
