This calm figure took thirty-six years to arrive, and Ingres didn't finish her on his own.

He began the painting in Florence, around eighteen twenty. Decades passed. He only completed it in Paris in eighteen fifty-six, when he was seventy-six years old. And to get it done, he brought in two of his pupils, Paul Balze and Alexandre Desgoffe. They painted the accessories and the background. That was common practice for large formats.

Look at how he sets her. The canvas is tall and narrow, and she stands in it as if in an alcove. Between that frame and her pose, she takes on the stillness of a marble statue. Ingres's debt to sculpture is plain to see. She isn't caught in a moment. She is placed, like something carved.

Her first owner wanted the opposite effect. A year after it was finished, Count Duchâtel bought the painting from Ingres for twenty-five thousand francs. At his home, he surrounded it with large plants and water flowers, so that the nymph of the spring would look even more like a real person. A painted statue, given a little garden to make her breathe.

Countess Duchâtel later left the painting to the Louvre, and it came to this museum when it opened.

So stand back and see which way it works for you. A woman, or a statue. Ingres, I suspect, wanted you to feel both at once.
