Jean-Baptiste Carpeaux arrived in Rome almost two years late, which was not the best way to start.

As a winner of the Prix de Rome, he had a place at the Villa Medici. He got there in eighteen fifty-six, and from the very beginning, things were awkward with the director, the painter Victor Schnetz. Then Carpeaux chose his subject, and worked on it for four years, well beyond the time he was allowed to stay.

The subject came from Dante's Inferno. In thirteenth-century Pisa, Ugolino della Gherardesca betrayed his party. The archbishop, Ubaldini, had him shut up in a tower to starve to death. Carpeaux shows him with his children.

Look at the father. Everything in this group is about his pain and his anguish. The museum points to what Carpeaux had been studying: the ancient Laocoön, Michelangelo, and Géricault's Raft of the Medusa. All three stand behind this one father's face.

The academy in Paris judged the plaster, and the verdict fell short of his hopes. Its remarks even appeared in the press. Then the State offered him a commission for a marble, at a fee he thought far too low. Carpeaux negotiated hard, and in the end the result was this bronze, shown at the Salon of eighteen sixty-three.

And then came a nice twist: for some forty years, it stood out in the Tuileries Garden, as a partner to a Laocoön. The sculpture he had learned from became his neighbour.

Now cross to the Seine side and find room 4, for Daumier's small painted clay heads of public men, the Celebrities of the Juste Milieu. Play its track there.
