This face spent decades in a doctor's house.

It came from the collection of Dr Paul Gachet, the doctor who looked after Van Gogh at Auvers-sur-Oise. It stayed in the doctor's family there until 1949, when Gachet's children, Paul and Marguerite, gave it to the nation.

He painted it in 1889, at the hospital in Saint-Rémy-de-Provence. That September, he wrote a line about the problem every self-portrait faces. "It's difficult to know oneself — but it isn't easy to paint oneself either."

So look at how he solved it. He gives himself a plain head-and-shoulders view, in a suit. The features are hard and thin. The eyes are rimmed with green, and they look anxious. The whole picture is tuned to two colours, an absinthe green and a pale turquoise, and against them, the fiery orange of his beard and hair.

Now watch what moves and what doesn't. The man himself is utterly still. But his hair and beard ripple, and the background takes those ripples and spreads them, curling across the whole canvas. The stillness sits in the middle of all that movement.

About two weeks later, writing about a self-portrait from those weeks, he said his "physiognomy has grown much calmer, although the gaze may be vaguer than before."

Antonin Artaud, writing about Van Gogh, described the face in his self-portrait as "inspecting and watching us, scrutinising us with a glowering eye." Stand in front of it for a moment, and let it scrutinise you back.

Somewhere in the Van Gogh rooms, find his bedroom in Arles: a small room with a yellow bed and chairs and a red floor. Play its track there.
