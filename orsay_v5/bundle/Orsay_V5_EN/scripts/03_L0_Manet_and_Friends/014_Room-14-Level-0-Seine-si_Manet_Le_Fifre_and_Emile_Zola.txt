These two paintings tell one story. It begins with a rejection and ends with a thank-you note, painted in oils.

In eighteen sixty-five Manet travelled to Spain and saw the Prado for the first time. A portrait by Velázquez, of a man called Pablo de Valladolid, bowled him over. He wrote to Fantin-Latour that it was "the most astonishing piece of painting ever done."

Back in Paris, he painted Le Fifre, the Fifer. Look at him. An ordinary boy from a military band, with no name and no setting, just a plain ground behind him. Flat areas of colour, thick streaks of white. And yet, as the museum says, he carries the dignity of a Spanish grandee.

The Salon jury of eighteen sixty-six turned it down. But it was one of the paintings that fired the enthusiasm of Émile Zola, who went on to write a pamphlet defending Manet.

The portrait of Zola is Manet's way of saying thank you. Zola sits at his work table with a book, an inkwell and a quill. On the wall behind him hangs a reproduction of Olympia, the painting Zola thought Manet's best. Behind it, a print after Velázquez, and a Japanese print of a wrestler. A Japanese screen stands on the left.

Now find the blue pamphlet on the table. It's Zola's defence of Manet. And it carries Manet's name. That is the signature of the whole painting.

Fantin-Latour painted these friends too, with Manet among them. Find his big group portrait, Un atelier aux Batignolles, in the Seine gallery, on the river side of this floor, and play its track there.
