In eighteen ninety, Claude Monet passed the hat. Nearly a hundred people chipped in, among them artists, collectors, dealers, writers and even politicians. They weren't buying a painting for themselves. They were buying this one, to give it to France.

Why did a painting need rescuing by its friends? Because twenty-five years earlier, at the Salon of eighteen sixty-five, it had caused a scandal.

On paper, the subject was nothing new. A reclining nude, a servant bringing flowers, the old tradition of the odalisque. Manet knew that tradition from the inside. He had copied Titian's Venus of Urbino, and his Olympia quotes it, along with Goya's naked Maja. But he moved the goddess into the Paris of his own day. The museum puts it in five words. "Venus has become a prostitute."

Now look at her face. The model was Victorine Meurent, and she looks straight back at you. The museum calls that look calculating. She isn't waiting to be admired. She's sizing you up.

In the centre stands a black woman holding a bouquet. For a long time she was treated as almost invisible, and the museum's own commentary says so. In an exhibition here in 2019, called Le Modèle noir, she got her name back. She was Laure.

And on the right, where Titian painted a little dog, Manet put a black cat. The museum calls it probably the most famous cat in the history of painting. It's hard to argue.

So the friends won, and Olympia went into the national collections at the Musée du Luxembourg, and in time it came here. The scandal of the Salon now belongs to everyone, and the woman on the bed is still looking straight back at you.
