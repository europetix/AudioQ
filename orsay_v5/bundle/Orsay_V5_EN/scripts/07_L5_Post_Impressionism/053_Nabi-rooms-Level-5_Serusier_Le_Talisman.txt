Some of the most consequential paintings in this museum are huge. This one is not. It's a little panel of wood, twenty-seven centimetres by twenty-one, about the size of a sheet of paper.

It was painted in October 1888 by Paul Sérusier, in the Bois d'Amour at Pont-Aven. Yes, the same wood where Émile Bernard laid his sister down life-size a few rooms back. Sérusier painted it under Gauguin's direction. Gauguin was forty, and he had been urging the painters around him to work in masses and solid areas of colour.

What exactly Gauguin said to Sérusier that day, we know only from the later account of the painter Maurice Denis. So rather than put words in his mouth, look at the result.

Its other title, L'Aven au Bois d'Amour, names a real place. Look at how Sérusier has built that place, with Gauguin's advice about masses of colour in mind. Then ask yourself how much of the view is left, and how much is simply colour.

This little panel became the founding work of the group called the Nabis. The museum says it made the Bois d'Amour famous. Not bad for something you could slip into a coat pocket.

You'll meet the Nabis again on level two, in Maurice Denis's own work among others.

Before you go down, walk on to the exit of the gallery, to the Café Campana behind the great clock. Play its track there.
