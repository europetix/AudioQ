This is a wedding portrait, though nobody ever called it that. Paul Signac painted Femme à l'ombrelle, Woman with a Parasol, in 1893. A few months earlier, in November 1892, he had married the woman in it.

Her name was Berthe Roblès, a distant cousin of the painter Camille Pissarro. Signac met her in the early eighteen-eighties at the Chat Noir cabaret, and she lived with him from then on. So the wedding came after about ten years together. The portrait came right after the wedding.

That alone makes it unusual. Finished portraits are rare in Signac's work. This is the last of a small series of family and friends, which includes the writer Félix Fénéon, now in New York at the Museum of Modern Art.

And the subject is a nod to the generation before. A woman with a parasol was an Impressionist favourite. Monet painted two such studies of a figure out of doors, and both belong to this museum. Signac takes the same idea and does it, as the museum puts it, in a purely Neo-Impressionist manner.

Signac called the method divisionism. The colours go down as separate touches, meant to mix in your eye and stay luminous. Step close and Berthe breaks into colour. Step back and she comes together again.

Walk on into the Pont-Aven rooms. Look for a life-size young woman lying in a wood, Émile Bernard's Madeleine au Bois d'Amour, and play its track there.
