Paul Signac met the woman in this picture at a cabaret, the Chat Noir, in the early eighteen-eighties. From then on, she lived with him.

Her name was Berthe Roblès, a distant cousin of the painter Camille Pissarro. They married in November 1892, after about ten years together. The next year he painted her here, as Femme à l'ombrelle, Woman with a Parasol.

That alone makes it unusual. Finished portraits are rare in Signac's work. This is the last of a small series of family and friends, which includes the writer Félix Fénéon, now in New York at the Museum of Modern Art.

And the subject is a nod to the generation before. A woman with a parasol was an Impressionist favourite. Monet painted two such studies of a figure out of doors, and both belong to this museum. Signac takes the same idea and does it, as the museum puts it, in a purely Neo-Impressionist manner.

Signac called the method divisionism. The colours go down as separate touches, meant to mix in your eye and stay luminous. Step close and Berthe breaks into colour. Step back and she comes together again. Try it a couple of times. The picture changes with every step you take.

Walk on to room 43, the Pont-Aven room. Look for a life-size young woman lying full length in a wood, Émile Bernard's Madeleine au Bois d'Amour. Her track is waiting for you there.
