When Paris first saw this painting, it laughed at the dog.

Gauguin showed Arearea in Paris in November 1893, with the pictures from his first stay in Tahiti. And what people noticed was a red dog, which drew a good deal of sarcasm.

One practical word. Over the winter of 2026 to 2027 this painting is on loan abroad. If its place is empty, look in these rooms for Femmes de Tahiti, Tahitian Women, if it is on display. I'll come to it at the end.

The jokes didn't change his mind, for he thought Arearea one of his best works. In 1895, before he left Europe for good, he bought it back himself.

So look at what he couldn't let go of. Two women seated at the centre, and a tree cutting across the whole picture. The red dog in the foreground, unbothered. And behind them, women worshipping a great statue.

Here's the turn: that statue is invented. Gauguin took a small Maori motif, enlarged it to the size of a great Buddha, and imagined a sacred rite around it. The museum says so plainly.

Keep that in mind with everything Gauguin told about Tahiti. His book Noa Noa reads like a memoir, but it's a romanticised account, reworked with Charles Morice. Even the ages he gave for the young Tahitian girls in his life come from his own telling. They should be heard that way, and not as fact.

If you're standing at Femmes de Tahiti instead, it comes from the start of that first stay. Tahitian women at simple daily tasks, heavy and still, each in her own space. Their faces are like masks, touched with melancholy. In a later version he swapped the strict mission dress of the woman on the right for a flowered pareo.

He had come, he said, "to live there in ecstasy, calm and art". Arearea is that wish, painted. Perhaps that's why he wanted it back.
