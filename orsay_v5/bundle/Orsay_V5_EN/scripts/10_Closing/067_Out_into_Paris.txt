In 1900, when this building was brand new, the painter Édouard Detaille wrote a line about it. "The station is superb and looks like a Palais des beaux-arts…" He meant a palace of fine arts. It took most of the century for him to be proved right.

Look down from the terrace. Down there, where the sculptures stand along the central aisle, the trains once pulled in. Victor Laloux built this as a railway station for the Universal Exposition, with an iron frame hidden behind cut stone. It ran as a terminus for almost forty years, then slowly lost its purpose. In December 1986 it opened its doors again, as a museum. This is its fortieth year.

And now you know its three floors. You began down there, on the ground floor, with the academic painters, Millet and Courbet, and Manet's Olympia. You climbed to the top, under the roof, to the Impressionists, Van Gogh and Gauguin. And you came down here, to the Nabis, Art Nouveau, Claudel and Rodin's Balzac.

Before you go, look all the way to the far end of the nave. Between the two towers stands Rodin's Gates of Hell, on the axis of the aisle, facing the whole length of the old station. You probably passed close to it hours ago. From up here, you can take it in with everything else at once.

The museum calls this great hall the main artery of the visit. It's a good phrase. Almost everything you've seen today opened off it, on one level or another.

Thank you for spending these hours with me. I hope a few of these faces come home with you: a black cat, a little dancer, a man in a sack of plaster, a girl holding a poet's head.

When you're ready, follow the exit signs out into Paris. Goodbye, and enjoy the city.
