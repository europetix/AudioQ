Most buildings keep their secrets behind a wall. This one has had its wall taken off. What you're looking at is the Paris Opéra, sliced lengthwise from the façade all the way to the back of the stage.

The model was made in Rome in nineteen eighty-five, by the Gianese workshop, under the artistic direction of Richard Peduzzi. It follows Garnier's own drawing, published in his book Le Nouvel Opéra de Paris in eighteen eighty. The scale is one to twenty-five, and still the model is nearly six metres long.

Read it in four parts. First, take in the whole cross-section, end to end. Then find the façade, the foyer and the grand staircase. Next comes the auditorium. And then the backstage, the part the audience never sees.

In front of the model there's usually a long panel with texts in French and English, braille and tactile pictures. Since June twenty twenty-three, it has been the museum's first station designed to be accessible to every visitor.

Why put an opera house in an art museum? Because it ties together works you meet across this museum. The model explains how Garnier's building works, the decoration you've just seen in Carpeaux's La Danse, and, through Degas, the life of the corps de ballet. You'll meet Degas's dancers upstairs.
