One night at the end of August, eighteen sixty-nine, someone threw a bottle of ink at this sculpture. It had only just been unveiled on the front of Charles Garnier's new Opéra, and plenty of Parisians were furious.

Garnier had commissioned four groups for his façade, from four sculptors who had all won the Prix de Rome. Carpeaux took Dance. He spent three years on sketches and models. What he delivered was this, a swirling ring of six women around the Genius of Dance, with a little putto at his feet.

And the public was outraged. Not by the idea of dance, but by these bodies. They were too real, and too naked, for the front of an opera house.

A replacement group was ordered from another sculptor, named Gumery. Then history stepped in. War with Prussia came, the Empire fell, and Gumery himself died early the next year.

Carpeaux didn't outlive the quarrel by much. He died nine months after the Opéra finally opened, at forty-eight. The war, the fall of the Empire and his death, between them, saved his dancers from removal.

Now look at the ring. Follow it all the way round with your eyes, figure by figure. The museum calls it swirling, and it is. This is stone, about eighteen tonnes of it, and it still seems to be moving.

And here's the part most people walking past the Opéra never learn. Since nineteen sixty-four, the group on the façade has been a copy. The original went to the Louvre, and when this museum opened, it came here. So the dancers who were once splashed with ink are the ones in front of you.

Carpeaux's dancers were made for the Opéra, and the Opéra itself is waiting at the end of the nave. Find the great model of its quarter, seen from above, in the Opéra space there, and play its track.
