[quietly] Few painters have had worse timing than Georges Seurat. In March 1891 he showed this canvas at the Salon des Indépendants in Paris. A few days after the show opened, he died of diphtheria, at thirty-one.

So Le Cirque, Circus, was never finished, and the museum calls it his artistic testament. It was the third of his big pictures of Paris entertainment after dark, after two others called Parade and Chahut.

Seurat didn't paint by instinct alone. He studied the colour theories of Chevreul and Rood, and the ideas of Charles Henry on how lines and colours act on our feelings. His own name for the method was chromo-luminarism. And in this picture, lines that rise are meant to feel like joy.

[curious] So follow them up. In front, a clown with his back to you, standing where Chahut had its musician. Beyond him, a horsewoman and acrobats in the ring. A critic of the day saw the whole thing working towards gaiety, with rising lines and orange in command.

Now look at the edges. Seurat painted a dark border straight onto the canvas, and the flat frame around it is the same blue. [pause] The frame is part of the painting.

[warmly] It never got its finishing touches. But it still does what Seurat asked of it. Everything in it climbs.

For almost a quarter of a century, this canvas belonged to the painter Paul Signac. His own work is just ahead. In room 40, look for his Femme à l'ombrelle, a woman with a parasol built from small separate touches of colour, and press play when you're there.
