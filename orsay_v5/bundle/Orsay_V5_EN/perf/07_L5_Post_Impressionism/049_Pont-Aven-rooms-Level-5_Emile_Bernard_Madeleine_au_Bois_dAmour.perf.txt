[curious] The painter was twenty. His sister was seventeen. And the painter everyone at Pont-Aven followed had fallen in love with her.

The painter was Émile Bernard. In 1888 he painted his sister Madeleine life-size, lying in the Bois d'Amour, a wood on the edge of Pont-Aven. The two of them were very close to Paul Gauguin, then forty, who led the little group of painters working there. The museum calls them Impressionist and Synthetist. Gauguin was pushing them to paint in masses and solid areas of colour.

And Gauguin, the museum tells us, was in love with Madeleine.

[pause] So this isn't quite a girl resting in the woods. The museum calls it a portrait with allegorical overtones. What the allegory says, nobody spells out for us. A young woman lying in a place called the Wood of Love. Painted by her brother, in the circle of the man who loved her.

[quietly] Take in the scale first. She is as big as you are, stretched out across the canvas. Then look at the wood around her. Ask how much of it is an ordinary forest scene, and how much is the kind of painting Gauguin was preaching that year.

Keep the name of that wood in mind. Further on, you'll meet a tiny painting made there the same year, and the museum says it made the Bois d'Amour famous.

Now find Gauguin himself. In the Gauguin rooms, look for La Belle Angèle, a woman's portrait set inside a circle, and play its track there.
