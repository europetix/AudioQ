[warmly] While Monet was wrestling with that cathedral in Rouen, he was also making a pond. In 1893 he created a water garden at his house in Giverny.

For the last part of his life, from the nineteen tens until his death in 1926, that pond was his sole source of inspiration. He had spent years chasing light across a stone front. Now the light came to him, on the water in his own garden.

[pause] This canvas is one of those late pictures. It's big, two metres high and almost exactly as wide, and that square shape matters.

[quietly] Try something. Let your eye look for a place to rest, a bank or a horizon or a patch of sky, and you won't find one. No point in the picture pulls your attention more than any other. There's no edge of the pond, no up or down to hold on to. Just the lilies, the water, and the colour of the title.

The museum describes the effect as a sense of infinity, of limitlessness, and standing this close it's easy to see why. The canvas stops at the frame, but the pond doesn't seem to.

[long pause] The cathedrals were about one thing changing. Here, there's barely a thing at all.

Go on into the Cézanne rooms and look for a small painting of card players, one of them smoking a pipe, and play its track there.
