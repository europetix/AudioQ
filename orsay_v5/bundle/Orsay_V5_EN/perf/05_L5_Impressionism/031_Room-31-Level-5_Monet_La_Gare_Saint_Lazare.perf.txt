[amused] Here's a small irony to enjoy. You're about to look at a railway station, and you're standing inside one.

When Monet painted this, the Gare d'Orsay didn't exist yet. Saint-Lazare did, and it was one of the busiest stations in Paris, as it still is. In eighteen seventy-seven Monet had just left Argenteuil and moved into the Nouvelle Athènes district, and the station ran right along the edge of it. So he asked for permission to set up and work inside.

[pause] He got in, and he came back again and again. He made a whole series of the station.

Now look at what he chose to paint, and what he didn't. You won't find a careful portrait of a locomotive here, or of the people on the platforms. Monet was after something else entirely: the colour, the light, and above all the steam. Let your eye move through the hall and follow the steam. That's the real subject, not the machine itself but the weather the machine makes.

[warmly] The first person to buy it was a painter, Gustave Caillebotte. On the tenth of March eighteen seventy-eight, he paid Monet six hundred and eighty-five francs for it. When Caillebotte died, he left his collection to the nation. The State hesitated for years, and only accepted it in eighteen ninety-six. That's how this canvas ended up in public hands.

[lightly] So a painting of one station, bought by one painter, now hangs in another station. Monet would have liked the timetable.

In this same room, find Caillebotte's own painting, Raboteurs de parquet, The Floor Scrapers, and play its track there.
