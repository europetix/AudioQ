[quietly] The title of this room is a question. À qui appartiennent ces œuvres? Who do these works belong to?

During the Second World War, around a hundred thousand works of art were taken from their owners in France, many of them Jewish families. After the war, about sixty thousand were recovered in Germany and Austria and sent back to France. About forty-five thousand were returned to their owners. Some thirteen thousand were sold by the State in the early nineteen fifties. About two thousand two hundred were entrusted to the national museums, to hold until their owners are found. Those are the MNR, for Musées Nationaux Récupération.

[pause] Orsay holds two hundred and twenty-five of them. In thirty years, fifteen have gone back to their rightful owners. Why so few? Mostly because the history of each work is incomplete. And some turn out not to have been stolen at all, so there is no one to return them to.

This room opened in 2026, and its selection changes. Look for the paintings hung so that you can see their backs. The labels and stamps there are part of the evidence.

If they're on show, two works carry heavy stories. A supper at a ball by Degas, a free copy after the German painter Menzel, once belonged to Fernand Ochsé. In July 1944 he and his wife, the sculptor Louise Ochsé, were deported to Auschwitz. And a small beach scene by Alfred Stevens was acquired for the museum Hitler planned in Linz.

[long pause] When you're ready, head back into the central sculpture aisle and find Carpeaux's La Danse, a great ring of dancers in stone. Play its track there.
