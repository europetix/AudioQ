[warmly] Jane Avril discovered she could dance in a hospital.

She was born Jeanne Beaudon in 1868, and her childhood was hard, with a mother who beat her. As a girl she suffered from chorea, a nervous illness that brings convulsive movements, and at fourteen she was interned at the Salpêtrière hospital in Paris.

Then came a masked ball there, at mid-Lent. [pause] A waltz began, and she was carried away by it. She danced, and the others made a circle around her and applauded. In the museum's words, that was the night her vocation was born.

She went on to dance at the Moulin Rouge, the Jardin de Paris and the Folies-Bergère. People who saw her called her "the incarnation of dance".

[curious] Now look at how Lautrec paints her. The picture is tall and narrow, on cardboard, and the paint is thin. He diluted his oils with turpentine, so they flow, and in broad strokes he set down only the essentials of her movement. Nothing more, so it feels less like a portrait than a glimpse.

Behind her stand two figures. One is a woman in a hat whom no one has identified. The other is an Englishman the museum calls the impresario Warner. He also posed for one of Lautrec's best-known prints, The Englishman at the Moulin Rouge.

[quietly] Jane Avril left the stage in 1911, at forty-two, when she married the poster artist Maurice Biais. She moved to a quiet life at Jouy-en-Josas, outside Paris.

Stay with Lautrec. Nearby, look for a small painting of a clown in a private room, fastening a big yellow ruffle to her costume. Play its track there.
