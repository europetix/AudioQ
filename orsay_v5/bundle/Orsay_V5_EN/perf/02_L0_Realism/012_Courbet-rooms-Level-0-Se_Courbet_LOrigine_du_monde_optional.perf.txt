This track is optional. It is about Courbet's L'Origine du monde, The Origin of the World, a small and very frank painting of the female body. If you'd rather not, go straight on to Manet's Olympia in room 14.

Courbet painted it in eighteen sixty-six. It is small, about forty-six by fifty-five centimetres. Its first owner, who probably commissioned it, was a Turkish-Egyptian diplomat, Khalil-Bey. He built up a short-lived collection celebrating the female body, and he was ruined by gambling debts.

For most of its history, very few people saw this painting. It stayed in private hands. [pause] Among its later owners was the psychoanalyst Jacques Lacan. The museum describes it as the paradox of a famous work that is seldom actually seen.

It came to the nation in nineteen ninety-five, as payment of inheritance tax in kind. That is how a picture made for one private collection came to belong to everyone.

[quietly] For a long time, the woman who posed had no name. Then, in twenty eighteen, the scholar Claude Schopp found a letter from the writer Alexandre Dumas the younger to George Sand. On the strength of that letter, the model was identified as Constance Quéniaux, a dancer at the Paris Opéra. Sylvie Aubenas, of the French national library, said she was ninety-nine per cent certain.

So a painting that was so seldom seen now belongs to a public museum, and the woman in it has a name.
