[curious] Standing in front of a church in a French village, Van Gogh found himself thinking of a tower in Holland.

He painted this in the first week of June 1890, soon after arriving in Auvers. And on the fifth of June, in the middle of the work, he described it in a letter. He wrote that it was "almost the same thing as the studies I did in Nuenen of the old tower and the cemetery." Only now, he went on, "the colour is probably more expressive, more sumptuous."

Nuenen was a Dutch village where he had painted years before. Now the same kind of subject came back to him, in new colour. And this is the only painting in which he showed the church at Auvers in full.

[warmly] After his death, it stayed with Dr Gachet, and then with the doctor's son. In 1952 it came into the national collection. It was not simply a gift. The son supported the purchase, and so did someone who has never been named: an anonymous donor from Canada. A stranger, helping a village church into the museum.

[pause] Now look at the windows. In the same letter, Van Gogh says the stained-glass windows "look like ultramarine blue patches," set against "a sky of a deep and simple blue of pure cobalt." So read them as he did. [quietly] Two blues, one inside the other.

Then go into room 37, the Gachet room, and find the portrait of the doctor himself: a melancholy man in cold colours, with a sprig of foxglove. Play its track there.
