[quietly] This night sky was not painted from memory. Van Gogh painted it at night, with the stars overhead, by the light of the gas lamps.

If this wall is empty — the painting travels to Tokyo this winter — walk to the self-portrait in a suit, which hangs in this room, and listen there.

[curious] It's September 1888, in Arles. Van Gogh writes to Theo that he has painted a starry sky, and that it was actually painted at night, under a gas jet. Then he describes what he saw. "Against the green-blue field of the sky the Great Bear has a green and pink sparkle whose discreet paleness contrasts with the harsh gold of the gaslight."

[pause] On the canvas, the blues do most of the work: Prussian blue, ultramarine and cobalt. In the museum's words, he set down the colours he perceived in the dark. The town's gaslights glow an intense orange, and their reflections spill into the Rhône. Above it all, the stars sparkle like gemstones.

[warmly] And right down at the bottom there's a couple, a pair of lovers, easy to miss. They change the mood. They make the whole night more serene.

The next year, Theo sent the canvas with the Irises to the exhibition of the Indépendants. One critic, Félix Fénéon, called Van Gogh "a diverting colourist even in eccentricities like his Starry Night." A backhanded compliment. It hasn't aged as well as the painting.

[lightly] Now give the self-portrait a look, whether you're standing at it already or can see it across the room. He painted it a year after that night by the Rhône, at Saint-Rémy. The stars are gone. But the background still moves, in pale green and turquoise, curling round a man who sits completely still.

In room 36, find the church at Auvers, its windows deep blue under a cobalt sky, and listen to its track there.
