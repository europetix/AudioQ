# Yo Tours · Museo Nacional Thyssen-Bornemisza · audio guide scripts (EN) · 23 Oct 2026 edition

## Opening

### 001 · Welcome — a palace, two barons and seven centuries
*Entrance hall · 357 words*

**Where:** In the central hall on Level 0, just inside the entrance from the Paseo del Prado, before you walk to the far end of the hall and go up.

**What:** Welcome and how the guide works: the Thyssen-Bornemisza collection, built by Heinrich (1875–1947) and Hans Heinrich Thyssen-Bornemisza (1921–2002); 1988 loan of 775 works; opened 8 October 1992 in the Palacio de Villahermosa, converted by Rafael Moneo; 1993 purchase by the Spanish State; Carmen Thyssen collection on Level 0. In passing: Tintoretto, Paradise, ca. 1588, oil on canvas, Central hall.

This museum began as a loan. In 1988, Baron Hans Heinrich Thyssen-Bornemisza lent Spain 775 works from his family's collection, with a view to selling them later. In 1993 the Spanish State bought them. They are the heart of what you'll see today, gathered over two generations, by his father Heinrich and then by Hans Heinrich himself.

To house the collection, the architect Rafael Moneo converted an old aristocratic palace on the Paseo del Prado, the Palacio de Villahermosa. The museum opened in the autumn of 1992. Today the ground floor also holds his wife Carmen Thyssen's collection.

We'll walk it from the top down. On Level 2 you start with gold-ground panels from medieval Italy and move forward in time, room by room. Level 1 carries the story on to Pop Art. Then the Carmen Thyssen collection on Level 0, and out onto the Paseo del Prado. Every stop gives the room number from the museum's map, often with the room's title.

This guide comes in three lengths. The full tour takes about two and a half hours, the essential version about an hour and a quarter, and the highlights about forty minutes. Whichever one is on your phone, play it in order. The numbers may jump, and every stop tells you where to go next.

Some stops are short Extras, to skip whenever you like. And one honest warning. This museum lends a great deal, and the map itself warns that works are often out on loan. Now and then you may find a note where a painting should be. The temporary exhibitions are not part of this guide.

On your way, look out in this hall for a long, crowded canvas of heaven. It's Tintoretto's Paradise, his proposal for the great council hall of the Doge's Palace in Venice after a fire. That palace comes back upstairs.

Walk to the far end of the central hall, away from the Paseo del Prado doors, and take the stairs or the lift up to Level 2. Room 1 is where the map marks "Recommended start of the visit". Look for small gold-ground panels from 14th-century Siena.

## Level 2 · The first faces

### 002 · Room 1 · Duccio di Buoninsegna — Christ and the Samaritan Woman
*Room 1 · 330 words*

**Where:** Room 1, "14th Century. Early Italian Painting", the first room at the top of the stairs. Find the small, almost square gold-ground panel, about 44 by 46 cm: Christ seated on a well, a woman with a pitcher on her head, and a walled city with a gate on the right.

**What:** Duccio di Buoninsegna, Christ and the Samaritan Woman, 1310–1311, tempera and gold on panel, 43.5 × 46 cm, inv. 133 (1971.7). Also: Master of the Magdalen, The Virgin and Child enthroned with Saints Dominic and Martin, and two angels, ca. 1290, tempera and gold on panel, 177 × 86.5 cm, Room 1.

When this little panel was finished, a procession of dignitaries walked it through the streets of Siena. Well, not this panel alone. It was one small piece of an enormous altarpiece, the Maestà, carried from Duccio's workshop to the cathedral in 1311.

Welcome to Level 2 and the old masters. The walls up here keep their classic salmon colour, and the rooms run in order of time, so as you walk you move forward through the centuries. You start in the 1300s.

The Maestà was huge, around five metres tall, and painted on both sides. This scene came from the back, from the predella, the strip of small pictures along the base. The altarpiece was taken apart around 1771, and its panels scattered. This one passed through collections in London and New York before it came here.

The story is from the Gospel of John. Christ, tired from the road, sits on the edge of Jacob's well outside the Samaritan city of Sychar. A woman arrives with a pitcher balanced on her head, and he asks her for a drink. Now watch the hands. There's no speech here, so the whole conversation is carried by gestures. On the right, four apostles come out through the city gate carrying food, and a rocky road runs down to the well.

The background is still pure gold, the timeless space of heaven. But look at the fine gold lines tracing the folds of Christ's cloak, and the soft light and shade on the faces. Duccio is giving his figures weight, and his town real depth.

Before you move on, find the tall Virgin and Child enthroned between Saint Dominic and Saint Martin, still in its original stuccoed frame. It was painted around 1290, and it's the oldest painting in the whole collection. Seven centuries start right here.

Room 2 has a short Extra on a 15th-century birth scene full of busy women. The next main stop is Room 3, for Gerard David's Crucifixion.

### 003 · Room 2 · Extra · Anonymous German artist active in Konstanz — The Birth of the Virgin
*Room 2 · 225 words*

**Where:** Room 2, "15th Century. German and Spanish Painting". Look for a tall, narrow panel, about 85 by 35 cm: a bedroom with a bed set at a slant, Saint Anne sitting up in it and women busy all around her.

**What:** Anonymous German artist, active in Konstanz, ca. 1430, The Birth of the Virgin, ca. 1430, panel, 84.5 × 34.7 cm, inv. 262 (1967.1.1). Also: Joan Mates, Saint John the Baptist and Saint John the Evangelist with a Donor, ca. 1410, oil on panel, 191 × 121 cm, Room 2.

Everyone around this bed is busy except the new mother. And even she is getting ready for dinner.

The Gospels say nothing about the birth of the Virgin Mary. The story comes from the apocryphal gospels. Around 1430, an unknown painter in Konstanz, in southern Germany, imagined it as a birth in a house of his own day.

Saint Anne sits up in bed, fully dressed, with her headdress and her halo. She's washing her hands before she eats, and a woman helps her. Another woman comes in carrying food in two covered dishes. In front, on a bench, a young woman cradles the baby, already bathed and tightly swaddled. The bed cuts across the room on a slant, and divides it into little zones of activity. The perspective wobbles a bit. Nobody seems to mind.

At the right-hand edge, the panel has been cut down, and a pair of hands survives there, perhaps from someone kneeling in prayer.

Also in this room is the big Catalan panel of the two Saint Johns with a kneeling donor, painted around 1410 by an artist working in Barcelona. Until 1989 it was catalogued as the work of an anonymous master. Today it carries the name of Joan Mates.

Next door, Room 3, early Netherlandish painting: look for Gerard David's Crucifixion, with its sharp oranges, greens and yellows.

### 004 · Room 3 · Gerard David — The Crucifixion
*Room 3 · 349 words*

**Where:** Room 3, "15th Century. Early Netherlandish Painting". Find the upright panel, about 88 by 56 cm: Christ on the cross just off centre, the Virgin, Saint John and the Magdalen on the left, a crowd of onlookers on the right with a man in bright striped clothes in front, all in sharp oranges, greens and yellows.

**What:** Gerard David, The Crucifixion (La Crucifixión), ca. 1475, oil on panel, 88 × 56 cm, inv. 125 (1928.3). Also: Follower of Dirck Bouts, The Virgin and Child (La Virgen con el Niño), ca. 1465, oil on panel, 28.5 × 20 cm, inv. 60 (1933.10), Room 3. Jan van Eyck, The Annunciation Diptych, named only as away on loan.

Here is a Crucifixion that holds back the pain. Gerard David barely dwells on the wounds in the hands and feet. What he gives you instead is a crowd, and colour.

David was born in Oudewater, in the northern Low Countries, and made his career in Bruges, where he's first recorded in 1484. This panel is thought to be one of his earliest, from around 1475, though its date has been much debated.

Christ hangs just off centre, and divides the picture in two. On the left, the Virgin, Saint John and Mary Magdalene are set along diagonal lines. That grouping looks back to Rogier van der Weyden, the great Flemish painter of the generation before. On the right stand the onlookers. Find the man in front, in bright striped clothes. Scholars see Robert Campin behind him, because Campin loved striped costume.

And he has a twin. He and the man beside him are identical to two figures in a famous illuminated prayer book, the Hours of Catherine of Cleves, made around 1440 and now in New York.

Now the colour. Those sharp, almost acid oranges, greens and yellows are David's signature. His real gift, in the end, was landscape, and it leads on to Patinir, whom you'll meet in Room 10.

One honest note. Jan van Eyck's tiny Annunciation, which usually hangs in this room, is on loan to the Mauritshuis in The Hague until January 2027.

But Van Eyck is still here, in a way. Find the small Virgin and Child by a follower of Dirck Bouts, made around 1465 for private prayer. It's built on Van Eyck's Virgin at the Fountain, with the same embroidered cloth behind Mary and the same low wall of stone blocks. The angels who held up the cloth have gone, and Van Eyck's flower garden has turned to grass. On the Child's leg rests a small apple. It marks him as the Redeemer, and Mary as the new Eve.

Room 4 has a short Extra on Bramantino's pale, red-eyed Risen Christ. The next main stop is Room 5, the portrait room.

### 005 · Room 4 · Extra · Bramantino — The Risen Christ
*Room 4 · 225 words*

**Where:** Room 4, "15th Century. Italian Painting". Look for an upright panel, about 109 by 73 cm: a pale Christ facing you to below the waist, wounds in his hands and side, red-rimmed eyes, a dark background and a small river view with a tall-masted ship on the left.

**What:** Bramantino (Bartolomeo Suardi), The Risen Christ, ca. 1490, mixed media on panel, 109 × 73 cm, inv. 61 (1937.1). Also: Paolo Uccello, The Crucifixion with the Virgin, Saint John the Baptist, Saint John the Evangelist and Saint Francis, ca. 1460–1465, tempera on panel, 45 × 67 cm, Room 4.

This is Christ after the Resurrection. And he doesn't look as if he has won.

For a long time the picture had the wrong painter and the wrong title. When it was first published, in 1898, it was given to the great architect Bramante. A few years later a scholar suggested his pupil instead, Bartolomeo Suardi, known as Bramantino, and today the museum counts it among Bramantino's masterpieces.

The title changed too. It used to be called an Ecce Homo, Christ shown to the crowd before his death. But there's no crown of thorns here, no reed, no bound hands. Instead, look at the wounds in his hands and his side. This is after the Crucifixion, and the dark shape behind him may be the tomb.

Look at his eyes. They're red, as if from weeping. His body has an almost ghostly pallor, while his face, hair and hands are warmer. And on the left, a small, calm world goes on without him: a river, a tall-masted ship, two tents topped with golden balls.

In this room too is a small Crucifixion by Paolo Uccello. Vasari said Uccello was so absorbed in perspective that he ended up as poor as he was famous.

Next, Room 5, "Renaissance Portraiture": look for the young Florentine woman in profile, the image on the cover of the museum map.

## Level 2 · Renaissance portraits and the Villahermosa Gallery

### 006 · Room 5 · Domenico Ghirlandaio — Portrait of Giovanna degli Albizzi Tornabuoni
*Room 5 · 259 words*

**Where:** Room 5, "15th and 16th Centuries. Renaissance Portraiture". Find the young woman in strict profile, richly dressed, in front of a dark niche holding a jewel, a small book, a string of beads and a little slip of paper with writing: the picture on the cover of the museum map, about 77 by 49 cm.

**What:** Domenico Ghirlandaio (Domenico Bigordi), Portrait of Giovanna degli Albizzi Tornabuoni, 1489–1490, mixed media on panel, 77 × 49 cm, inv. 158 (1935.6). Also named: Robert Campin, Portrait of a Stout Man. Robert de Masmines (?), ca. 1425, Room 5; Francesco del Cossa, Portrait of a Man with a Ring, ca. 1472–1477, Room 5.

Behind this young woman there's a little slip of paper, painted with a Latin verse and a date in Roman numerals. The date is 1488. That's the year she died.

Her name was Giovanna degli Albizzi. She married Lorenzo Tornabuoni of Florence in 1486, and two years later she died in childbirth. Ghirlandaio also painted her full-length in a fresco in the church of Santa Maria Novella. So was this panel painted from life, or as a memorial? Scholars disagree: one argued it came before the fresco, another saw it as a portrait made after her death.

Look into the niche behind her. A jewel with a dragon, two pearls and a ruby, for her public life. A prayer book and a string of coral beads, a rosary, for her inner life. And between them that verse, adapted from the Roman poet Martial. "Art, if only you could reproduce the character and the spirit. There would be no finer portrait in the world."

This is the portrait room, and it shows how the portrait was born. In Italy, painters kept the strict profile, like a face on a coin. In the north, they turned their sitters towards you. Find Robert Campin's stout man, every wrinkle and bristle of stubble on show. Then Francesco del Cossa's man holding up a ring, almost full face. An Italian who has turned to meet you.

Giovanna's profile is the museum's signature image, and now you know why it feels so still.

Stay in Room 5 for Holbein's tiny portrait of Henry the Eighth.

### 007 · Room 5 · Hans Holbein the Younger — Portrait of Henry VIII of England
*Room 5 · 311 words*

**Where:** Room 5, "15th and 16th Centuries. Renaissance Portraiture". Look for the smallest royal face in the room, a panel only about 28 by 20 cm: Henry VIII square to you, in a flat hat with a jewelled pendant, hands visible below.

**What:** Hans Holbein the Younger, Portrait of Henry VIII of England, ca. 1537, oil on panel, 28 × 20 cm, inv. 191 (1934.39). Also: Robert Campin, Portrait of a Stout Man. Robert de Masmines (?), ca. 1425, oil on panel, 35.4 × 23.7 cm, Room 5.

One of the most powerful men in Europe, and he would fit inside a school notebook. This panel is only about twenty-eight centimetres tall.

Hans Holbein came back to England in 1532, after hard years in Basel. Many of his old patrons were dead or out of favour. Within a few years he was working for the king, and he was valued for one thing above all: faces that looked alive. He painted Henry's queens and possible queens, and he painted Henry.

This little portrait dates from around 1537. Henry had already broken with Rome and made himself head of the Church in England. Now look at how Holbein builds that power. The body is square to you, filling the panel. There's a sense of distance, as if you're being received. The hands sit firmly in view. One scholar spoke of the head's architectural solidity, and you can see what he meant. It's built like a wall.

Here's the twist. The Henry most of us picture, the giant figure who seems to fill a room, mostly comes from somewhere else. Holbein painted a great mural for a private room in Whitehall Palace, with Henry full-length beside his parents and Jane Seymour. It was destroyed in 1698. We know it from a copy, now in London, and the head in that copy is this head, with the same hat and the same splendid pendant jewel.

So this panel is a close-up of a lost icon. Compare it with Campin's stout man nearby, painted about a century earlier. Different worlds, the same refusal to flatter.

Small enough to hold in two hands. Hard enough to stop you in your tracks.

Room 5 has a short Extra on Memling's praying young man and the flowers painted on the back of his panel. The next main stop is the long gallery next door, Room 6.

### 008 · Room 5 · Extra · Hans Memling — Portrait of a Young Man praying
*Room 5 · 210 words*

**Where:** Room 5, "15th and 16th Centuries. Renaissance Portraiture". Look for a small panel, about 29 by 22 cm: a young man in three-quarter view with his hands joined in prayer, in a loggia that opens onto a landscape, with a patterned rug on the ledge in front of him.

**What:** Hans Memling, Portrait of a Young Man praying (recto), ca. 1485, oil on panel, 29.2 × 22.5 cm, inv. 284.a (1938.1.a). On the reverse: Flowers in a Jug.

This young man is praying to someone who isn't here anymore.

The pose gives it away: his hands are joined, and his eyes are fixed on something beyond the frame. The museum thinks he was one wing of a small triptych. In the centre, a Virgin and Child. On the other wing, probably a woman donor, praying too. Only his panel is here, and nobody knows his name. He may have belonged to the Italian community in Bruges, where Memling worked, though some scholars have suggested a Spanish client.

Look at what's around him. A loggia opens onto a soft landscape, and there's an oriental rug on the ledge. Memling didn't invent this kind of open background, but he did a great deal to spread it.

Now the surprise. On the back of this panel Memling painted a jug of flowers, marked with Christ's monogram. Lilies for Mary's purity, irises for her as Queen of Heaven, and aquilegias for the Holy Spirit. It's one of the first independent still lifes known in art. If the panel is shown so that you can see its back, walk round and look.

Step into the long Villahermosa Gallery, Room 6: look for a Venetian woman with roses and a monkey on a chain.

### 009 · Room 6 · Paris Bordone — Portrait of a Young Woman
*Room 6 · 277 words*

**Where:** Room 6, "16th Century. Villahermosa Gallery", the long gallery that runs from Room 5 towards Room 13. Find the half-length Venetian woman, about 103 by 83 cm, in a low-cut, costly dress, holding two roses, with a small monkey on a chain eating on a ledge beside her.

**What:** Paris Bordone, Portrait of a Young Woman, ca. 1543–1550, oil on canvas, 103 × 83 cm, inv. 55 (1936.5). Also: Pier Francesco Mola, Saint John the Baptist Preaching in the Desert, ca. 1650–1655, oil on canvas, 73.5 × 99 cm, Room 6. The building: Palacio de Villahermosa.

In 1844, Franz Liszt gave a concert in this building. A plaque outside still remembers it. Back then this was a palace, the home of the Dukes of Villahermosa, and its main floor housed the Artistic and Literary Lyceum, a meeting place of Madrid's Romantic writers and artists.

The palace's sober façades took their present shape in the early 1800s, by the architect Antonio López Aguado. The museum's map calls this long room the Villahermosa Gallery, the name of the palace you are standing in. Today it mixes centuries, so a sixteenth-century Venetian hangs here beside a seventeenth-century painter who worked in Rome.

Find the young woman holding two roses. Paris Bordone trained in Titian's workshop, and she's a type Titian and Palma had made popular: poised and beautiful, in a costly dress cut very low. The roses belong to Venus. She has probably just taken them from that fine vessel decorated with satyrs.

Now follow the thin chain in her left hand. It runs along her arm and round her back, all the way to a small monkey, eating on a ledge. In the language of the time, the monkey stood for vice and lust. And she's the one holding the chain. The museum reads the whole picture in an amorous key. Love, kept under control. Just about.

In this gallery too, look for Mola's Saint John the Baptist preaching, where the desert has turned green and a crowd sits at the foot of a mountain.

Rooms 7 to 10 open off the long side of this gallery. Start with Room 7: a young knight standing full length in a landscape full of plants and animals.

### 010 · Room 7 · Vittore Carpaccio — Young Knight in a Landscape
*Room 7 · 310 words*

**Where:** Room 7, "16th Century. Italian Painting", one of the rooms off the long side of Room 6. Find the tall canvas, over two metres high: a young man in armour standing full length in a meadow thick with plants and flowers, a deer by a lake, a rider in the background and a small weasel near a scroll with writing.

**What:** Vittore Carpaccio, Young Knight in a Landscape, ca. 1505 (cartellino dated M.D.X.), oil on canvas, 218.5 × 151.5 cm, inv. 82 (1935.3).

When this painting was sold in London in 1919, it was called Saint Eustace. There's a deer beside the lake, and a deer is Saint Eustace's sign. So for a while, this young man was a saint.

Today he's a knight, and if he's a real person, he's a pioneer. The museum puts it carefully: if it is in fact a portrait, it would be the first known example in which the sitter is depicted full-length. Carpaccio gives you a whole man, from his hair to his armoured feet, on a canvas more than two metres tall.

When was it painted? The museum dates it around 1505. But a little slip of paper on the canvas is signed Victor Carpathius and dated in Roman numerals: 1510. So, somewhere between the two.

Then there's the motto. On a small scroll in the landscape are the Latin words Malo mori quam foedari. Better to die than be defiled. Beside it sits a small weasel, an ermine. That was the motto and the emblem of the Order of the Ermine, so he may have been one of its knights.

But who? Many names have been suggested, from a king of Aragon to a Venetian captain who fought the Turks. The most accepted answer is Francesco Maria della Rovere, later Duke of Urbino, though even that is not settled.

Look at the plants around him. Every flower and leaf is painted so minutely that the picture was once thought to be by Dürer. Then find the rider in black and gold in the background, and the deer by the lake that once made him a saint.

A saint, a duke, or a captain. Whoever he was, he's still standing his ground.

Room 7 has a short Extra on Titian's portrait of Doge Francesco Venier. The next main stop is Room 9, through Room 8.

### 011 · Room 7 · Extra · Titian — Portrait of Doge Francesco Venier
*Room 7 · 212 words*

**Where:** Room 7, "16th Century. Italian Painting". Look for an old man in the doge's gold brocade robes and cap, about 113 by 99 cm, standing before a curtain, with a window on the left where a boat is burning on the water.

**What:** Titian (Tiziano Vecellio), Portrait of Doge Francesco Venier, ca. 1554–1556, oil on canvas, 113 × 99 cm, inv. 405 (1930.116).

Francesco Venier ruled Venice as its doge for just two years. He was so unwell that he needed help to move about.

Look at how Titian shows that. The body under the gold brocade seems fragile, almost propped up. But the eyes are sharp. Nothing gets past this man.

As official painter to the Venetian Republic, Titian painted each new doge for a long frieze of portraits in the great council hall of the Doge's Palace. Then, in 1577, fire swept through the palace, and that gallery of faces was lost, Venier's official portrait with it.

This canvas survived because it was never there. It's Titian's own replica of the lost image, perhaps made for the doge's brother, or for one of the city's offices. So this is now the face of Venier.

Through the window on the left, out on the water, a boat is burning. Nobody has identified the episode. It may simply stand for the power of the city.

If you saw Tintoretto's long Paradise in the central hall downstairs, that was part of the same story: the palace needed new paintings after that fire.

On through Room 8, German painting, where Cranach's Virgin holds a bunch of grapes, to Room 9: Cranach's portrait of the Emperor Charles V.

### 012 · Room 9 · Lucas Cranach the Elder — Portrait of the Emperor Charles V
*Room 9 · 316 words*

**Where:** Room 9, "15th and 16th Centuries. German Painting", after Room 8. Find the half-length portrait, about 51 by 36 cm, of a man turned to the left, with a long face, a jutting jaw and a gold chain with a small golden fleece hanging from it.

**What:** Lucas Cranach the Elder, Portrait of the Emperor Charles V, 1533, oil on panel, 51.2 × 36 cm, inv. 112 (1933.7). Also: Hans Holbein the Elder, Portrait of a Woman, ca. 1518–1520, oil on panel, 23.6 × 17 cm, Room 9; Albrecht Altdorfer, Portrait of a young Woman, ca. 1522, oil on panel, 59 × 45 cm, Room 9; passed in Room 8: Lucas Cranach the Elder, The Virgin and Child with a Bunch of Grapes, ca. 1509–1510, oil on panel.

Here is the Catholic emperor who ruled Spain, painted by the great painter of the Reformation. History has a sense of humour.

You've just walked through Room 8 into the German rooms. Lucas Cranach, whose Virgin with a bunch of grapes you passed next door, was court painter at Wittenberg and a personal friend of Martin Luther. And here he paints Charles V, the emperor who fought Luther's princes, in 1533.

They had met. Cranach first painted Charles as a boy of eight, a portrait now lost. Years later, after Charles defeated the Protestant princes at Mühlberg, the old painter went to plead with him for his own captured prince. One theory even says this panel belonged to a set of royal portraits that the reformers gave the emperor as a present. It's only a theory, but a tempting one.

Now look at the face. Titian's official portraits of Charles soften it. Cranach doesn't. The long face, the jutting jaw, the big nose and the full lower lip are all there. And there's almost nothing to tell you he's an emperor. Just one gold chain, made of links shaped like flames and flints, with the little golden fleece of his chivalric order hanging from it.

Two women in this room have been given the wrong names. Holbein the Elder, father of the man who painted Henry the Eighth, painted this tiny profile, and it was once identified with its pair as Luther and his wife. That's now rejected. And Altdorfer's young woman was said to be Barbara Blomberg, later the mother of Charles's son, Don John of Austria. The trouble is, Barbara wasn't born until 1527.

One note for this season: Dürer's Jesus Among the Doctors, usually in these rooms, is on loan to The Hague until January 2027.

Next, Room 10, 16th-century Netherlandish painting: Patinir's wide landscape with a tiny Holy Family resting in it.

### 013 · Room 10 · Joachim Patinir — Landscape with the Rest on the Flight into Egypt
*Room 10 · 314 words*

**Where:** Room 10, "16th Century. Netherlandish Painting". Find the small horizontal panel, about 32 by 58 cm, seen from high up: the Virgin and Child resting by a fountain in front, Joseph in the trees behind them, a river winding away on the left and a fortress on a rocky hill on the right.

**What:** Joachim Patinir, Landscape with the Rest on the Flight into Egypt, ca. 1518–1524, oil on panel, 31.5 × 57.5 cm, inv. 314 (1930.85). Also: Jan Wellens de Cock, The Temptation of Saint Anthony, ca. 1520, oil on panel, 60 × 45.5 cm, Room 10; Jan Mostaert, The Expulsion of Hagar, ca. 1520–1525, oil on panel, 94 × 131 cm, Room 10.

In 1520, Albrecht Dürer travelled to the Low Countries. In Antwerp he visited the workshop of Joachim Patinir, and called him "a good painter of landscapes". Coming from Dürer, that was a compliment.

The museum calls Patinir the great early forerunner of landscape painting. Only around twenty works are given to him, and just five are signed. If you've been to the Prado, you may already have met him there.

The title says this is the Rest on the Flight into Egypt. The Holy Family is escaping King Herod. But look how small they've become. Mary sits with the Child by a fountain, their luggage around them: a wicker basket, saddle-bags, a rough walking staff. Behind them, Joseph is searching the branches of a tree for something to eat.

Now let your eye travel. Patinir puts you up high, and builds the world in bands, one behind another, broken by tall trees. On the left, hills roll away and a river winds into the distance. On the right, a fortress sits on a rocky hill among the trees. The story is still there. But the real subject is the world itself.

For a very different mood, find Jan de Cock's Temptation of Saint Anthony. The hermit kneels and prays on the left, while on the right the Devil sends a group of women wearing almost nothing but thin veils. Strange hybrid creatures, straight out of Bosch, crowd the landscape. The scene alludes to the saint's chastity, though the museum admits it is certainly not devoid of erotic connotations.

And in Jan Mostaert's large Expulsion of Hagar, Abraham sends a mother and her son out into a desert that looks remarkably green.

Next, back through the long gallery to Room 11, "Tiziano, Tintoretto, Bassano and El Greco": look for an early Annunciation by the young El Greco, with a chequered floor and a pink curtain.

## Level 2 · Venice and the Baroque

### 014 · Room 11 · El Greco — The Annunciation
*Room 11 · 270 words*

**Where:** Room 11, "Tiziano, Tintoretto, Bassano and El Greco". Find the canvas about 117 by 98 cm: the Virgin at a prayer desk on the left, the angel arriving on the right, a chequered floor, a pink curtain and angels in a cloud of light above.

**What:** El Greco (Domenikos Theotokopoulos), The Annunciation, ca. 1576, oil on canvas, 117 × 98 cm, inv. 172 (1975.35). Also: Tintoretto, The Meeting of Tamar and Judah, and The Annunciation to Manoah's Wife, both ca. 1555–1559, oil on canvas, 150 × 155 cm, Room 11.

In Florence, painters started with drawing. In Venice, they started with colour and light, and let the paint itself carry the feeling. This room is the Venetian sixteenth century, and its title names its heroes.

And one outsider. Before he became El Greco of Toledo, Domenikos Theotokopoulos was a painter from Crete working in Italy, soaking up everything Venice could teach him. This Annunciation, from around 1576, is one of the last versions he painted in Italy, before he left for Spain.

Look at how many teachers are in it. The elegant angel is very much in the style of Veronese. The light and colour owe a great deal to Titian. And the way the two figures are placed comes from a composition by Tintoretto in Venice. Mary sits at her prayer desk on the left, listening intently. A chequered floor runs back, with a pink curtain and a balustrade behind.

Now look up. In the cloud of light, some small angels are seen sharply from below. That's a glimpse of the painter he's about to become in Spain.

The museum owns a second, much later Annunciation by El Greco, painted in Spain. That one is on loan to The Hague until January 2027.

Before you leave, find Tintoretto's pair of large canvases, a woman on the left in each, open landscape between. In one, the widow Tamar tricks her father-in-law Judah, who finally has to admit, "She hath been more righteous than I."

Room 11 has a short Extra on Titian's late Penitent Saint Jerome. The next main stop is Room 12: Caravaggio's Saint Catherine beside her spiked wheel.

### 015 · Room 11 · Extra · Titian — The Penitent Saint Jerome
*Room 11 · 223 words*

**Where:** Room 11, "Tiziano, Tintoretto, Bassano and El Greco". Look for a dark night scene, about 137 by 97 cm: an old, half-naked saint kneeling among rocks in a red tunic, a stone in his hand, a crucifix on a branch and a lion in the lower right corner.

**What:** Titian (Tiziano Vecellio), The Penitent Saint Jerome, ca. 1575, oil on canvas, 137 × 97 cm, inv. 406 (1933.4).

There's a moon in this painting, but you can't see it. It's hidden behind the thick tree trunks, and still it lights up the night sky.

Titian painted this in his last years, around 1575. Twenty years earlier he had painted the same saint for a church in Venice, surrounded by symbols: a skull, an hourglass, books, ivy on the rocks. Here he lets almost all of that go. What remains is an old man, a stone in his right hand to beat his breast, his eyes on a crucifix set on a long branch.

Look at the colour. It's nearly all browns and earth, and in places the bare canvas shows through. The only strong colour is the red of his tunic, flicked quickly across his leg. And down in the lower right corner, a lion, its head almost lost in the dark.

The story goes that in his final years Titian painted more with his fingers than with his brushes. Whether or not that's true, you can see why people said it.

He died in 1576, during an outbreak of plague in Venice. This is the work of a man who no longer needed to explain anything.

Back in the long gallery, the next doorway along is Room 12, "Caravaggio and Baroque Painting": Saint Catherine kneeling by her broken, spiked wheel.

### 016 · Room 12 · Caravaggio — Saint Catherine of Alexandria
*Room 12 · 310 words*

**Where:** Room 12, "17th Century. Caravaggio and Baroque Painting". Find the large dark canvas, about 173 by 133 cm: a young woman in a rich blue-violet dress kneeling on a red cushion, leaning on a broken spiked wheel, a sword across her lap, light falling on her from the right.

**What:** Caravaggio (Michelangelo Merisi), Saint Catherine of Alexandria, ca. 1598–99, oil on canvas, 173 × 133 cm, inv. 81 (1934.37). Also: Jose de Ribera, The Pietà, 1633, oil on canvas, 157 × 210 cm, Room 12.

The model for this saint has been identified as a celebrated courtesan, Fillide Melandroni. In Rome around 1598 she posed for Caravaggio as a princess and martyr.

This is the seventeenth century arriving. Caravaggio painted real people from life and set them against deep darkness, picked out by strong light. Painters all over Europe followed him.

The picture was almost certainly made for Cardinal Francesco Maria del Monte, Caravaggio's first patron. After the cardinal's heir ran into debt, it was sold, and it passed to the Barberini family. It came to this collection in 1934.

Catherine of Alexandria was condemned to be broken on a spiked wheel. Here is the wheel, cracked and splintered. Here is the sword that took her life instead, and the martyr's palm. She kneels on a costly red damask cushion, in a rich dress of blues and violets, as befits a princess.

Now look at where the light comes from. It falls from the right. That's unusual for Caravaggio, and it has led to the idea that the painting was made for a particular place, with real light coming from that side.

And look at her face. Your eye follows the diagonals, the wheel, the sword, the turn of her body, and arrives at her gaze. A real woman of Caravaggio's Rome, looking back at you with complete composure.

Then find the wide Pietà by Jusepe de Ribera, the Spaniard who made his career in Naples. He signed it on the stone at the lower right, Jusepe de Ribera, español, with the date 1633. Here the darkness is starting to give way to colour.

Make your way to Room 13, at the end of the long gallery, with Claude's pastoral landscape and Vouet's Europa riding the white bull. Then turn into Room 14: a huge canvas where a baby is held up by one ankle.

### 017 · Room 14 · Luca Giordano — The Judgement of Solomon
*Room 14 · 321 words*

**Where:** Room 14, "17th Century. Italian, French and Spanish Painting", beside Room 13. Find the huge canvas, about 2.5 by 3 metres: King Solomon on his throne with his arm stretched out, two women below, and a baby held upside down by one ankle at the top of a pyramid of figures.

**What:** Luca Giordano, The Judgement of Solomon (El juicio de Salomón), ca. 1665, oil on canvas, 250.8 × 308 cm, inv. CTB.1991.8, Carmen Thyssen Collection. Also: Bernardo Cavallino, Lot and his Daughters, ca. 1640–1645, oil on panel, 41 × 38 cm, CTB.1994.3.1, Carmen Thyssen Collection, Room 14.

Two women, one baby, and both of them swear the child is theirs. King Solomon calls for a sword and orders the baby cut in two, one half for each mother.

It's the most famous judgement in the Bible, and Luca Giordano paints the second before anyone knows how it ends. Look at how the whole picture climbs, a pyramid of bodies rising to its peak. And at the very top, held upside down by one ankle, the baby.

Below Solomon's outstretched arm stand his armed men. In front of the throne, one woman kneels and the other stands. The Bible says the real mother is the one who would rather give up her child than see it die. Which one would you choose?

Giordano was from Naples, and he painted this around 1665, early in his career. You can see whom he learned from. The tough, real faces come from Ribera, the Spaniard in Naples whose Pietà you've just seen. The deep shadows come from Caravaggio's followers in the city.

This canvas belongs to the Carmen Thyssen collection, which you'll meet on the ground floor, but it hangs here among the old masters. In the eighteenth century it was in a palace in Vicenza, where a French abbé travelling through Italy with the young Fragonard wrote it down in his diary.

Giordano was astonishingly prolific. The museum says that his output in Spain over a decade exceeded what other artists could manage in a lifetime. In 1692 he was invited to Madrid as court painter to King Charles the Second. There was hardly a royal building he didn't touch, from the Escorial to the Casón del Buen Retiro, a few minutes' walk from here.

Also here, look for Bernardo Cavallino's small round panel of Lot and his daughters, one of them pouring the wine with a knowing smile.

Next, Room 15: Zurbarán's Christ on the Cross against a black background.

### 018 · Room 15 · Francisco de Zurbarán — Christ on the Cross
*Room 15 · 340 words*

**Where:** Room 15, "17th Century. Italian, French and Spanish Painting". Stand before the tall canvas, over two metres high, of Christ alone on the cross, pale and strongly lit against a black background. The large, soft-coloured Murillo altarpiece with a young saint and the Virgin and Child is in the same room.

**What:** Francisco de Zurbarán, Christ on the Cross, ca. 1630, oil on canvas, 214 × 143.5 cm, inv. 447. Also: Bartolomé Esteban Murillo, The Virgin and Child with Saint Rose of Viterbo, ca. 1670, oil on canvas, 190 × 147 cm, inv. 296.

Most paintings of the Crucifixion show the moment after. The head has dropped, and it's over. Zurbarán paints the moment before. This Christ is still alive.

He painted it around 1630, early in his career, when he was making his name in Seville with great series of paintings for the city's monasteries. The canvas is more than two metres tall, and there's almost nothing in it. A cross, a body, and darkness.

Look at the face first. The head tilts to one side. The eyes are raised, the mouth half open, as if in a last prayer. A few years earlier, in a canvas now in Chicago, Zurbarán had shown Christ serenely dead. Here he chose the pleading instead.

Then follow the light down the body. It falls from the front and lifts the pale figure clean off that black ground. Count the nails. There are four, one for each foot as well as each hand. That was the Seville way of painting the Crucifixion, and its great champion was the painter and writer Francisco Pacheco, Velázquez's father-in-law. Zurbarán followed it almost every time.

For nearly three centuries the picture went unpublished. It was first published in 1918, when it was in a private collection in Munich, and Baron Hans Heinrich Thyssen bought it in New York in 1956.

Now turn to the large Murillo, painted some forty years later and probably made for an altar. Everything softens. Rose of Viterbo, a young Franciscan saint, shares the foreground with the Virgin and Child. For years she was mistaken for another saint, Rosalia of Palermo. Behind her, four young martyrs in white carry palms, and on the left, in a little scene of its own, she preaches to a crowd. A British ambassador bought the painting in Spain, and it stayed in England for two centuries.

Two Seville painters, two ways of praying. Zurbarán asks you to stand still. Murillo invites you in.

Next, Room 16, 18th-century Italian painting: look for a young woman in profile, holding a little carnival mask.

## Level 2 · The 18th century in Italy

### 019 · Room 16 · Giambattista Piazzetta — Portrait of a Young Woman in Profile with a Mask in her Right Hand
*Room 16 · 249 words*

**Where:** Room 16, "18th Century. Italian Painting". Look for a small canvas, under half a metre high, of a young woman turning her head in profile, a black cloak over her shoulders and a tiny mask held between finger and thumb. Longhi's small interior with a girl tickling a sleeping man, and Tiepolo's small oil sketch of a dying queen, are in the same room.

**What:** Giambattista Piazzetta, Portrait of a Young Woman in Profile with a Mask in her Right Hand (Retrato de una joven de perfil con una máscara en la mano derecha), ca. 1720–1730, oil on canvas, 46 × 36.2 cm, Carmen Thyssen Collection, CTB.1999.5. Also: Pietro Longhi, The Tickle, ca. 1755, oil on canvas, inv. 224; Giambattista Tiepolo, The Death of Sophonisba, ca. 1760, oil on canvas, inv. 396.

She has just turned her head, as if someone had called her name across a crowded room. And in her right hand, held between finger and thumb, is the little mask she was wearing a moment ago.

You've reached the eighteenth century. Rooms 16 to 18 are Italian painting, and most of it is Venetian. It's the Venice of Carnival and masks, of view painters working for travellers, and of Tiepolo's theatrical light.

Giambattista Piazzetta painted this small canvas in the 1720s. Over her shoulders she wears the black bautta, the cloak of Venetian women, opened just enough to show a modest neckline. She has been identified as Rosa Muziolo, a model who became the painter's wife in 1724. That may or may not be right. But the same face turns up again and again in Piazzetta's work, so painter and model clearly knew each other well. Piazzetta was usually a painter of deep shadow and heavy drama. Here he's almost tender.

For pure Venetian comedy, find Pietro Longhi's The Tickle. A young woman tickles a sleeping man's face with a feather, while two maids look on. She glances straight out at you, inviting you to keep the secret.

And Tiepolo's little oil sketch is pure tragedy. Sophonisba, a queen in Africa, lies dying beside a cup of poison. Rome wanted her as a prisoner, so her own husband sent it to her. She drank.

Next, Room 17: two sunlit views of Venice by Canaletto, hung as a pair.

### 020 · Room 17 · Canaletto — The Grand Canal from San Vio, Venice
*Room 17 · 333 words*

**Where:** Room 17, "18th Century. Italian Painting". Stand before the two large, bright Canalettos hung as a pair, each about two metres wide: the Grand Canal under a big blue sky, and the Piazza San Marco seen from high up. The two small, misty Guardi views of the Grand Canal are in the same room.

**What:** Canaletto (Giovanni Antonio Canal), The Grand Canal from San Vio, Venice, ca. 1723–1724, oil on canvas, 140.5 × 204.5 cm, inv. 76. Also: Canaletto, The Piazza San Marco in Venice, ca. 1723–1724, oil on canvas, 141.5 × 204.5 cm, inv. 75; Francesco Guardi, The Grand Canal with Santa Lucia and Santa Maria di Nazareth and The Grand Canal with San Simeone Piccolo and Santa Lucia, both ca. 1780, oil on canvas, 48 × 78 cm, inv. 174 and 175.

You can date this pair of paintings by the pavement.

Start with the view of the Piazza San Marco. Canaletto painted it from high up, where a church once stood at the far end of the square. In 1723, Venice began relaying the square to a new design. Look at the ground. The paving near the arcades is finished, but the middle is still bare. So Canaletto was painting at the very start of the work, around 1723 or 1724.

Now the Grand Canal from San Vio, one of the earliest views by him that we know. On the right, on the Palazzo Barbarigo, a woman leans out of a window and a man is sweeping a chimney. The line of palaces leads your eye down the water to the white dome of the Salute. The boats in the foreground sit in shadow and pull you in. Above it all, greenish water meets an enormous blue sky.

These two belonged to a series owned by the Princes of Liechtenstein in Vienna. Two more from the same series are back in Venice today, in the Ca' Rezzonico. Baron Thyssen bought this pair two years apart, in the 1950s.

Then step across to the two small Guardis, painted about half a century later. Francesco Guardi was Tiepolo's brother-in-law, and he started out imitating Canaletto. By around 1780 he had his own way of seeing. The land shrinks. Water and sky almost melt into each other, and the boats are just flicks of the brush.

Look for the church of Santa Lucia on the bank. If you've ever arrived in Venice by train, you've stood on that spot. The church was pulled down in the nineteenth century to make way for the railway station.

Canaletto shows you Venice as it was measured. Guardi shows you Venice as it felt.

### 021 · Room 17 · Extra · Giambattista Tiepolo — The Death of Hyacinthus
*Room 17 · 218 words*

**Where:** Room 17. Find the huge canvas, nearly three metres high, of a youth lying on the ground and an anguished Apollo bending over him, with a tennis racquet and balls on the tiled floor.

**What:** Giambattista Tiepolo, The Death of Hyacinthus, ca. 1752–1753, oil on canvas, 287 × 232 cm, inv. 394. Cue only: Giambattista Piazzetta, Portrait of Giulia Lama, ca. 1715–1720, oil on canvas, inv. 316, Room 18.

In Ovid's poem, the god Apollo kills the boy he loves with a discus. Tiepolo has swapped the discus for a tennis ball.

He had a reason. An Italian translation of Ovid from 1561 had already turned the discus into a ball, and the court game was still the height of fashion among the nobility. Tiepolo painted this huge canvas around 1752, during his years in Würzburg, in Germany.

Look down at the floor. There's the racquet with its balls, and a third ball has rolled away to the left. Behind the onlookers you can see part of the net. Hyacinthus lies with a bruise on his cheek, looking up at Apollo. And the stone god Pan seems to have turned his head, smiling, so as not to miss a thing. In the right-hand corner, the flowers are already growing.

Go back into Room 16 and walk on north through Room 18, where you'll find Piazzetta's portrait of Giulia Lama, a Venetian painter in her own right, palette in hand. Straight ahead, Room 22 has a short Extra on Crespi, and the next main stop is Room 23. Until 10 January 2027, the Classical rooms 19 to 21, along the corridor to your left, hold a visiting exhibition from the Mauritshuis, which is not part of this guide.

### 022 · Room 22 · Extra · Giuseppe Maria Crespi — Portrait of Count Fulvio Grati, and two peasant scenes on copper
*Room 22 · 169 words*

**Where:** Room 22, "18th Century. Italian Painting". Stand before the tall, almost life-size portrait of a seated nobleman with a lute on his lap, a curtain behind him and a Black page at the right. The two small paintings on copper of peasant life are by the same painter, in the same room.

**What:** Giuseppe Maria Crespi, Portrait of Count Fulvio Grati, ca. 1720–1723, oil on canvas, 228 × 153 cm, inv. 116. Also: Crespi, Peasants with Donkeys (Campesinos con asnos) and Interior with a Family of Peasants (Interior con familia de campesinos), both ca. 1709, oil on copper, 39.4 × 31.1 cm, Carmen Thyssen Collection, CTB.1994.1.2 and CTB.1994.1.1.

Same painter, two ends of society.

On one wall is Count Fulvio Grati, painted almost life-size around 1720. He has a lute on his lap and his fingertips on a little mandolin. A servant holds up a great curtain behind him, and a richly dressed Black page brings the music. A small dog peeps out from behind the lute case, looking straight at you. The message is clear. This count is a man of music.

Now find the two little paintings on copper, each not much bigger than a large book. In one, peasants stand with their donkeys. In the other, a family is at home, a woman cooks and two boys play a game. Crespi painted them around 1709, after visits to the Medici court in Florence, where he studied the Bamboccianti, painters of humble everyday life.

What's striking is the respect. No mockery, no sermon. Just people, painted as carefully as the count.

## Level 2 · The Dutch Golden Age and the French salon

### 023 · Room 23 · Frans Hals — Family Group in a Landscape
*Room 23 · 248 words*

**Where:** Room 23, "17th Century. Dutch Painting. Landscape". Stand before the huge canvas, about two metres high and almost three wide, of a family posed outdoors, husband and wife holding hands, a dog beside a little girl, and a wide landscape opening on the right. Van Goyen's small frozen river scene is in the same room.

**What:** Frans Hals, Family Group in a Landscape (Grupo familiar ante un paisaje), 1645–1648, oil on canvas, 202 × 285 cm, inv. 179. Also: Jan Josephsz. van Goyen, Winter Landscape with Figures on Ice (Paisaje invernal con figuras en el hielo), 1643, oil on panel, inv. 167.

Why does a family portrait hang in a landscape room? The answer is on the right-hand side of this canvas, and it may not be by Frans Hals at all.

You're entering the Dutch seventeenth century. A small, rich republic, with no king and a church that wanted no altarpieces, bought paintings for its own houses. Faces, interiors, still lifes and, above all, its own flat country under enormous skies. This is the first Dutch room. Next comes Room 24, the long gallery of French and English painting, and the other Dutch rooms, 25 to 28, open off it.

Hals painted this huge Family Group between 1645 and 1648. A husband and wife hold hands, an old emblem of a faithful marriage, and the dog beside their little girl stands for loyalty. Step close to the clothes and you'll see Hals's quick, fluid strokes. The faces are smoother and more careful.

Now the landscape on the right. The Hals scholar Seymour Slive suggested that another artist painted it, the landscape specialist Pieter de Molijn. Hals did the people. A colleague did the view.

For a landscape that's all Dutch, find van Goyen's small winter scene of 1643. The river at Dordrecht has frozen solid. People haul goods across the ice, a man kneels to fish, and boys with long poles are getting a game going.

Next, Room 24, the long gallery of 18th-century French and English painting: look for Fragonard's children playing on a see-saw in a wood.

### 024 · Room 24 · Jean-Honoré Fragonard — The See-Saw
*Room 24 · 304 words*

**Where:** Room 24, the long gallery of "18th Century. French and English Painting". Look for an upright canvas, just over a metre high, of children playing in a leafy wood, with a girl flying up on a see-saw. Boucher's small, cluttered boudoir scene and Gainsborough's young woman in pale blue in a garden are in the same gallery.

**What:** Jean-Honoré Fragonard, The See-Saw (El columpio), ca. 1750–1752, oil on canvas, 120 × 94.5 cm, inv. 148. Also: François Boucher, La Toilette, 1742, oil on canvas, inv. 58; Thomas Gainsborough, Portrait of Sarah Buxton, ca. 1776–1777, oil on canvas, 110 × 87 cm, inv. 153.

Fragonard's most famous painting shows a young woman flying high on a swing. It's in London. This canvas is one of the first steps towards it.

He painted The See-Saw around 1750, when he was still a pupil in François Boucher's studio and had not yet been to Italy. Four children play in a wood. A girl swings up through the air, and the whole picture tilts along the diagonal of her movement. Leaves and branches spill across the canvas. The brush moves as fast as the game.

Here's the thread. This canvas and its pair, a game of blind man's buff now in Toledo, Ohio, belonged to a Paris collector, the Baron de Saint-Julien. The famous Swing in the Wallace Collection, painted some fifteen years later, belonged to him too. The pair was split up in America, and Baron Thyssen bought this half in the 1950s.

Now look along this long gallery. It's the French and English eighteenth century, running between the Dutch rooms, so you'll cross it more than once.

Boucher, Fragonard's teacher, painted La Toilette for the Swedish ambassador in Paris. A young woman ties her garter while her maid holds up a cap. There's a gold Chinese screen, and a cat plays with a loose thread. The museum calls it an invaluable document of how French rooms really looked.

And Gainsborough's Sarah Buxton sits calmly in a garden, in a pale blue gown, painted just before her marriage. What you see is a fragment. The original canvas was cut up after a fire, and another piece shows a dog at her feet. Gainsborough earned his living with portraits, but landscape was his real vocation, and you can feel it in those trees.

Stay in Room 24: Louise Moillon's still life of fruit, painted more than a century before Fragonard's See-Saw.

### 025 · Room 24 · Louise Moillon — Still-Life with Fruit
*Room 24 · 338 words*

**Where:** Room 24, the long gallery of 18th-century French and English painting. Look for a wide still life, about a metre across, of spring fruit and vegetables on a table under a canopy of leaves: a Chinese bowl of strawberries on the left, artichokes and asparagus behind, a basket of apricots on a dish propped on two bricks at the right.

**What:** Louise Moillon, Still-Life with Fruit (Bodegón con frutas), ca. 1637, oil on canvas, 87.5 × 112 cm, Carmen Thyssen Collection, CTB.1956.12.

At the front of this table lie three halved apricots, and one of them still holds its stone. It's the kind of detail that made Louise Moillon one of the finest still-life painters in seventeenth-century France.

This picture, from around 1637, is older than almost everything else in this eighteenth-century gallery. But she deserves a stop of her own.

Moillon was born in Paris in 1610, into a Protestant family of painters and picture dealers. Her father ran a shop selling paintings in Saint-Germain-des-Prés, and after his death her stepfather, also a painter and dealer, went on training her.

Now read the table from left to right. A Chinese bowl heaped with strawberries, with a loose stalk and some redcurrants beside it. Behind, three artichokes and two bunches of asparagus. On the right, a dish propped up on two rough bricks holds a basket of apricots, strawberries and cherries. Above everything arches a canopy of leaves, and she has painted both sides of them, the glossy front and the paler back. The light comes from the left and lands on the apricots.

Her earliest pictures are much simpler. The table is seen from above, every fruit sits apart, and the light is flat. Here things overlap naturally, the colour is richer, and the light is focused. The museum's catalogue calls this painting a key work in that change. It's also honest enough to say that her later dated works decline. Which makes this one, from her best years, all the more precious.

People love to find sermons in still lifes. A fly for corruption, strawberries for the Resurrection. For this picture, the catalogue suggests there is probably no hidden message at all. Just spring, set out on a table by a painter who knew exactly what she was doing.

### 026 · Room 24 · Extra · Jean Antoine Watteau — Pierrot Content
*Room 24 · 201 words*

**Where:** Room 24. Look for one of the smallest pictures in the gallery, about 35 centimetres high: a garden corner under dark trees, with Pierrot in white sitting between two women and two men, one woman playing a guitar.

**What:** Jean Antoine Watteau, Pierrot Content (Pierrot contento), ca. 1712, oil on canvas, 35 × 31 cm, inv. 432.

It's one of the smallest paintings in this gallery, and it travels further than you'd think.

Watteau painted Pierrot Content around 1712. He had learned the world of the Italian comedy in Paris, from his master Claude Gillot. In a corner of a garden, under dense trees and a statue of Pan, Pierrot sits in the middle between two men and two women. One woman plays a guitar, the other holds a fan, and the man on the left is dressed as Mezzetin, another stock character. Nobody quite looks at anybody. Everyone seems lost in thought.

Look into the dark leaves behind them. An engraving made in 1728 shows two more figures peeping out of the trees. They're still there, but the paint has darkened and they have almost vanished. The canvas has also been cut down on both sides.

More than two and a half centuries later, Lucian Freud chose this little picture for the background of his portrait of Baron Thyssen. It also inspired a large painting of his own. You'll meet the Baron in Room 49, on Level 1.

Room 25 opens off this gallery: Maes's Naughty Drummer, a mother raising a small rod at her drumming son.

### 027 · Room 25 · Nicolaes Maes — The Naughty Drummer
*Room 25 · 305 words*

**Where:** Room 25, "17th Century. Dutch Painting. Scenes of Daily Life and Interiors". Look for a small, almost square interior, about 60 centimetres across: a mother by the window with sewing on her lap, raising a little rod at a boy with a drum, a baby asleep in a wicker cradle. Kalf's still life with a Chinese bowl and a nautilus cup, and Steen's self-portrait with a lute, are in the same room.

**What:** Nicolaes Maes, The Naughty Drummer (El tamborilero desobediente), ca. 1655, oil on canvas, 62 × 66.4 cm, inv. 241. Also: Willem Kalf, Still Life with a Chinese Bowl, Nautilus Cup and Other Objects, 1662, oil on canvas, inv. 203; Jan Havicksz. Steen, Self-Portrait playing the Lute, ca. 1663–1665, oil on panel, inv. 373.

Every parent knows this moment. The baby has finally gone to sleep, and then somebody starts banging a drum.

Nicolaes Maes painted it around 1655, soon after leaving Rembrandt's studio in Amsterdam. A mother sits by the window with her sewing on her lap. Her little boy is drumming, the baby lies asleep in its wicker cradle, and she raises a small rod at him. Light falls in from the left, warm on her reds and on the rug over the table.

There's a mirror on the wall, and a man in it. In 1819 a Scottish visitor described it as The Artist's Family. On that reading, the man in the mirror is Maes, the mother his wife, the boy her son from an earlier marriage, and the baby their daughter. Much later, one scholar read it as an allegory of three kinds of life. The thoughtful man in the mirror, the active mother, the pleasure-seeking boy. Another saw a political message about Holland at the time. The museum says these readings are not mutually exclusive.

It's also, quite simply, a lesson in bringing up children, which Dutch moralists of the day counted among a mother's duties.

In this room, Willem Kalf's still life of 1662 is the Dutch Golden Age on one tabletop. A late Ming bowl from China, a carpet from Herat, and a nautilus shell set in silver gilt. Look at the very top of the cup. A sea monster is about to swallow a tiny figure, either Jonah or Ulysses.

And Jan Steen, the painter people compare to Molière, has painted himself with a lute, in an actor's old-fashioned costume, with a satisfied air and an ironic look. He's inviting you to the party.

Next, Room 26: Saenredam's view of a church front in Utrecht, with three tiny figures for scale.

### 028 · Room 26 · Pieter Jansz. Saenredam — The West Façade of the Church of Saint Mary in Utrecht
*Room 26 · 277 words*

**Where:** Room 26, "17th Century. Dutch Painting. Landscape". Look for a pale, upright panel, about 65 centimetres high, of a plain Romanesque church front among trees, with three tiny figures at its foot. Cuyp's golden sunset with a cowherd and a wooden bridge is in the same room.

**What:** Pieter Jansz. Saenredam, The West Façade of the Church of Saint Mary in Utrecht, 1662, oil on panel, 65.1 × 51.2 cm, inv. 362. Also: Aelbert Jacobsz. Cuyp, Landscape with a Sunset, ca. 1655, oil on panel, 48.3 × 74.9 cm, inv. 117.

This church no longer exists. And the painting doesn't show it quite as it was, either.

Pieter Saenredam was the first painter to make true portraits of real buildings. A biography published in his lifetime called him "the first architectural portraitist". He measured on site, drew, went home, made careful construction drawings, and then sometimes waited years before he painted.

That's what happened here. In the summer of 1636 he spent four months in Utrecht, drawing its churches. His drawing of this one, the Romanesque church of Saint Mary, is dated the thirtieth of August. He painted this panel twenty-six years later. The church itself was demolished in the nineteenth century.

Now compare it with reality, because he improved it. At each end of the façade there used to be a ruined structure. He took both away. He changed the size of the round window in the middle. And at the foot of the building he added three small figures. Find them. They give you the scale, and they make that pale stone feel enormous.

Until 10 January 2027, the visiting Mauritshuis exhibition in the Classical rooms includes another Saenredam of this same church.

Cuyp probably never went to Italy. He learned that golden Mediterranean light from painters who had, and British collectors in the eighteenth century couldn't get enough of it.

### 029 · Room 27 · Rembrandt — Self-portrait wearing a hat and two Chains
*Room 27 · 304 words*

**Where:** Room 27, "17th Century. Dutch Painting. Portrait". Look for a dark panel, about 70 centimetres high, of a man almost face on in a dark hat and dark clothes, with a white shirt and two gold chains as the only bright notes. De Keyser's small portrait of two women and a boy with a watch is in the same room.

**What:** Rembrandt Harmensz. van Rijn, Self-portrait wearing a hat and two Chains (Autorretrato con gorra y dos cadenas), ca. 1642–1643, oil on panel, 72 × 54.8 cm, inv. 331. Also: Thomas Hendricksz. de Keyser, Portrait of Two Women and a Boy, 1632, oil on panel, 70.2 × 50.2 cm, inv. 209.

For years, some experts doubted that Rembrandt painted this self-portrait. In the end, part of the answer came from the tree.

He painted himself here around 1642, the year his wife Saskia died. He's almost face on, in a dark hat and dark clothes. The only bright notes are the white of his shirt and the two gold chains across his chest. Chains like these were honours that princes gave. As far as we know, Rembrandt never received one. He liked to dress up in them anyway.

The doubts were real. The panel was in poor condition, with old repainting and losses, and it has been cut down on all four sides. One scholar thought it might be a copy made by someone in Rembrandt's workshop. So the wood itself was examined. Tree-ring dating placed it in the 1630s or early 1640s, and later tests matched it to the wood of four other Rembrandt panels. The Rembrandt Research Project found no reason to doubt it. And in 2006 the Rembrandt House in Amsterdam showed it as his, without reservation.

Now look at the face, coming out of the shadow under the hat. The critic Kenneth Clark called Rembrandt's self-portraits an autobiography. This is a chapter from the middle. Successful, watchful, a little guarded.

Before Rembrandt arrived in Amsterdam, the city's most celebrated portraitist was Thomas de Keyser. In his small painting of two women and a boy, from ten years earlier, the older woman shows you a watch, and the boy points at it. Beside the curtain, a chair stands empty. It may be about time passing. It may be about a father who is no longer there.

Two portraits, ten years apart. And in both, perhaps, someone is missing.

### 030 · Room 28 · Jacob van Ruisdael — View of Naarden, and Stormy Sea with Sailing Vessels
*Room 28 · 317 words*

**Where:** Room 28, "17th Century. Dutch Painting. Landscape". Start with the small, wide panel, under 70 centimetres across, of flat fields and a distant town under a sky of cloud bands. The stormy sea, with a narrow strip of grey water under a towering sky, and the view of a road through ripe wheat are by the same painter, in the same room.

**What:** Jacob Isaacksz. van Ruisdael, View of Naarden, 1647, oil on panel, 34.8 × 67 cm, inv. 354; Stormy Sea with Sailing Vessels (Mar tormentoso con barcos de vela), ca. 1668, oil on canvas, 50.1 × 62.5 cm, inv. 359. Also: Road trough Grain Fields near the Zuider Zee (official title spelling), ca. 1660–1662, oil on canvas, inv. 357.

Jacob van Ruisdael was eighteen, perhaps nineteen, when he signed and dated this little panel in 1647. And he was painting home ground. His grandfather had moved to Naarden, and his father and uncle were born there.

Look at the proportions. The panel is small, but the view is panoramic. He drops the horizon low, so the sky takes more than half the picture, in bands of cloud and blue. Light breaks through one cloud and runs across the fields in stripes of sun and shadow. Naarden sits in the middle, the Zuiderzee opens on the right, and far off stand the ruins of a church at Muiderberg.

Now find the sea painting, made about twenty years later. He has pushed the idea as far as it will go. The sky fills about three quarters of the canvas, and the sea is a narrow strip. Wind fills the slanting sails. A great S-shaped shadow of cloud falls across the biggest ship, and on the right, white foam breaks around a buoy.

This canvas was in London from 1817, and it was sold there twice. One scholar noticed how closely it resembles Turner's Port Ruysdael, a picture whose very name salutes the Dutchman. Turner might have seen it. Nobody can prove that he did.

The third Ruisdael here is quieter, from the early 1660s. A road winds through ripe wheat, with cattle and walkers, towards a village in the trees. It's thought to be near Naarden again, with the Zuiderzee in the distance.

From the family town to the open water, the real subject never changed. It was always the sky.

## Level 2 · Goya's century without Goya

### 031 · Room 29 · Caspar David Friedrich — Easter Morning
*Room 29 · 247 words*

**Where:** Room 29, "19th Century. European Painting. Goya and Romanticism". Look for a small upright canvas, under half a metre high, of three women on a steep, winding path at dawn, with snow on the ground, old trees and a pale moon high in the sky. Delacroix's two small paintings are in the same room.

**What:** Caspar David Friedrich, Easter Morning (Mañana de Pascua), ca. 1828–1835, oil on canvas, 43.7 × 34.4 cm, inv. 792.

It's very early on Easter morning, and three women are walking to the cemetery.

You've reached the last room on this floor, and the start of the nineteenth century. Around 1800, revolution and war shook Europe, and many painters turned inward, to feeling, nature and the self. The room's title promises Goya, but this season its Goyas are off display.

Caspar David Friedrich painted Easter Morning late in his career, before a stroke in 1835 largely ended his work in oils. It's small and very still. You stand high on a steep path that winds away and vanishes round a bend, in front of a dark wood. Snow still lies on the ground. The three women walk stiffly, and further along, at the bends, more women stand as if waiting.

Look up. The moon is still high, but it no longer lights anything. It casts no shadows. The cold light of dawn is spreading along the horizon instead. Now look at the old trees by the road. They're putting out new shoots, and green stalks have come through the winter in the fields.

There's no angel and no empty tomb. Friedrich paints the Resurrection with a landscape. Night giving way to morning, and winter to spring.

*Room 29 · 190 words*

**Where:** Room 29. Look for a small upright panel, about 35 centimetres high, of a man resting on the ground by a fire with a restless black horse standing behind him. Delacroix's other small painting here shows a duke lifting a sheet beside a curtained bed.

In 1832, Delacroix spent six months travelling with a French diplomatic mission to Morocco. He made sketch after sketch, and he lived off that journey for the rest of his career.

This little panel came more than twenty years later. On the twenty-third of April 1854 he noted in his diary "a small Arab seated and his horse behind him". That's what you see. A man rests on the ground by a fire, a sword at his belt. Behind him stands a fiery black horse with a long mane. Delacroix loved horses for their energy and spirit, and he cared less about describing them than about making you feel them.

The other Delacroix in this room is much earlier, and much more scandalous. A medieval duke lifts a sheet to show a visitor the body of his lover, keeping her face hidden. The visitor is her husband. He doesn't recognise her.

## Level 1 · America and the Impressionists

### 033 · Room 30 · Circle of Sir Joshua Reynolds (?) — Portrait of a Man from the Island of Dominica (?)
*Room 30 · 309 words*

**Where:** Room 30, "18th and 19th Century. Transatlantic relations", beside the lift and stairs. Look for a bust portrait, under a metre high, of a Black man in white, wearing an unusual hat, set in a painted oval. Copley's portrait of a Boston merchant's wife in pink, with pearls and a blue shawl, is in the same room.

**What:** Circle of Sir Joshua Reynolds (?), Portrait of a Man from the Island of Dominica (?), ca. 1770–1780, oil on canvas, 76 × 63.5 cm, inv. 383. Also: John Singleton Copley, Portrait of Mrs. Joshua Henshaw II (Catherine Hill), ca. 1772, oil on canvas, 77 × 56 cm, inv. 97.

For about forty years, this portrait was published as George Washington's cook, painted by Gilbert Stuart. It isn't by Stuart, and he wasn't Washington's cook.

Welcome to Level 1. The walls turn white here, and the story moves into the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. It begins in America, thanks to Baron Hans Heinrich. As a boy he devoured Karl May's novels of the Wild West, and in 1979 he started buying nineteenth-century American painting, when hardly any European collector did. One of the museum's own writers calls him a rara avis, a rare bird, in European collecting. Today the museum calls this collection an essential point of reference in Europe.

He may have been a servant in a wealthy British household. He may have been a visitor from Africa. The best clue is the hat. Its closest match is worn by a free West Indian, in a painting of the island of Dominica made a few years earlier. If he was from Dominica, he may have come to England just before the French took the island in 1778.

The painter is unknown too. The broad strokes follow the manner of Sir Joshua Reynolds, and the hand is clearly talented. His catalogue entry admits that he may always remain partly a mystery. So look at him properly. A confident, beautifully painted face, and a name we've lost.

Nearby is John Singleton Copley, Boston's leading portraitist on the eve of the American Revolution. He painted Catherine Hill, a merchant's wife, in pink and pearls, with a direct, calm gaze.

### 034 · Room 31 · Frederic Edwin Church — Cross in the Wilderness
*Room 31 · 285 words*

**Where:** Room 31, "19th Century. American Landscape and Environmental Awareness". Look for a small, wide canvas, about 60 centimetres across, of a lone cross hung with garlands in a rocky landscape, a waterfall and lake to its right, under a vast evening sky. Thomas Cole's larger, darker scene of a stone bridge and glowing arch over an abyss is in the same room.

**What:** Frederic Edwin Church, Cross in the Wilderness, 1857, oil on canvas, 41.3 × 61.5 cm, inv. 508. Also: Thomas Cole, Expulsion. Moon and Firelight (Expulsión. Luna y luz de fuego), ca. 1828, oil on canvas, 91.4 × 122 cm, inv. 95.

A family had lost a child. They asked Frederic Church for a painting in memory, and in 1857 he gave them this.

There's no portrait and no angel. In the foreground stands a simple cross, hung with garlands. To its right, a waterfall drops into a still lake beneath a great wall of rock. The far peaks have almost dissolved, and over everything spreads a vast evening sky.

Church had travelled through Ecuador and Colombia, and he was about to go back. In the Andes, travellers described places along the road where the dead were laid down, each one marked with a simple cross of twigs. A scholar has linked this cross to those places. Church came to know that grief himself. Eight years later, two of his own young children died of diphtheria, and he painted two small pictures on this same theme.

He had learned his art from Thomas Cole, the painter usually called the founder of the Hudson River School, America's first real fellowship of artists. Cole's Expulsion, here in the same room, is a stage set. A stone bridge over an abyss, a waterfall, a volcano, the moon. It shows Adam and Eve being driven out of Paradise, yet neither of them appears. Eden simply glows through the arch on the right.

Cole worried about what rapid industry was doing to America's wild places. He wrote: "We are still in Eden. The wall that shuts us out of the garden is our own ignorance and folly." That's why this room is called American Landscape and Environmental Awareness. The wilderness here is beautiful, and it's also something that can be lost.

### 035 · Room 32 · John Singer Sargent — Portrait of Millicent, Duchess of Sutherland
*Room 32 · 335 words*

**Where:** Room 32, "19th Century. American Landscape and Urban Life". Stand before the tall full-length portrait, two and a half metres high, of a red-haired woman in a flowered green dress in a shaded garden, one hand on a fountain. Harnett's small trompe-l'oeil table with a pipe and a newspaper is in the same room.

**What:** John Singer Sargent, Portrait of Millicent, Duchess of Sutherland, 1904, oil on canvas, 254 × 146 cm, inv. 732. Also: William Michael Harnett, Materials for a Leisure Hour (Objetos para un rato de ocio), 1879, oil on canvas, 38 × 51.5 cm, inv. 574.

She married at seventeen. By the time John Singer Sargent painted her, in 1904, Millicent, Duchess of Sutherland, was thirty-seven and one of the great hostesses of her day. She ran Stafford House in London, across from Buckingham Palace, and Dunrobin Castle in Scotland.

That was only half of her. She was also a writer and a tireless charity worker for the poorest people of her time. And ten years later, when the First World War broke out, she went to work as a nurse, and France gave her the Croix de Guerre.

By then Sargent was the portraitist the British aristocracy wanted. He had left Paris after the scandal over his Madame X, settled in London, and learned to paint in the grand manner of Van Dyck and Reynolds.

Look at how he sets her. She stands full length in a shaded garden, in a green dress with a pattern of flowers, one hand resting on a fountain. On her red hair there's a tiara of laurel leaves, as if she had just stepped out of the trees. The pose borrows from Reynolds, who liked to paint duchesses as classical goddesses.

When it was shown at the Royal Academy that year, the critic Roger Fry didn't like it. Others compared it to Gainsborough and Reynolds. And the American writer Henry Adams, who saw it in Paris, wrote: "Never did any artist paint such psychology as Sargent."

Before you leave, lean in to the small still life by William Harnett, from 1879. A pipe is still smouldering on the edge of the table, among spent matches and a half-eaten biscuit. The folded newspaper even shows its date. It's a vanitas, a reminder that life is short, made not with skulls but with the things someone leaves behind when they step out of the room. One writer called the effect a magic palpability. You may want to brush the crumbs away.

### 036 · Room 33 · Berthe Morisot — The Psyche mirror
*Room 33 · 315 words*

**Where:** Room 33, "The Impressionist Period". Stand in front of the medium-sized canvas of a half-dressed young woman standing in her bedroom before a tall, tilting mirror on a stand.

**What:** Berthe Morisot, The Psyche mirror (El espejo psiqué), 1876, oil on canvas, 65 × 54 cm. Also: Édouard Manet, Horsewoman, Full-Face (L'Amazone), about 1882, oil on canvas, and Edgar Degas, Swaying Dancer (Dancer in Green), 1877–1879, pastel and gouache on paper, both in Room 33.

In the spring of 1874, a Paris critic walked round an exhibition by a group of young painters and went home sharpening his pen. One picture, by Claude Monet, was called Impression. The critic, Louis Leroy, borrowed the word for the title of a biting article in the satirical paper Le Charivari. A few days later another critic used the same word as praise, and the name stuck.

Berthe Morisot was one of the founders of that group. She showed with them almost every time they exhibited, alongside Monet, Renoir and Degas.

Her painting here is from 1876. A young woman stands in her bedroom, half dressed, in front of a tall mirror that tilts on its stand. The French call that kind of mirror a psyché. The title plays on both meanings: a piece of furniture, and Psyche, the girl loved by Eros in the old myth. The mirror was Morisot's own. Now look at the whites and soft greys of the fabrics. When the painting was shown at the group's third exhibition, Émile Zola praised exactly those greys and whites.

Two men in this room complete the picture. Édouard Manet, the older rebel, never exhibited with the group. His horsewoman looks straight out at you, but she is no rider. The model was a bookseller's daughter, and the riding habit was borrowed from a painter friend. Manet meant the picture for the Salon of 1883. He died that spring, and it stayed unfinished.

And then Degas. His dancer in green spins on one leg while the others are cut off by the edge, as if you were watching through opera glasses from a high side box. One of its first owners was the British painter Walter Sickert.

### 037 · Room 33 · Extra · Camille Pissarro — Rue Saint-Honoré in the Afternoon. Effect of Rain
*Room 33 · 216 words*

**Where:** Room 33, "The Impressionist Period". Look for the upright canvas of a long Paris street seen from high above as the rain stops, with horse-drawn carriages, a few umbrellas and the corner of a square in the foreground.

**What:** Camille Pissarro, Rue Saint-Honoré in the Afternoon. Effect of Rain, 1897, oil on canvas, 81 × 65 cm.

Camille Pissarro spent most of his life in the countryside, painting landscapes. Then, late in life and for the sake of his health, he moved into the city and started painting out of windows.

In the winter of 1897, he took a hotel room on a square in the heart of Paris. His dealer, Durand-Ruel, promised to show whatever came of it. Pissarro painted every view the room could give him: the avenue de l'Opéra, the square beside the hotel, and this street, the rue Saint-Honoré. He made fifteen pictures that winter.

He painted this exact view three times, in three kinds of light. One has bright morning sun. One has the dim light of dusk. This one is early afternoon, and the rain is only just stopping. Look at the street, the carriages, and the people picked out one by one, some still holding their umbrellas. You are looking down at them from high up, the way Monet had looked down on a Paris boulevard back at the first Impressionist exhibition.

Pissarro knew some people found these streets ugly. He wrote that perhaps it was not aesthetic, but he was delighted to paint them anyway.

## Level 1 · Expressionism and the avant-gardes

### 038 · Room 34 · Vincent van Gogh — The Stevedores in Arles
*Room 34 · 275 words*

**Where:** Room 34, "Expressionist Landscapes". Find the small horizontal canvas of dark boats and tiny figures against a blazing orange sunset over a river.

**What:** Vincent van Gogh, The Stevedores in Arles, 1888, oil on canvas, 54 × 65 cm. Also: André Derain, Waterloo Bridge, 1906, oil on canvas, Room 34.

In 1961, Baron Hans Heinrich Thyssen-Bornemisza wanted to leave his own mark on a family collection built on old masters. So he bought modern art for the first time: German Expressionists, Kirchner, Nolde, Heckel and their friends. These rooms grew out of that decision, and from here on the walls get louder.

This room is called "Expressionist Landscapes". And it starts with the painter many of them saw as a forerunner, Vincent van Gogh.

He arrived in Arles, in the south of France, in February 1888, looking for light. That August he wrote to his brother Theo about an evening by the Rhône. A big boat loaded with coal was moored at the quay. Workmen in dirty blue and white carried the cargo ashore. The sky was lilac with an orange streak in the west, and the town was violet. "It was pure Hokusai," he wrote.

That evening became three paintings. This is one of the two sunset versions, painted later from memory in his studio. Look at the dark boats and the tiny workers standing out against that hot orange air. The black of the coal, and the coming night.

Now find the large London view nearby, Derain's Waterloo Bridge. A Paris dealer bought everything Derain painted and sent him to London three times, to answer Monet's famous views of the Thames. Derain answered in pure colour. He called colours "cartridges of dynamite whose purpose is to discharge light."

One more Van Gogh is on show today, painted years before Arles. You will meet it on Level 0, in Room C.

### 039 · Room 35 · Gabriele Münter — Self-portrait
*Room 35 · 324 words*

**Where:** Room 35, "Expressionist Portraits". Find the small upright picture on cardboard of a woman with a serious, inward look, her face built of thick brushstrokes and the rest barely sketched.

**What:** Gabriele Münter, Self-portrait, about 1909–1910, oil on cardboard, 49 × 33.6 cm. Also: Ernst Ludwig Kirchner, Fränzi in front of Carved Chair, 1910, oil on canvas, and Doris with Ruff Collar, about 1906, oil on cardboard, both Room 35.

Gabriele Münter painted herself at least sixteen times in oil. This is one of them, from around 1910. And she never let the public see it. She never exhibited it, and it stayed in her studio until her death, more than fifty years later.

At the time, Münter was at the heart of the avant-garde in Munich, and within two years she would help found the group called the Blue Rider. Her portraits of friends from those years are bold and flat, with thick dark outlines. This one is different. The brushwork is rough and textured, light and shadow are strong, and almost everything except the face is only sketched in. Look at the neckline of her smock, pulled off-centre, so that the whole picture tips slightly off balance.

She once wrote that painting a portrait is "the boldest and the most difficult, the most spiritual, the most extreme task of the artist." Here she sets herself that task in private. She is not showing off as a painter. She is looking for her own soul.

Now the two Kirchners in this room. They were among the very first modern paintings the Baron bought, and they arrived together. Doris, in a high ruff collar, was Kirchner's companion in Dresden. Late in life Kirchner gave it a date years too early, which scholars say is impossible.

The other is Fränzi, a girl of about twelve from a working-class district of Dresden. Her face is yellow-green, her lips deep red, and she stares straight at you. Behind her is the mural of Kirchner's studio, nudes on a deep green ground. The chair she leans on is carved in the shape of a woman, most likely by Kirchner himself.

### 040 · Room 36 · Extra · James Ensor — Theatre of Masks
*Room 36 · 225 words*

**Where:** Room 36, "The Language of the Body". Look for the horizontal painting of a brightly lit stage crowded with grotesque masked faces, in a gilt frame shaped like a little theatre.

**What:** James Ensor, Theatre of Masks, 1908, oil on canvas, 72 × 86 cm. Also: Mikhail Larionov, Blue Nude, about 1908, oil on canvas, Room 36.

James Ensor grew up surrounded by masks. His parents kept a shop in Ostend, on the Belgian coast, and every year they sold masks for the town's carnival.

He never let them go. Masks run through his whole career, sometimes funny, sometimes diabolic. He used them to say something about modern life: the loneliness of the individual in the new society of the masses. Look at this stage. The actors under the lights wear masks, and so does the audience, and the two crowds melt into one. Who is performing, and who is watching?

Even the frame joins in. It is gilded and shaped like a stage, and according to a previous owner it was made to Ensor's own instructions. A Belgian poet compared these masked theatres to Watteau's elegant parties, like the one upstairs in Room 24.

In the same room, Mikhail Larionov's Blue Nude was a young Russian's answer to the Gauguins he saw in a Moscow collector's house. His companion, later his wife, was the painter Natalia Goncharova. You will meet her work on Level 0.

### 041 · Room 37 · George Grosz — Metropolis
*Room 37 · 313 words*

**Where:** Room 37, "Urban unrest". Stand in front of the square canvas that burns red from edge to edge: a crowded city street under a blazing, unreal sun.

**What:** George Grosz, Metropolis, 1916–1917, oil on canvas, 100 × 102 cm. Also: Wassily Kandinsky, Murnau: Top of the Johannisstrasse, 1908, oil on cardboard, Room 37.

In 1916, in the middle of a war against Britain and France, a young Berlin painter called Georg Gross decided to sound American. He added an E to his first name, so that Georg became the English George. And Gross, spelled with a double S, became Grosz, with an S and a Z.

He started this picture that December, in wartime Berlin, and finished it the following summer. The army called him up again halfway through, and for months the canvas waited. Look at what he came back to. A city street crammed with people, figures and buildings overlapping, everything soaked in red, and over it all a blazing, unreal sun. Grosz borrowed the broken planes of Cubism and the speed of Futurism, but not their optimism. This is a city heading for self-destruction. The writer Walter Mehring gave it a second title, Reminiscences of the Entrance to Manhattan, because many of Grosz's drawings for it imagined New York.

Then the painting's own story begins. In the mid-1920s a German museum in Mannheim bought it. Under the Nazis it was hung in their exhibition of so-called degenerate art and then sold off through a gallery in Lucerne, in Switzerland. A German dealer who had emigrated to New York bought it, and in the end Grosz bought his own painting back. Only later did it come to this collection.

Now find a much smaller, brighter picture in this room: Kandinsky's Murnau, a village street in Bavaria from 1908. On the right is the inn where he and Gabriele Münter always stayed. When the First World War sent Kandinsky back to Russia, Münter kept this painting. It is Kandinsky before abstraction, still looking at houses and roofs.

### 042 · Room 38 · Extra · Henri Matisse — The Yellow Flowers
*Room 38 · 202 words*

**Where:** Room 38, "Flowers", a small room. Look for the modest horizontal still life in muted tones: a vase of yellow flowers, with its reflection glowing in a mirror behind it.

**What:** Henri Matisse, The Yellow Flowers (Las flores amarillas), 1902, oil on canvas, 46 × 54.5 cm. Also: Max Beckmann, Still life with Yellow Roses, 1937, oil on canvas, Room 38.

In 1902, Henri Matisse was having a terrible year. His wife's parents, who had been his main financial support, went bankrupt in a public scandal. His painting turned sombre, and he made a series of modest flower pieces, what his biographer called small, inexpensive bunches of chrysanthemums, yellow ranunculus or ragged sunflowers.

This is one of them. The colours are muted, almost drab. But look at the mirror. The reflection of the yellow flowers glows with an energy the rest of the picture holds back. Within a few years Matisse would be painting in the pure, blazing colour of the Fauves, and you can feel it starting here.

Now find the tall still life of yellow roses nearby. Max Beckmann painted it in 1937 in Amsterdam. He had left Berlin that July, the week the Nazis opened their exhibition mocking modern art as "degenerate". In exile he painted big allegorical pictures about the horror of the regime. And alongside them, as a kind of relief, still lifes like this one, with no politics at all. Follow the yellow strips up from the roses to the ears of corn in the corner.

### 043 · Room 39 · Wassily Kandinsky — In the Bright Oval
*Room 39 · 291 words*

**Where:** Room 39, "Pioneers of abstraction". Stand in front of the upright painting on cardboard with a large bright oval and geometric shapes, lines and points of colour.

**What:** Wassily Kandinsky, In the Bright Oval (En el óvalo claro), 1925, oil on cardboard, 73 × 59 cm. Also: František Kupka, Localization of Graphic Mobiles I, 1912–1913, oil on canvas, 200 × 194 cm, Room 39.

Seventeen years separate Kandinsky's little Murnau street, back in Room 37, from this painting. Same artist. And not a single house left.

By 1925, Wassily Kandinsky was teaching at the Bauhaus, the German school of art and design, which had just moved from Weimar to the town of Dessau. That year he finished a book with a wonderfully dry title, Point and Line to Plane. In it he treated form and colour as things in their own right, with no need to describe anything. He even taught his students to match the three primary colours to three shapes: yellow to the triangle, red to the square, blue to the circle.

Now look at the painting: the bright oval of the title, and around it shapes, lines and points of colour. It could almost be a demonstration from the classroom. But the museum's text warns against reading it that way. This, it argues, is not an experiment with geometry. Like his earlier work, it carries a hidden cosmic meaning, a small universe of its own.

Now turn to the huge, almost square canvas in this room, by the Czech painter František Kupka. It is two metres high. Kupka, like Kandinsky, believed painting could work like music, with no subject at all. He worked on two versions of this composition through the winter of 1912, and both were shown in Paris the following autumn, at the Salon d'Automne of 1913. Who painted the very first abstract picture is still argued about, and Kupka is often named among the pioneers. Stand back and let it turn.

Downstairs in Room G there is one more Kandinsky, from his Munich years. Next, Room 40, "Popular flavor": Chagall's village Madonna, with a flying cow playing the violin.

### 044 · Room 40 · Marc Chagall — The Madonna of the Village
*Room 40 · 317 words*

**Where:** Room 40, "Popular flavor". Stand in front of the large, nearly square canvas of a huge Madonna in a white bridal gown holding the Child, floating among angels, with a flying cow playing the violin.

**What:** Marc Chagall, The Madonna of the Village (La Virgen de la aldea), 1938–1942, oil on canvas, 102.5 × 98 cm. Also: Marc Chagall, The Grey House, 1917, oil on canvas, Room 40.

A cow is flying across this painting, and it is playing the violin. In Marc Chagall's world, that is perfectly normal. What is not normal is the journey this canvas made.

In the late 1930s, as the persecution of Jews in Europe grew into the Holocaust, Chagall, who was Jewish, began painting scenes from the Christian story, among them a Crucifixion. He started this Madonna soon afterwards. Then came the war. Fleeing the German advance, he took refuge in Gordes, a hill town in Provence, and there he went on working on the canvas, repainting parts he had already laid in. He only finished it in 1942, in New York.

Look at her. The Virgin is enormous, dressed not in blue but in a white bridal gown, holding her child, floating near a small village. Angels sing and play around her. And the cow keeps the music going. It is a dream of peace, painted by a man on the run.

Now find The Grey House, a smaller picture from 1917. In the summer of 1914, Chagall went home to Vitebsk, in what is now Belarus, for what was meant to be a short visit. The war and then the Revolution kept him in Russia for eight years. Here is his hometown in grey: a wooden house, a church steeple, wild clouds. To him the town was both sad and gay. On the left a small figure stands with a hand on his chest, possibly Chagall himself. His name is written on the jacket. And on the wooden fence, in Russian, is the word for idiot. Two scholars read it as a private joke: "Chagall is the village idiot."

The poet André Breton said that with Chagall, "the metaphor made its triumphant return into modern painting."

### 045 · Room 41 · Pablo Picasso — Man with a Clarinet, and Georges Braque — Woman with a Mandolin
*Room 41 · 347 words*

**Where:** Room 41, "The Cubist Tradition I". Find the two upright Cubist canvases in browns, ochres and greys: Picasso's pyramid-shaped figure built of splintered planes, and Braque's slightly smaller seated woman whose mandolin and hand you can just make out.

**What:** Pablo Picasso, Man with a Clarinet, 1911–1912, oil on canvas, 106 × 69 cm; Georges Braque, Woman with a Mandolin (Mujer con mandolina), 1910, oil on canvas, 80.5 × 54 cm. Also: Paul Cézanne, Seated Man, 1905–1906, and Juan Gris, The Smoker (Frank Haviland), 1913, both oil on canvas, Room 41.

Before the Cubists, there is Cézanne. His Seated Man, probably his old gardener, was painted in the last years of his life, and parts of the canvas are simply left bare. Cézanne said he wanted to make of Impressionism "something solid and durable, like the art of museums." Picasso and Braque started from there, and this room hangs him at the head of the story.

Now the two partners. Braque painted his Woman with a Mandolin in the spring of 1910. He had been looking at Corot's quiet women with musical instruments, and he understood that an instrument gives a sitter the calm of a still life. The woman has broken into small planes of ochre, grey and brown. Look for the mandolin first, then the hand that holds it. The head and shoulders are almost gone.

Picasso's Man with a Clarinet came a year and a half later, after a summer working beside Braque in Céret, in the French Pyrenees. Find the shape of a pyramid, wide at the bottom. That is the man. The cylinder at the centre is his instrument, perhaps a clarinet, though one scholar thinks it is a tenora, the reed instrument of Catalan sardana bands.

Then the war turned both paintings into enemy property. The Picasso belonged to a German collector in Paris, and the Braque to the German dealer Kahnweiler. France confiscated what they owned, and in 1921 it was sold off at auctions in Paris. The poet André Breton went to the sales. He bought the Braque, and he called the Picasso "a work of fabulous elegance."

One more painter here was born in Madrid: Juan Gris. His Smoker is probably a wealthy American friend, in a top hat and bow tie, painted at Céret two years after the Picasso. Gris used planes of green, blue, orange and red, colours the two inventors were not using at the time. When Kahnweiler's confiscated stock came up for sale, he bought this one back.

Next, Room 42, "The Cubist Tradition II": Sonia Delaunay's Simultaneous Contrasts, a painting made of pure colour.

### 046 · Room 42 · Sonia Delaunay — Simultaneous Contrasts
*Room 42 · 301 words*

**Where:** Room 42, "The Cubist Tradition II". Find the small horizontal canvas made only of curved bands and segments of clashing colour, reds against blues. Robert Delaunay's taller Woman with a Parasol hangs in the same room.

**What:** Sonia Delaunay, Simultaneous Contrasts (Contrastes simultáneos), 1913, oil on canvas, 46 × 55 cm. Also: Robert Delaunay, Woman with a Parasol, 1913, oil on canvas, 121 × 85 cm, and Giacomo Balla, Patriotic Demonstration, 1915, oil on canvas, Room 42.

This painting may once have been hung the wrong way up. Sonia Delaunay signed it as if it were upright. But compared with her other pictures of the same name, it clearly belongs on its side, wider than it is tall.

How does a painter lose track of which way is up? One scholar has a lovely answer. After her marriage to the painter Robert Delaunay, and the birth of their son Charles in 1911, Sonia stopped painting for more than two years. Instead she designed bedspreads, pillows and tablecloths. And a bedspread has no top or bottom. When she came back to the canvas, she kept that freedom.

So try it. Tilt your head and turn the picture upright in your mind. It still works. There is nothing in it to fall over. Just curved bands and segments of colour pushing against each other, red against blue, warm against cold. That clash is the subject. For the Delaunays, colour itself could move, and that movement could build a new kind of space.

Now find Robert's Woman with a Parasol, in the same room. Early in 1913 he took a picture with this title to Berlin, to the Der Sturm gallery, with the poet Guillaume Apollinaire. Apollinaire gave Robert's colour painting a name, Orphism. "Line is limitation," Robert wrote. Look at the woman's dress, broken into coloured patterns, very close to the designs Sonia was making to dress modern women. Both paintings came to the museum from the same collection, husband and wife side by side.

One more story in this room. Balla's crowd of Italian flags caught the eye of the choreographer Léonide Massine, who bought it straight from the painter in 1917 for a Stravinsky ballet in Rome.

### 047 · Room 43 · Liubov Popova — Painterly Architectonic, and Olga Rozanova — Man on the Street (Analysis of Volumes)
*Room 43 · 327 words*

**Where:** Room 43, "Abstract Utopias". Find Popova's small horizontal canvas of overlapping coloured planes, and nearby Rozanova's taller canvas of a figure in the street analysed into geometric volumes.

**What:** Liubov Popova, Painterly Architectonic (Arquitectura pictórica), 1918, oil on canvas, 45 × 53 cm; Olga Rozanova, Man on the Street (Analysis of Volumes), 1913, oil on canvas, 83 × 61.5 cm. Also: El Lissitzky, Proun 1 C, 1919, oil on panel, Room 43.

In 1916, the painter Kazimir Malevich founded a group called Supremus. Among those who joined were two women whose work hangs in this room, Liubov Popova and Olga Rozanova.

None of them followed Malevich to the letter. One historian put it well: his ideas were "more an impulse to seek out their own individual direction within the new art." Popova found hers by joining Malevich's pure floating shapes to the built, constructed geometry of Vladimir Tatlin. The result was a series of Painterly Architectonics.

Look at hers. Planes of colour lie over one another, cut across one another and seem to push forward and back. There is no object here, only colour, light and space, built like architecture. One critic wrote that "the forms vibrate with color and texture, creating a dynamic whole."

Now find Rozanova's Man on the Street, painted five years earlier. Most of the Russian avant-garde travelled around Europe to see the new art for themselves. Rozanova did not. Yet she was one of the first Russians to make friends with the Italian Futurists, and this painting was shown in their exhibition in Rome in the spring of 1914. Her figure in the city is analysed into volumes, just as the subtitle says: French Cubism and Italian Futurism, mixed with a Russian primitivism of her own.

Then there is the square panel by El Lissitzky, painted in 1919 in the provincial town of Vitebsk, where he taught alongside Malevich. He called these works Prouns, an abbreviation of a Russian phrase meaning project for the affirmation of the new. Everything is drawn with ruler and compasses, and seems to float without weight. Lissitzky said a Proun was no longer a picture. It was a building, to be walked around and seen from every side.

### 048 · Room 43 · Extra · Piet Mondrian — New York City, 3 (unfinished)
*Room 43 · 228 words*

**Where:** Room 43, "Abstract Utopias". Look for the nearly square canvas crossed by thin coloured lines, made of strips of adhesive tape rather than paint.

**What:** Piet Mondrian, New York City, 3 (unfinished), 1941, oil, pencil, chalk and coloured tape on canvas, 117 × 110 cm. Also: Piet Mondrian, Composition No. XIII/ Composition 2, 1913, oil on canvas, Room 43.

The lines in this painting are not painted. Lean in and you will see why: they are strips of coloured adhesive tape.

Piet Mondrian had lived in Paris for nearly twenty years. In 1938, as war came closer, he moved to London. Two years later came the air raids on London and the German occupation of Paris. He crossed the Atlantic and settled in New York, where he spent the last years of his life.

New York loosened him up. He loved the city's grid of streets and its towers, which he called "the furthest from nature." He loved jazz and boogie-woogie. In his last studio he worked with tape, because tape can be moved. He shifted the strips again and again, looking for the right balance of line and colour. Only one picture in this group ever had its tape replaced with paint. This one was left as it was, unfinished, and you can still see how he was thinking.

In the same room hangs a very different Mondrian, Composition Number Thirteen, from his first months in Paris before the First World War. It seems to grow out of the branches of a tree. Years later, Mondrian called it a eucalyptus. Between the two, you can follow a painter from a tree to a city.

### 049 · Room 44 · René Magritte — The Key of the Fields (La Clef des champs)
*Room 44 · 263 words*

**Where:** Room 44, "Dada and Surrealism". Stand in front of the upright canvas of a window smashed open onto a landscape, with shards of glass on the floor that still carry pieces of the view.

**What:** René Magritte, The Key of the Fields (La Clef des champs), 1936, oil on canvas, 80 × 60 cm. Also: Kurt Schwitters, Merzbild Kijkduin, 1923, oil, pencil and wood assemblage on cardboard, Room 44.

Something has smashed straight through this window. The glass lies in pieces on the floor. And each piece still shows a bit of the landscape.

This room is "Dada and Surrealism". Dada was born of disgust at the First World War: artists who answered the slaughter with nonsense, chance and rubbish. Surrealism grew out of it in Paris in the 1920s, led by the poet André Breton, and went looking for dreams and the hidden mind.

Now look again at René Magritte's window. If the shards on the floor carry the view, then what you saw through the glass was never the real landscape. It was a picture painted on the window. Magritte wrote to Breton that the essential thing was to wipe out the difference between what you see from outside a window and what you see from inside. The title helps. In French, la clé des champs, the key of the fields, means freedom. Break the glass, and you are out.

The museum's own Dalí dream picture is in the temporary exhibition The Freudian Universe of Dalí, on the ground floor, until 24 January 2027.

Before you leave, find Kurt Schwitters's Merzbild Kijkduin. In 1923, on a Dada tour of Holland, he picked up driftwood and other debris from a beach near The Hague and built this picture out of it. For years it belonged to the artist Hannah Höch, who kept her collection hidden through the Nazi years in a dry well in her garden.

### 050 · Room 45 · Edward Hopper — Hotel Room
*Room 45 · 306 words*

**Where:** Room 45, "Interwar Realisms". Stand in front of the big, almost square canvas, about one and a half metres across, of a woman in her slip sitting on the edge of a hotel bed under hard light, reading a yellowed sheet of paper.

**What:** Edward Hopper, Hotel Room (Habitación de hotel), 1931, oil on canvas, 152.4 × 165.7 cm. Also: Otto Dix, Hugo Erfurth with Dog, 1926, tempera and oil on panel, and Pablo Picasso, Harlequin with a Mirror, 1923, oil on canvas, both Room 45.

Edward Hopper once said, "What I wanted to do was paint sunlight on the side of a house." There is no sunlight here. It is night, the light is electric, and a woman sits alone on the edge of a hotel bed.

She is tired. She has taken off her hat, her dress and her shoes, and she is too exhausted to unpack. In her hands is a yellowed sheet of paper. We know exactly what it is, because Hopper's wife, Jo, kept careful notes on his paintings. It is a train timetable. She is checking the time of tomorrow's train. Jo knew the scene well. She posed for it herself, in their studio on Washington Square.

Hopper painted it in 1931, on a big canvas, just after his first real success. It was the first of many hotel rooms in his work. Now look at how he closes her in. A wall cuts across the front. A chest of drawers blocks the right. The long diagonal of the bed pulls your eye to a half-open window at the back, and outside it there is nothing but black night. And that open window turns you into the intruder, looking in on a stranger's private moment.

Two other portraits in this room keep their secrets too. Otto Dix painted his friend Hugo Erfurth, a famous society photographer, in a tweed jacket with his big dog, but without a single camera. Dix remembered the painter Max Liebermann's advice: "everything we Germans paint is a portrait."

And Picasso's Harlequin holds up a mirror and gazes into it. X-rays show that the face began as Picasso's own. He turned it into a mask, the pale face of Pierrot.

### 051 · Room 45 · Extra · Balthus — The Card Game
*Room 45 · 223 words*

**Where:** Room 45, "Interwar Realisms". Look for the wide canvas, almost two metres across, of a boy and a girl playing cards at a table with a candlestick, in a bare, stark room lit from the right.

**What:** Balthus (Balthazar Klossowski de Rola), The Card Game, 1948–1950, oil on canvas, 140 × 194 cm. Also: Max Beckmann, Quappi in Pink Jumper, 1932–1934, oil on canvas, Room 45.

In the world of Balthus, the girls always win. The boys can try anything, but they usually lose.

Here a boy and a girl sit at a table with a candlestick, playing cards in a bare, stark room. Light comes in coldly from the right. Look at the boy, his body twisted so that you see him partly from the front and partly in profile. He is ready to cheat. And look at the girl's half-hidden smile. It suggests she will win in the end.

Balthus painted it just after the Second World War, when he came back to painting with new energy. He kept away from every modern movement and looked back to the old masters, above all to Piero della Francesca, in the austerity, the geometry and the colour. X-rays show that he changed a good deal as he worked, and even painted out a hat.

Now find Max Beckmann's portrait of his wife, Quappi, in a bright pink jumper and matching hat, a cigarette in her hand. He almost finished it in Frankfurt in 1932. Two years later, the Nazis had driven him from his teaching post. He took it up again in Berlin, changed the date and, according to a friend, made her smile a little less.

### 052 · Room 46 · Jackson Pollock — Brown and Silver I
*Room 46 · 300 words*

**Where:** Room 46, "American Abstraction I". Stand in front of the canvas, about one and a half metres by one, of dark brown lines and shapes on raw canvas, splashed with patches of silver paint.

**What:** Jackson Pollock, Brown and Silver I, about 1951, enamel and silver paint on canvas, 144.7 × 107.9 cm. Also: Arshile Gorky, Last Painting, 1948, oil on canvas, Room 46.

Several of the Europeans in these rooms, Mondrian and Max Ernst among them, ended up in New York, fleeing the war. They found a city where American painters were ready to take the lead, and after the war the centre of modern art moved across the Atlantic.

The most famous of those Americans is Jackson Pollock. From the winter of 1947 he left the easel behind and dripped and poured paint onto his canvases with sticks, spoons and knives. "When I'm in the painting," he said, "I'm not aware of what I'm doing." The drip paintings made his name. And then, at the height of that success, he changed direction. In June 1951 he wrote to friends that he had had "a period of drawing on canvas in black."

This is one of those Black Paintings. Here the black has turned to brown, laid on straight from the tin with sticks and dry brushes, over raw, unprimed canvas. Silver paint is splashed across it. Look for the shapes that seem almost to be figures. The open, all-over web of the drip paintings has gone.

And there is a mystery. A photograph of Pollock's studio taken that same year shows this canvas next to its twin, Brown and Silver II. The two were painted side by side on one long roll of canvas and then cut apart. Neither is signed, so no one can be sure which way up they belong. You may be looking at it sideways. It is also the only Pollock in this collection.

Nearby hangs Arshile Gorky's Last Painting. It was on his easel the day he took his own life, in July 1948. Its other title, The Black Monk, may come from a story by Chekhov.

### 053 · Room 46 · Georgia O'Keeffe — White Iris No. 7
*Room 46 · 339 words*

**Where:** In Room 46, "American Abstraction I", the same room as the Pollock. White Iris No. 7 is an upright canvas about a metre high, one pale flower so close that its petals run off every edge. From the Plains II is the wide canvas nearly two metres across, in bands of flaming red, orange and yellow.

**What:** Georgia O'Keeffe, White Iris No. 7 (Lirio blanco nº 7), 1957, oil on canvas, 102 × 76.2 cm, inv. 697. Also: Georgia O'Keeffe, From the Plains II (Desde las llanuras II), 1954, oil on canvas, 122 × 183 cm, inv. 696. Both Room 46, Level 1.

Georgia O'Keeffe spent much of her life telling people what her flowers were not about. This painting shows you why she had to keep saying it.

It's a white iris, painted in 1957, long after she had left New York for the light of New Mexico. There's no vase here, no stem, no garden. The flower fills the whole canvas and runs off its edges, as if you had pushed your face right into the petals. She seems to have learned that framing from the camera, from the photographers around Alfred Stieglitz in New York. Cropping the bloom so tightly turns it into something close to abstract.

For decades, critics read these close-up flowers as bodies, as sexual images. O'Keeffe always rejected that. In a catalogue text she wrote that people were seeing their own associations in her flowers, not hers. The museum suggests another way in: a meditation on creation, on how the spiritual and the material meet.

So look at it the way she asked. Follow one petal until the edge of the canvas cuts it off. You never get the whole flower. You only get what's in front of your eyes.

Now turn to the wide canvas of red, orange and yellow. It began as a memory of Texas, where she had watched cattle driven across the dry plains, kicking up dust and making a deafening noise. She painted it first thirty-five years earlier, as an upright picture. In 1954 she made it again, laid flat and wide, in the flaming colours of a sunset. Writing about this picture to the gallery owner Edith Halpert, she said the colour was "just plain color out of the tube", red and orange to lemon. "It shocks me so that I'm rather struck with it," she added.

A flower, and a plain. Get close enough, or far enough, and each one stops being a thing and becomes a feeling.

### 054 · Room 47 · Mark Rothko — Untitled (Green on Maroon)
*Room 47 · 279 words*

**Where:** In Room 47, "American Abstraction II", the small room between 46 and 48. The Rothko is the biggest canvas here, taller than a person: a dark field of green floating on maroon, as the title says. Stand about an arm's length or two away.

**What:** Mark Rothko, Untitled (Green on Maroon) (ES: Sin título, Verde sobre morado), 1961, mixed media on canvas, 258 × 229 cm, inv. 729, Room 47, Level 1. English title used, as PLAN directs.

Mark Rothko made his paintings huge for an unexpected reason. Not to overwhelm you from across a room. He wanted you close, and he believed a big canvas made the meeting more intimate.

So come in a little. Close enough that the edges slip out of the corners of your eyes.

Up close, you'll see there's almost no surface to speak of. No brushstrokes, hardly any texture. He laid the colour on in thin, diluted layers, more like watercolour than oil, one veil over another. That's why the green doesn't sit on the maroon. It hovers, and never quite separates from it.

This was painted in 1961, the year New York's Museum of Modern Art gave him a major show. Rothko oversaw every detail. He hung the pictures close together, in a soft, diffuse light. Around then his bright, glowing colours gave way to maroons, greys, dark greens and browns. This canvas was most likely painted just after that show closed, though no one can be quite sure.

He thought of his paintings as dramas, the performance of a timeless tragedy. Colour was the only actor.

Give it a minute, if you can. Let the green settle.

The critic Robert Rosenblum saw where this came from. Not from Paris, but from northern Romantic painting, and he compared Rothko to Caspar David Friedrich. Both, he wrote, "look for the sublime in a profane world." You met Friedrich's women walking out into the dawn upstairs, in Room 29. Here there are no women and no moon. Only the dawn, or the dusk, and you.

### 055 · Room 48 · Romare Bearden — Sunday After Sermon
*Room 48 · 346 words*

**Where:** In Room 48, "Post-war American Art", the corner room at the end of this wing. Sunday After Sermon is a collage about a metre high and a little wider, a street full of figures cut from magazines and newspapers. Express is the long canvas, three metres wide, crowded with grainy printed photographs.

**What:** Romare Bearden, Sunday After Sermon (Domingo después del sermón), 1969, collage on cardboard, 101.6 × 127 cm, inv. 462, Room 48, Level 1. Also: Robert Rauschenberg, Express, 1963, oil and silkscreen ink on canvas, 184.2 × 305.2 cm, inv. 721; Joseph Cornell, Juan Gris Cockatoo No. 4, ca. 1953–1954, construction and collage, inv. 491. All Room 48.

In 1963, shortly before Martin Luther King's march on Washington, a group of black artists met in a studio in Harlem. The question on the table was simple and hard. What should artists do about civil rights?

The studio belonged to Romare Bearden. His family had come north from North Carolina in the Great Migration and settled in Harlem. He had studied under George Grosz, whose red, apocalyptic Berlin hangs back in Room 37. Out of that meeting came a group called Spiral. It didn't last long, but it set out to make collages on socially committed themes. Collage became Bearden's way of telling stories.

And this is a story with no drama at all. It's Sunday. The service is over, and people stand about in the street, talking to each other. That's the whole event. For Bearden, this ordinary ritual of black American life was something bigger, a social and moral picture of a community holding together.

Look at how it's made. Faces, hands and clothes are cut from newspapers and magazines, with a few scraps of painted paper. Nothing matches in scale, and yet the street feels crowded and alive.

Now the long canvas nearby. Robert Rauschenberg made Express in 1963, using silkscreens to print photographs straight onto the canvas, a technique he took up under Warhol's influence. Find the horse and rider about to jump. The dancers are a tribute to his friend Merce Cunningham. There's a climber hanging from a rope, and a nude on a staircase that nods to Marcel Duchamp. Express went to the Venice Biennale in 1964, the year Rauschenberg became the first American to win its top prize. Some images, he said, "insist on being themselves, do what you will to them."

And the small glass box is Joseph Cornell's homage to Juan Gris, a paper cockatoo with its own black paper shadow. Gris's own Smoker hangs back in Room 41.

### 056 · Room 49 · Lucian Freud — Portrait of a Man (Baron H.H. Thyssen-Bornemisza)
*Room 49 · 335 words*

**Where:** In Room 49, "Post-war European Figurative Art". The Baron's portrait is small, about half a metre high: a man in a checked jacket, white shirt and dark tie, head tipped forward, eyes lowered. Reflection with Two Children is the square canvas nearby, Freud seen from below with two tiny children in the lower corner.

**What:** Lucian Freud, Portrait of a Man (Baron H.H. Thyssen-Bornemisza), 1981–1982, oil on canvas, 51 × 40 cm, inv. 551, Room 49, Level 1. Also: Lucian Freud, Reflection with Two Children (Self-Portrait), 1965, oil on canvas, 91 × 91 cm, inv. 550, Room 49.

Baron Hans Heinrich Thyssen-Bornemisza spent his life choosing portraits, buying them and living with them. Until 1981, he had never sat for one.

That July he went to Lucian Freud's studio in London, near Notting Hill Gate, for the first sitting. Freud worked slowly, and the sessions were long, almost endless. The Baron kept coming back, punctually, for nearly two years. The portrait was finished in October 1982.

Freud made no preparatory drawings. He started straight on the canvas with a charcoal sketch. Then he built the face up in layer after layer of paint, some of it laid on with a palette knife. He said of his sitters, "I try to enter as far as possible into their feelings, so the picture will be about them, not me."

Now look at what he saw. The canvas is small, and he placed the Baron very close. A checked jacket, a white shirt, a dark tie. The head tips forward and the eyes look down, away from you. It's not the face of a man showing off his treasures. It's gloomier than that, more private.

And then look behind him, above his shoulder. Freud has barely sketched in a fragment of another painting. It's Watteau's Pierrot Content, which the Baron had owned since 1977, and which Freud kept pinned up on his studio wall as a reproduction. The Baron sits exactly where Pierrot sits in the Watteau, and seems to borrow his pose and his expression. You saw that Pierrot upstairs, in Room 24.

Nearby, Freud turns the scrutiny on himself. In Reflection with Two Children he looms over a mirror at his feet, and his children Rose and Ali appear tiny in the lower corner.

A collector who spent his life choosing what to look at, sitting still at last, and being looked at, in the pose of a painting he loved.

### 057 · Room 50 · Extra · Frank Auerbach — Head of J.Y.M.
*Room 50 · 186 words*

**Where:** In Room 50, "Informalisms". Head of J.Y.M. is a small, almost square canvas, about sixty centimetres across, where a head emerges from thick ridges of paint. Wild Horses by Karel Appel is the tall canvas in loud primary colours.

**What:** Frank Auerbach, Head of J.Y.M. (Cabeza de J. Y. M.), 1978, oil on canvas, 61 × 66 cm, inv. 455, Room 50, Level 1. Also: Karel Appel, Wild Horses, 1954, oil on canvas, 194.5 × 113 cm, inv. 454, Room 50.

Three initials, and a great deal of paint. J.Y.M. stands for Juliet Yardley Mills, a professional model whom Frank Auerbach painted often. Like his wife Julia, she appears again and again in his work.

He hid her name behind initials on purpose. The anonymity is part of the point. This is less a likeness of one woman than a painting made from her presence.

Now look at the surface. The paint is so heavy it's almost sculpture, ridges and furrows of it, pushed around with big gestures. There are no outlines. The brushwork alone builds the head, and the same thick paint swallows the background too. Auerbach wanted to trap the physical essence of a person, not to describe her.

The tall canvas nearby goes the other way, loud instead of dense. Karel Appel was one of the founders of CoBrA, named after Copenhagen, Brussels and Amsterdam. They painted fast and free on purpose, in garish primary colours, almost like children.

Room 51 has a short Extra on Richard Estes's Telephone Booths. The next main stop is Room 52, Pop Art: Lichtenstein's comic-strip Woman in Bath.

### 058 · Room 51 · Extra · Richard Estes — Telephone Booths
*Room 51 · 227 words*

**Where:** In Room 51, "Homo Ludens". Telephone Booths is the wide picture, nearly two metres across, of a row of glass and metal phone booths on a New York corner, each with a person inside, back turned. People's Flowers is the tall upright canvas by the same artist, a shopfront with a neon sign at its centre.

**What:** Richard Estes, Telephone Booths, 1967, acrylic on masonite, 122 × 175.3 cm, inv. 539, Room 51, Level 1. Also: Richard Estes, People's Flowers, 1971, oil on canvas, 153 × 101.2 cm, CTB.1975.24 (Carmen Thyssen Collection), Room 51.

Nothing in this picture is quite where your eye first puts it. That's deliberate.

Richard Estes is one of the leading names of American Photorealism. But he didn't copy a single photograph. For Telephone Booths he took several pictures of a row of phone booths where Broadway, Sixth Avenue and 34th Street meet, and then combined them into one scene.

Now try to read it. The glass is see-through and a mirror at the same time. Some reflections are crisp on the glass, others warp across the metal. You can see the street in front of the booths and the buildings behind you, all at once. Look for the signs of Macy's and Woolworth, caught among the reflections.

And there's something unusual here. Estes's cities are nearly always empty. In this one, every booth is taken, each person with their back to you, talking to someone you'll never hear.

The tall canvas is his too. People's Flowers belongs to the Carmen Thyssen collection, which you'll see on the ground floor, but it hangs up here. Follow the frames and rectangles inward, and they lead you to the neon sign in the middle. Keep Estes in mind. He comes back in the last room of this guide.

Next, Room 52, Pop Art: Lichtenstein's Woman in Bath, a big square canvas of a smiling woman against a tiled wall.

### 059 · Room 52 · Roy Lichtenstein — Woman in Bath, and David Hockney — In Memoriam of Cecchino Bracci
*Room 52 · 338 words*

**Where:** In Room 52, "Pop Art", the last room on this floor. Woman in Bath is the big square canvas, about 1.7 metres each way, of a smiling comic-strip woman against a tiled wall, in red, yellow and blue dots. Hockney's In Memoriam of Cecchino Bracci is the tall, narrow canvas, over two metres high, shaped like a tomb.

**What:** Roy Lichtenstein, Woman in Bath, 1963, oil and Magna on canvas, 173.3 × 173.3 cm, inv. 648, Room 52, Level 1. David Hockney, In Memoriam of Cecchino Bracci, 1962, oil on canvas, 213.3 × 91.4 cm, inv. 584, Room 52, Level 1.

By the early 1960s, a group of young Americans had had enough of Abstract Expressionism, all that heroic paint and private anguish. Lichtenstein, Warhol, Wesselmann and Rosenquist turned instead to what everyone was already looking at: comics, adverts, billboards. That was Pop.

Roy Lichtenstein most likely found this woman in a romantic comic strip. Look how little he uses. Three primary colours, red, yellow and blue. Thick black outlines around her face and hands. A tiled wall behind her, as flat as graph paper. And those dots. Comic printers used them for cheap shading. They were invented by a printer called Benjamin Day. Lichtenstein painted every one of them by hand. The machine's work, done by the artist.

She's also very old news. A woman at her bath is an old subject in painting, Venus at her toilet. This Venus beams straight out at you. Lichtenstein once said that women draw themselves this way, and that this is what make-up really is.

Now the tall canvas. In 1962, the young David Hockney, still in London, painted a lament for Cecchino Bracci, a beautiful youth close to Michelangelo, who died at fifteen in January 1544. Michelangelo designed his tomb in Rome and wrote epitaphs for him. Hockney shaped the canvas like a coffin. Cecchino lies inside in his shroud, arms crossed, wearing a bowler hat. Look for the laurel wreath around his name, and, very small at the lower right, the first lines of one of Michelangelo's epitaphs. The coffin shape, Hockney said, let him paint "merrily in a flat style."

The Postpop rooms, 53 to 56, hold a Kenny Scharf exhibition until 17 January 2027, and they are not part of this guide.

## Level 0 · Carmen Thyssen collection · From Old Masters to the avant-gardes

### 060 · Room A · Jan Brueghel the Elder — The Garden of Eden
*Room A · 316 words*

**Where:** In Room A, "Old Masters", the first room of the Carmen Thyssen collection. The Garden of Eden is a small panel, about a metre wide, packed with animals: look for a horse, a lion and lioness on the left, and two tiny figures far in the background.

**What:** Jan Brueghel the Elder, The Garden of Eden, ca. 1610–1612, oil on panel, 59.4 × 95.6 cm, CTB.1988.29, Carmen Thyssen Collection, Room A, Level 0. Collection intro: Carmen Thyssen collection, ground floor.

Everything upstairs began with one family. This floor belongs to one woman's eye.

Carmen Cervera, the Spanish wife of Baron Hans Heinrich, was the decisive voice in bringing his collection to Madrid. When the State bought the heart of it in 1993, the family kept other works. Those became the core of her own collection, and she went on buying, above all landscape, Impressionism and German Expressionism. Since 2022, about 330 of its works have been leased to the Spanish state until the late 2030s, with an option for the State to buy at the end. It's hung in order, from the 17th century to the 20th, and individual works are sometimes lent or rotated.

We start in Paradise. Jan Brueghel, younger son of the great Pieter Bruegel, painted this around 1610. It's one of the earliest of the many Gardens of Eden he would paint. In his day, he and Rubens were the leading painters in Antwerp.

Now look for Adam and Eve. Don't search the foreground. They're far away in the background, two small figures in a crowded, exotic world. The real subject is the animals.

And some of them are borrowed. The horse probably comes from a lost equestrian portrait by Rubens. The lion and lioness on the left come from Rubens's Daniel in the Lions' Den. The leopards repeat two from another lost Rubens, which survives only as an engraving. It isn't theft. The two men worked side by side, and on another version of this same subject, Brueghel painted the landscape and animals while Rubens painted the figures.

Infrared photographs show that Brueghel changed his mind about the horse and the camel while he worked. Even in Paradise, the details took some arranging.

Room B, North American landscape, has a short Extra on Bierstadt's Sundown at Yosemite. The next main stop is Room C, with Van Gogh's wide Watermill at Gennep.

### 061 · Room B · Extra · Albert Bierstadt — Sundown at Yosemite
*Room B · 192 words*

**Where:** In Room B, "North American Landscape". Sundown at Yosemite is a small oil sketch, about forty centimetres wide: a sunset breaking between the granite walls of a valley. Heade's Orchid and Hummingbird is the picture with a pink orchid in close-up and a tiny bird beside it.

**What:** Albert Bierstadt, Sundown at Yosemite, ca. 1863, oil on canvas, 30.5 × 40.6 cm, CTB.1980.9, Room B, Level 0. Also: Martin Johnson Heade, Orchid and Hummingbird near a Mountain Waterfall, 1902, oil on canvas, 38.2 × 51.5 cm, CTB.1979.44, Room B.

You've just left one Garden of Eden in Room A. Here's another. In 1863 the writer Fitz Hugh Ludlow travelled west with Albert Bierstadt to Yosemite. If the reports were true, he wrote, they were heading for the original site of the Garden of Eden.

They spent seven weeks in the valley. Bierstadt worked fast, making small, quick oil sketches on the spot. This is one of them. Look at the sun breaking through between the granite walls. He used that effect again, much bigger, in later pictures built from these studies.

For Americans at the time, a sunset over the West was a symbol of the country's destiny. And the valley was revealed to the country in the dark years of the Civil War, which gave that symbol extra force. You met this American wilderness upstairs, in Room 31.

Nearby, a pink orchid and a hummingbird. Martin Johnson Heade painted it in 1902, when he was eighty-three, living in Florida. He kept hummingbirds as pets there, and you can see how closely he had watched them.

Next, Room C, French naturalist landscape: Van Gogh's Watermill at Gennep, a long, dark canvas.

### 062 · Room C · Vincent van Gogh — Watermill at Gennep
*Room C · 297 words*

**Where:** In Room C, "French Naturalist Landscape". The Van Gogh is the long, dark canvas, a metre and a half wide: a wooden watermill with two wheels on a river, a farmhouse behind, and sunbeams breaking through grey cloud above. Degas's Pond in the Forest is the small, dark woodland picture nearby.

**What:** Vincent van Gogh, Watermill at Gennep, 1884, oil on canvas, 85 × 151 cm, CTB.1996.32, Room C, Level 0. Also: Edgar Degas, The Pond in the Forest, ca. 1867–1868, oil on canvas, 33.5 × 41.2 cm, CTB.2000.65, Room C.

In the third week of November 1884, the temperature in the Dutch countryside was dropping below freezing. Vincent van Gogh was still outside, painting this.

He was living at his parents' home in Nuenen, and the mill at Gennep was within walking distance. He wrote to his brother Theo that he was working out of doors on a large study of it, despite the cold. It would probably be his last outdoor work that year. It's the largest of the watermills he painted that year, a metre and a half wide.

Then something went wrong. A new friend from Eindhoven, Anton Kerssemakers, watched him work. He remembered that Vincent used too much copaiva balsam, a medium that slows drying. The sky simply melted. So Van Gogh scraped it off with a palette knife and painted it again.

Look closely and you can still find the first sky. There's a bluish reflection in the water on the right, and touches of blue near the wheel. The grey clouds probably came later. Above the mill, sunbeams break through, and the light on the water throws long reflections towards you.

Theo had complained that his paintings were too dark. Vincent felt this one was progress. And here, for the first time, he put down small touches of pure colour, a step towards Impressionism. Still, at heart it's a Dutch landscape, which is why it hangs here with the naturalist landscape painters, not with the Post-Impressionists. This is the Dutch Van Gogh, years before the blazing barge at sunset in Room 34.

Nearby, a rarity: a Degas landscape, a dark woodland pool scraped thin with a palette knife. He may have been studying water for a painting of a ballet, La Source.

Next, Room D, Impressionism: Berthe Morisot's Reclining Nude Shepherdess.

### 063 · Room D · Berthe Morisot — Reclining Nude Shepherdess
*Room D · 303 words*

**Where:** In Room D, "Impressionism". Morisot's painting is the horizontal canvas, almost a metre wide, of a nude young woman lying in the open air by water, still wearing a headscarf. Sisley's Flood at Port-Marly is the smaller street scene with floodwater spreading under a big sky.

**What:** Berthe Morisot, Reclining Nude Shepherdess, 1891, oil on canvas, 57.5 × 86.4 cm, CTB.2000.9, Room D, Level 0. Also: Alfred Sisley, The Flood at Port-Marly, 1876, oil on canvas, 50 × 61 cm, CTB.1974.25, Room D.

Upstairs, in Room 33, you saw Berthe Morisot indoors, with a young woman at her mirror. Here she has stepped outside, into a summer garden.

In 1890 and again in 1891, Morisot rented a house at Mézy, on the Seine, about fifty kilometres west of Paris. She came with her husband, Eugène Manet, and their daughter Julie. The house had an orchard and a terrace above the river, and the country setting pushed her towards new subjects.

This was one of them, and she went about it in an unexpected way. Morisot was one of the founders of Impressionism, a painter of the quick, fresh touch. Yet she built this picture, in the museum's phrase, in time-honoured academic fashion. She painted the river valley on its own. She painted a goat called Colette. She made drawings and a compositional sketch, and three painted versions of the reclining shepherdess.

She posed the model nude, to understand the body, but let her keep her headscarf on. Look for it. It's the one thing left of the shepherdess.

By then she admired Renoir's nudes, and Renoir came to visit her at Mézy. But a nude out in the open air also looks back to Corot, who had been her teacher. The figure here is more compact and contained than her earlier work. It's the most complete and ambitious study of the nude she ever painted.

And somewhere along the way the shepherdess turned into something else: a nymph, or a spring, lost in thought beside the water.

Nearby, Alfred Sisley's Flood at Port-Marly. In March 1876 the Seine broke its banks, and Sisley painted seven pictures of it, stage by stage. Here the water fills the rue de Paris.

Next, Room E, "Monet and North American Impressionism": Monet's house front, half hidden between two rose trees.

### 064 · Room E · Claude Monet — The House among the Roses
*Room E · 294 words*

**Where:** In Room E, "Monet and North American Impressionism". The Monet is an upright canvas, nearly a metre high: the front of his house at Giverny between two big rose trees in bloom, the left one cut off by the edge of the picture. Hassam's Plaza de la Merced, Ronda is the smaller sunlit Spanish square seen from above.

**What:** Claude Monet, The House among the Roses, 1925, oil on canvas, 92.3 × 73.3 cm, CTB.1998.52, Room E, Level 0. Also: Childe Hassam, Plaza de la Merced, Ronda, 1910, oil on panel, 64.6 × 52 cm, CTB.1996.22, Room E.

For more than ten years, Claude Monet had been losing his colours. Cataracts were clouding his eyes. An operation in 1923 changed the way he saw colour, and for a long time nothing seemed to help.

Then, in the spring of 1925, it came back. He began to work intensely. That July he wrote to a friend, apologising that he couldn't see him in the mornings, because he had to paint. His sight was much better, he said, and he was very pleased with his work. If only his lenses were better still, he would like to live to a hundred.

This is one of six pictures from that summer. It's his own house at Giverny, seen from the garden. The slate roof catches the light and turns violet. The upstairs windows are dark gaps. And two great rose trees in bloom frame the front, the left one so close that the edge of the canvas cuts it off.

Look at the brushwork. Short strokes, like commas, in far more colours than some of his earlier garden views, which drift towards a single tone. The house sits square in the middle, almost symmetrical, almost solemn. Some see it pointing straight towards the abstract painting of the new century. Monet died the following year.

Nearby, a Spanish square. The American Childe Hassam travelled through Spain in 1910, painting in Madrid, Toledo, Córdoba, Seville and Ronda. He looked at this plaza in Ronda, it seems, from his hotel window. Back in New York, critics loved the strong light and blue sky, and praised the pictures as "artistic, not operatic". No bullfights, no castanets. Just a place, and the sun on it.

Next, Room F, "Gauguin and Postimpressionism": Mata Mua, Tahitian women beneath a great stone idol.

### 065 · Room F · Paul Gauguin — Mata Mua (In Olden Times)
*Room F · 318 words*

**Where:** In Room F, "Gauguin and Postimpressionism". Mata Mua is the upright canvas, about ninety centimetres high, in flat, glowing colour: a giant tree trunk down the middle, a woman playing a flute in front, dancers around a great stone idol, and pink and purple mountains behind. Coming and Going, Martinique is the wider landscape with women carrying baskets on their heads.

**What:** Paul Gauguin, Mata Mua (In Olden Times) (Mata Mua, Erase una vez), 1892, oil on canvas, 91 × 69 cm, CTB.1984.8, Room F, Level 0. Also: Paul Gauguin, Coming and Going, Martinique, 1887, oil on canvas, 72.5 × 92 cm, CTB.1979.88, Room F.

When Paul Gauguin sailed for Tahiti in 1891, he was looking for a world untouched by Europe. He didn't find it. What he found were the remains of an old Maori culture, already worn away by colonial rule and by the missionaries.

So he painted the world he had hoped for. Mata Mua means "in olden times". It's an elegy for a golden age that had gone, or perhaps had never quite existed in this form. The scene isn't a real place. He put it together from pieces of other pictures, like a stage set.

Now read it the way you'd read a Japanese print, from the bottom up. In front, a woman plays the flute. A huge tree trunk runs down the middle, like a hinge. Beyond it, women dance around a giant stone idol of Hina, the moon goddess. And behind everything rise mountains in pink and purple. No colour here is trying to be natural. Each one sits flat, in its own patch, against the next.

Paris was baffled. When Gauguin auctioned his work to pay for his second journey to the Pacific, nobody bought this one, even at the five hundred francs he asked. In 1984 Baron Hans Heinrich bought it at a record price, at first sharing it with a partner, and then owning it alone.

It has travelled since. It left Madrid under an export licence, and came back in 2022 as part of the lease that keeps this collection here.

Next to it hangs Gauguin before Tahiti. A few years earlier he spent nearly four months on Martinique with the painter Charles Laval, and painted women carrying fruit to market. Look for the baskets on their heads. Edgar Degas owned that picture for the rest of his life.

Room G has a short Extra on Kandinsky's Ludwigskirche in Munich. The next main stop is Room H, "Early Avant-gardes": Natalia Goncharova's Fishing.

### 066 · Room G · Extra · Wassily Kandinsky — The Ludwigskirche in Munich
*Room G · 210 words*

**Where:** In Room G, "Neo Impressionism and its Wake". The Kandinsky is a wide picture on cardboard, about a metre across: a crowd in jewel-like dabs of colour under the dark arches of a church porch. Signac's Port-en-Bessin, The Beach is the smaller, quieter seaside view.

**What:** Wassily Kandinsky, The Ludwigskirche in Munich, 1908, oil on cardboard, 67.3 × 96 cm, CTB.1977.101, Room G, Level 0. Also: Paul Signac, Port-en-Bessin, The Beach, 1884, oil on canvas, 46 × 64.5 cm, CTB.2000.60, Room G.

In the autumn of 1908, Kandinsky moved into a flat and studio in Schwabing, the artists' quarter of Munich. A few metres away stood the church of Saint Ludwig. When a religious procession passed, he sketched it in a notebook.

The sketch survives, covered in colour notes. Red and rose pink above the porch. Green, violet and blue under the arches. He wanted the contrasts clear in his head before he painted.

Now look at what he did with them. The sun is so bright that the porch falls into deep shadow. Against that darkness, the crowd becomes small, glowing patches of colour, banners and priests in yellow vestments among them. Many of the patches aren't anything at all. They're just colour. That was something new for him.

Upstairs, in Room 37, you saw a bright Murnau street from the same year. And in Room 39, years later, the pure abstraction of In the Bright Oval. Here you can watch the first step from one to the other.

The quieter beach nearby is by Paul Signac, whose dotted technique had fascinated Kandinsky for years. Here Signac is young and still painting like Monet. It even carries the wrong year beside his signature.

Next, Room H, "Early Avant-gardes": Natalia Goncharova's Fishing.

### 067 · Room H · Natalia Goncharova — Fishing (fishers)
*Room H · 298 words*

**Where:** In Room H, "Early Avant-gardes". Goncharova's Fishing is the almost square canvas, just over a metre high, of peasants fishing, painted in bright, flat colour with heavy outlines. Picasso's Harvesters is the smaller rural scene with a figure striding towards you, arms raised. Delaunay's Large Portuguese is the very large canvas, two metres wide, in strong, saturated colour.

**What:** Natalia Goncharova, Fishing (fishers), 1909, oil on canvas, 112 × 99.7 cm, CTB.1977.34, Room H, Level 0. Also: Pablo Picasso, The Harvesters (Les Moissonneurs), 1907, oil on canvas, 65 × 81.5 cm, CTB.1979.39; Robert Delaunay, Portuguese Woman (The Large Portuguese), 1916, oil and wax on canvas, 180 × 205 cm, CTB.1998.8. All Room H.

Natalia Goncharova once gave a speech in Moscow about Cubism. It was, she said, "a positive phenomenon, but it is not altogether new." Russia, she argued, had been doing it for centuries, in ancient Scythian stone figures and in painted fairground dolls.

That's the spirit of this painting. The museum calls her the first, in time, of the leading women artists of 20th-century Russia. On visits to her family's country estate near Moscow, she watched the peasants at work. She loved the crafts and rituals of old Russia, its icons, its popular prints, its folk art. With Mikhail Larionov she would put on an exhibition of icons and broadsheets in Moscow, as if they were the newest thing in art.

Now look at Fishing. Bright colour, emphatic outlines, flat decorative shapes. The perspective is naive on purpose. Some of that comes from Russia. Some of it comes from France, from Gauguin, whose Tahiti you've just seen in Room F, and from Matisse. She takes both and makes them her own. Upstairs, in Room 43, you met other women of the Russian avant-garde.

Later she designed for Diaghilev's productions in Paris, The Golden Cockerel and The Firebird among them.

She keeps good company here. Picasso's Harvesters dates from 1907, the same months as his Demoiselles d'Avignon. Look for the peasant striding towards you with arms raised, and a cartwheel in the middle. And the huge canvas is Robert Delaunay's Large Portuguese. The First World War caught him and his wife Sonia on holiday in San Sebastián. They stayed in the Iberian peninsula, in Madrid and then in northern Portugal, painting its market women in that clear northern light.

Next, Room I: Juan Gris's Seated Woman, a tall panel of a woman in a pleated cloak, built from overlapping triangles.

### 068 · Room I · Juan Gris — Seated Woman
*Room I · 290 words*

**Where:** In Room I, "Between the Wars Painting. Cubism, Abstraction and Surrealism". Seated Woman is the tall panel, over a metre high, of a woman in a pleated cloak and party dress, her figure built from overlapping triangles and slanting planes.

**What:** Juan Gris, Seated Woman (Mujer sentada), 1917, oil on panel, 116 × 73 cm, CTB.1986.23, Carmen Thyssen Collection, Room I, Level 0.

Juan Gris was born here in Madrid. When this was painted, in the spring of 1917, he was in Paris, in the middle of a war that wasn't his.

As a Spaniard he didn't have to fight. But life was hard. Foreigners were under suspicion, and food and fuel came and went. His dealer, Kahnweiler, was in exile, and for a while Gris was really poor. Then a new dealer, Léonce Rosenberg, signed him up. This painting went to Rosenberg for three hundred and fifty francs.

The woman is probably Josette, his partner. No one has proved it, but this picture is the pair to a portrait of her from the year before. She's dressed up here, in a party dress. Gris once traded a small painting for a dress made for her by the sister of the couturier Paul Poiret. Is this that dress? It's a lovely thought. Nobody knows.

Now look at how he builds her. Overlapping triangles, a pleated cloak, oblique lines all leaning against each other. From above, the way Cézanne looked down on his seated women. But there's an older master in here too. Gris had been studying Corot, and making paintings after him. The play of curves against straight edges, the soft dark and light, comes from Corot. This was a time when he and Picasso were especially close, and Cubism was learning to look back.

You met Gris upstairs in Room 41, with The Smoker. Here, four years on, he is moving towards a calmer, purer Cubism. A Madrid painter, far from home, painting the woman he lived with in her best dress.

### 069 · Room J · Extra · Charles Bell — Thunder Smash
*Room J · 211 words*

**Where:** In Room J, "North American Painting and Others", the last room of the Carmen Thyssen collection. Thunder Smash is the big, glossy close-up of a pinball machine, about 1.7 metres wide, with stars, a crescent moon and comic-book explosions on a lit panel and a shining metal ball. Estes's Nedick's is the shopfront scene with a deep view into the store.

**What:** Charles Bell, Thunder Smash, 1977, acrylic on canvas, 137.8 × 167.6 cm, CTB.1977.91, Room J, Level 0. Also: Richard Estes, Nedick's, 1970, oil on canvas, 121.9 × 167.6 cm, CTB.1993.10, Room J.

The last painting in this guide is a pinball machine. After seven centuries, that seems fair.

Charles Bell was one of the pioneers of Photorealism. In the late 1960s he painted gumball machines, which reflect the light. Then, in 1976, he turned to pinball machines, which throw light out at you. Here you get both at once. A panel glowing from behind, and a metal ball that mirrors everything around it.

The panel speaks the language of comics: white and yellow stars, a crescent moon, thunderbolts and explosions. Where did all that come from? The museum suggests two sparks. The first colour photographs of the surface of Mars, sent back by the Viking 1 probe. And the premiere of Star Wars, in 1977, the year of this painting.

And here's Richard Estes again, whom you met in Room 51. Nedick's was a turning point for him. For the first time his view goes deep into the shop, to mirrors twenty feet back, and out through the windows to a distant city on the left.
