This cathedral stands where the Almohads built a great mosque in the twelfth century. Its bell tower was the mosque's minaret. After Fernando the Third took Seville in 1248, the mosque became the cathedral. In 1401 the canons decided to replace it with this Gothic church. ## La Giralda

### 002 · Giralda — The minaret of 1184
*Giralda · first ramps · 269 words*

**Where:** At the tower door or on the first ramps of the Giralda [O3, O4]: broad, gently sloping ramps with no stairs, turning between the outer wall and the central core. Heard while climbing; keep moving, keep right.

**What:** The Giralda, Almohad minaret ordered 1184 under the caliph Abu Ya'qub Yusuf, carried on under al-Mansur; laid out by Ahmad ibn Baso, continued in brick by Ali al-Ghumari; gilded yamur raised March 1198, brought down by the 1356 earthquake; Renaissance belfry of the sixteenth century; close to 100 m to the tip of the weathervane.

Eight centuries ago, this tower ended in four golden balls. They shone so brightly, it was said, that you could see them a day's march from the city.

The tower began as a minaret. In 1184 the Almohad caliph Abu Ya'qub Yusuf ordered one for his new mosque in Seville. He did not live to see it rise. His son, al-Mansur, carried the work on. The architect who laid it out was Ahmad ibn Baso. A second master, Ali al-Ghumari, finished it in brick.

In March 1198 the great gilded balls, the yamur, were hoisted to the very top. A chronicler of the time, Ibn Sahib al-Salat, wrote that travellers saw the minaret "as if suspended among the stars." For a century and a half the balls caught the sun. Then an earthquake in 1356 brought them crashing down.

By then Seville was a Christian city, and the minaret had become the cathedral's bell tower. In the sixteenth century a Renaissance belfry was built on top. That's where you're heading. From the ground to the tip of its weathervane, the tower rises close to a hundred metres.

As you climb, notice what's under your feet. These aren't stairs but broad, gently sloping ramps. They wind between the outer wall and an inner core, like one tower built around another.

The Giralda has famous sisters, the great Almohad minarets of Marrakesh and Rabat. And it has children. Towers modelled on it were built across Spain and the Americas.

Keep climbing, and keep to the right. The next stop plays on the ramps themselves, a few turns further up the Giralda.

### 003 · Giralda — Thirty-four ramps
*Giralda · ramps · 242 words*

**Where:** On the ramps inside the Giralda, a few turns above the door [O4]: a wide, gently sloping ramp between the outer wall and the central core, people coming down on the other side. Heard while climbing; keep moving, keep right.

**What:** The Almohad interior of the Giralda: 34 ramps and 7 vaulted chambers; ramps wide enough for a rider on horseback; double-walled tubular structure; more than 140 reused capitals and bases; builders' sketches found on the four faces during the 2017–2024 restoration.

The ramps you're climbing were made wide enough for a rider on horseback. Thirty-four of them wind up through the Almohad tower, which also holds seven vaulted chambers. Up and down share the same ramps, so keep to the right, as the cathedral asks.

The design is simple and very strong. One wall runs round the outside, a central core rises in the middle, with the seven chambers stacked inside it, and the ramps turn between the two. Scholars call it a double-walled tube. It has carried the weight of the Renaissance belfry on top for more than four centuries.

The builders were thrifty, too. More than a hundred and forty capitals and column bases, taken from older buildings, have been found reused in the tower and recorded one by one.

Then came a real surprise. Restorers working on the tower's four outer faces from 2017 found sketches left in the brick by the men who built it: lettering, geometric patterns, plants and figures. One scholar suggests they served as a drawing book for the craftsmen, a kind of medieval sketchbook. The findings were presented at a congress in 2025.

So the tower isn't only a monument. It's a record of the people who built it, written into its own bricks.

At the top, walk out onto the bell level of the Giralda. Go round to the side that looks down on the courtyard of orange trees, the Patio de los Naranjos.

### 006 · Giralda — From mosque to cathedral
*Giralda · on the way down · 300 words*

**Where:** On the ramps of the Giralda, going down, keeping to the right [O6]. Heard while walking; no need to stop.

**What:** The Great Mosque of Seville, ordered 1172 by the Almohad caliph Abu Ya'qub Yusuf, prayer hall 1172–76, minaret finished 1198 under his son; 1248 Fernando III takes Seville and the mosque becomes the cathedral; 1401 the chapter's decision; the "think us mad" tradition and the more sober wording handed down; building from the 1430s; finished 1506, opened 11 May 1507; what the mosque left: the minaret, the courtyard and the Puerta del Perdón.

In 1401, the canons of Seville made a decision that would take them more than a century to carry out. To understand it, go back to the mosque.

Eight and a half centuries ago, the Almohad caliph Abu Ya'qub Yusuf ordered a great Friday mosque for Seville, his capital in Spain. Its prayer hall went up in about four years. The minaret, the tower you're walking down now, was finished under his son.

In 1248, King Fernando the Third took Seville. The mosque was consecrated as the city's cathedral, adapted for Christian worship, and it served for a century and a half.

Then, in 1401, the canons voted to pull it down and build a new church. Tradition says one canon put it like this: "Let us build a church so great that those who see it finished will think we were mad." The decision itself, as it has come down to us, is more sober. It speaks of a church "so good that there is none its equal."

Building began in earnest some thirty years later. Stone by stone, the Gothic church rose where the prayer hall had stood. It opened in May 1507, a century after that vote.

But the builders didn't erase everything. They kept the minaret and made it their bell tower. They kept the courtyard where worshippers washed before prayer, now the courtyard of orange trees. And they kept its great gateway, the Puerta del Perdón, the Gate of Forgiveness. That's where you'll leave at the very end.

So as you come down these ramps, you're walking inside the part of the mosque that the cathedral never let go.

At the bottom, step just inside the Catedral, out of the way of the tower door. The next stop is at the foot of the Giralda.

## The heart of the cathedral

People in Seville have a name for this building. They call it a hollow mountain. Give your eyes a moment to adjust, and you'll see why.

Guinness lists it as the cathedral covering the greatest area of any in the world. Notice how wide it is. This is the Capilla de San Antonio, the chapel of Saint Anthony, and it is also the cathedral's baptistery. The thief cut out only the kneeling saint, a piece of canvas taller than a man, and left the angels behind. Rewards were offered. The saint sailed home by way of Havana and Cádiz, and the painting was restored that same year. One New York weekly of the time, The Nation, ended its report on a careful note. The chapel regains its complete altarpiece, it said, "if there is no counterfeiting in the case."

So there he is, still on his knees, still looking up. A saint who went missing, crossed an ocean, and came back to his chapel.

### 015 · Sepulcro de Colón — Whose bones?
*Sepulcro de Cristóbal Colón · 331 words*

**Where:** Still at the tomb of Columbus, by the Puerta del Príncipe: the coffin carried by four heralds.

**What:** The remains in Arturo Mélida's monument; their journey from Valladolid (1506) by way of the Cartuja in Seville, Santo Domingo and Havana; the 1877 Santo Domingo find; the University of Granada DNA studies announced in 2006 and 2024; Hernando Colón, buried in the cathedral, and his library.

Christopher Columbus never stopped travelling, not even after he died.
