### 002 · Museum plaza — Puppy, the guard dog in bloom
*Museum plaza · facing Puppy · 357 words*

**Where:** On the Museum plaza, facing Puppy, the twelve-metre West Highland terrier covered in flowering plants; stand where you can see all of him, clear of the broad stairway.

**What:** Jeff Koons, Puppy, 1992, stainless steel, soil and flowering plants, 1240 × 1240 × 820 cm, Guggenheim Bilbao Museoa (artist's proof; edition 1 at the Brant Foundation, Greenwich, Connecticut). Earlier versions: Arolsen 1992; Sydney 1995–96.

He's more than twelve metres tall. He's alive. And there are two of him.

This is Puppy, by Jeff Koons, made in 1992 of stainless steel, soil and flowering plants. The museum calls him "a behemoth West Highland terrier carpeted in bedding plants". He nods to the formal gardens of eighteenth-century Europe, and Koons wanted him, in his own words, to instil "confidence and security". He stands guard at the museum.

The first Puppy grew at the baroque castle of Arolsen, in Germany. He had about 20,000 plants on a wooden structure, and the design didn't last. He was reborn in Sydney, with about 60,000 plants on a stainless-steel frame worked out by computer. As the Art Gallery of New South Wales records it, the Puppy that "now stands outside the Guggenheim museum in Bilbao" is "the one designed and created" in Sydney. He was unveiled here just before the opening, "just arrived from Australia", as one Spanish paper put it.

You may read that Bilbao kept him by popular demand. "There is an idea that Puppy stayed in Bilbao by popular acclaim," a museum spokeswoman told the same paper. "It isn't so." The museum's art committee had decided to buy him before the opening. The museum acquired him.

And the twin? Koons's studio lists Puppy as an edition of one, plus an artist's proof. Bilbao has the proof. The other stood at Rockefeller Center in New York, and since 2002 has lived at the Brant Foundation in Connecticut.

Now look at his coat. About 40,000 plants, changed twice a year, in May and October. A team of twenty gardeners spends nine days on each change, following the artist's original designs. Since 2022 he has had a new remote-controlled watering system, with moisture sensors in six critical zones. Look closely, and you may find a place where the steel shows through.

If he's half-dressed today, you've caught the gardeners at work.

Before you go in, one stop that matters. In the small square just in front of Puppy, on the city side, there's a plaque. Find it, and stand quietly beside it: Museum plaza · Txema Agirre.

This small square is named after a police officer. His full name was José María Aguirre Larraona. He is remembered by the Basque form of his name, Txema Agirre.

He was an officer of the Ertzaintza, the Basque police. He was thirty-five. He had a newborn son.

On Monday 13 October 1997, five days before the museum opened, men dressed as gardeners were unloading flower planters from a van beside the building. Ertzaintza officers went to check the van. Txema Agirre was one of them.

He was shot. He died of his wounds.

The planters hid grenades, meant for the opening. ETA's target was the inauguration, which King Juan Carlos, the Spanish prime minister and the Basque president were to attend. The Basque president, José Antonio Ardanza, called Txema Agirre a hero.

In the square that bears his name, there is a plaque, and there is a monolith. The plaque took fourteen years to be put up. On anniversaries, flowers are laid here for him. On the twenty-fifth anniversary, in 2022, a tribute was held at the monolith.

### 004 · Museum plaza — "Our town is dying"
*Museum plaza · facing the building, Calle Iparragirre behind you · 349 words*

**Where:** On the Museum plaza, facing the whole front of the museum, with Calle Iparragirre, the long straight street into the city, at your back.

**What:** The 1991 agreement between the Basque institutions and the Solomon R. Guggenheim Foundation (Thomas Krens); the 1991 invited competition (Arata Isozaki, Coop Himmelb(l)au, Frank O. Gehry); the plaza and entrance on the Calle Iparragirre axis; the former wharf on the Nervión.

Frank Gehry remembered how the job was put to him. "They said: 'Mr Gehry, we need the Sydney Opera House. Our town is dying.'"

It began in early 1991, when the Basque government met Thomas Krens, who ran the Solomon R. Guggenheim Foundation in New York. Krens recalled that the conversation that set it all going lasted "no more than half an hour". He had a problem of his own. As one business-school case study tells it, the foundation could show only about three per cent of its collection in New York and Venice.

His terms were blunt. "All costs, operating and capital" would be borne by the Basque side. The Guggenheim kept the "aesthetic decisions": the architecture, the collection, the programme. The Basques also paid the foundation a fee of twenty million dollars, which critics called a "rental fee", and pledged fifty million dollars more to buy art. Krens never dressed it up. "It was commerce, pure and simple, that brought together the Guggenheim and Bilbao."

Then the architect. That same year, three were invited to compete: Arata Isozaki, Coop Himmelblau and Frank Gehry. Each had three weeks and ten thousand dollars to produce a design. Krens said Gehry "provided the most unusual project", and that he "was chosen for the strength of his vision".

Now look at this spot. In the museum's words, "the Museum plaza and main entrance lie in a direct line with Calle Iparragirre". So the long straight street at your back lines up with the door. In front of you, that straight line meets the curves. Beyond it is the river, and the working riverbank this museum replaced. And notice the height: Gehry made something spectacular without letting it rise above the buildings around it.

A dying town asked for an opera house. What it signed was a deal its own author called commerce. What it got is in front of you.

Now head for the broad stairway that goes down from the plaza to the entrance. Play the next stop as you walk down: The stairway · Going down to go in.

## Going in

### 005 · The stairway — Going down to go in
*The broad stairway down to the Hall (heard walking) · 268 words*

**Where:** Start it at the top of the broad stairway that leads down from the plaza to the entrance, and listen as you walk down into the Hall; nothing in it needs a view.

**What:** The descending entrance stairway (OUTSIDE); construction October 1993 – October 1997; executive architects IDOM (Bilbao); director Juan Ignacio Vidarte; main contractor Ferrovial; construction budget about US$100 million, delivered on time and on budget.

Most grand museums make you climb a flight of steps to reach the art. This one sends you down. In the museum's own words, "visitors access the Hall by making their way down a broad stairway", "an unusual feature that successfully overcomes the height difference". The plaza sits a whole floor above the Hall, so you go in by going down.

The building you're walking into took four years to build, from October 1993 to October 1997. And it was built on a tight rein. The executive architects were IDOM, a firm from Bilbao, and they priced every change to the design. Three times, design work was stopped for a complete re-estimate of the cost. The project's director, Juan Ignacio Vidarte, had a simple rule. Nothing was approved unless it could be built within the budget and the schedule.

It cost about 100 million dollars to build. And this is where the story usually goes wrong. You'll often read that it came in under budget. None of the sources we trust says so. What they say is better than a boast: it came in on time and on budget.

The builder was Ferrovial. Its works manager, Juan Ramón Pérez, gave the building a famous compliment. He called it "twenty years ahead of its time". He also admitted that just reaching the building's twisted frame to work on it was "indeed difficult, and financially incredibly costly".

At the bottom, in the Hall, look for ZERO, the small introductory room in the lobby. Play the next stop outside its door, before you go in: ZERO · The room that multiplies.

**Where:** In the Hall, just outside the door of ZERO, the small introductory room in the lobby; play it before you go in, standing clear of the doorway.

**What:** ZERO, the museum's introductory gallery and immersive optical device in the lobby, a commission of the Guggenheim Museum Bilbao created by Local Projects; curved projection with mirrors, a 300-degree view multiplying the room fourteen times; original soundtrack using the txalaparta; opened December 2018.

This room began as a little lounge that hardly anyone used. The design studio that remade it, Local Projects, says so plainly: "We transformed a small, underused lounge in the lobby into ZERO."

What they made, the museum calls "an introductory gallery and immersive optical device". A curved projection and a set of mirrors wrap around you, giving a view of three hundred degrees. The mirrors multiply the room fourteen times over. It's a kaleidoscope you stand inside.

The pictures are about this place. In the museum's words, they show "the history, cityscape, and industrial past of Bilbao and the region of Biscay". There's "a new approach to Frank O. Gehry's building", and there are images of works from the collection.

Listen as well as look. The soundtrack draws on the txalaparta, a traditional percussion instrument of the Basque Country, mixed with electronic sound. A traditional instrument, inside a kaleidoscope.

ZERO opened in December 2018, and the museum suggests starting every visit here. Think of it as an overture: the city and its past first, then the building, before you meet the real thing.

Go in and take a look. Afterwards, walk on into the Atrium, the tall, light-filled heart of the building. Stand somewhere near the middle where you can look straight up: Atrium · The heart of the building.

### 007 · Atrium — The heart of the building
*Atrium · floor 1, near the middle · 336 words*

**Where:** Near the middle of the Atrium, the tall, light-filled space beyond the Hall, at a spot where you can look straight up to the skylight and, ideally, see the columns of red and blue words in gallery 101.

**What:** The Atrium (55 m high, about 900 m²), with glass curtain walls, curved walkways, titanium-and-glass lifts and the metal-flower skylight; about twenty galleries, orthogonal and organic; Jenny Holzer's Installation for Bilbao, 1997/2017, gallery 101, Guggenheim Bilbao Museoa (named in one version-neutral sentence).

You'll often read that this Atrium is fifty metres high. The museum gives a different figure: a height of fifty-five metres. The fifty seems to come from the builders' scaffolding, which reached close to fifty metres in here. The room itself goes higher.

The museum describes it as "an ample space flooded with light and covered by a great skylight". It has "curved volumes and large glass curtain walls that connect the inside and the outside". It calls the Atrium "the heart and soul of the Museum" and "a point of reference for visitors". That's practical advice, too. From here you can reach every gallery, and the terrace outside.

Everything hangs on this space. "The three levels of the building are organized around the Atrium and are connected by means of curved walkways, titanium and glass elevators, and staircases." Around it open about twenty galleries, of two kinds. Some are "orthogonally shaped and with classical proportions": boxes, wrapped in stone. Others have "organic, irregular lines", the titanium curves you saw from the plaza.

Now look up, and take your time. Follow the glass walls, where inside meets outside. Find the walkways hanging across the void, and the glass lifts sliding between the floors. At the very top is the great skylight that covers it all. You'll see it closer from the top floor.

Look for one more thing. Those columns of moving red and blue words, in gallery 101, which you can see from the Atrium, are a work by Jenny Holzer, made for this building.

Not everyone was won over. In Sydney Pollack's film about Gehry, the critic Hal Foster argued that the building upstages the art it's meant to serve. Gehry, he said, had given his clients "a sublime space that overwhelms the viewer". Stand here a minute, and decide whether you agree.

Now find the spot in the Atrium from which you can see into gallery 101 and its tall columns of moving red and blue light: Atrium · Holzer's columns of light.

### 008 · Atrium — Holzer's columns of light
*Atrium · facing gallery 101 · 317 words*

**Where:** In the Atrium, at a spot where you can see into gallery 101 and its row of tall columns of moving red and blue words.

**What:** Jenny Holzer, Installation for Bilbao, 1997/2017, electronic LED sign, site-specific dimensions, Guggenheim Bilbao Museoa, gallery 101: nine vertical, double-sided LED signboards, each more than 12 m tall; texts in Basque, Spanish and English from Arno. Also named: LIKE BEAUTY IN FLAMES (2021), augmented reality, Guggenheim Bilbao Museoa.

You may know Jenny Holzer's words already, even if you don't know her name. In 1982 one of them ran on an electronic board in Times Square: "Abuse of power comes as no surprise." Short, sharp one-liners like that are her Truisms. But that is not what these columns are saying.

This is Installation for Bilbao, made for this place in 1997, the year the museum opened. The museum describes it as "a row of nine vertical, double-sided LED signboards, each more than 12 meters tall", which "bisect one of the Gehry building's tall, irregular galleries". The words run in red and blue light, and in three languages: Basque, Spanish and English.

You'll sometimes hear that these are Truisms too. They aren't. The text comes from a piece of hers called Arno. She first wrote it for a project benefiting AIDS research, and then adapted it for an outdoor projection in Florence, in 1996. Its lines are short and personal: "I say your name." "I save your clothes."

Now give it a minute. Pick one line and follow it as it runs along the column. Then wait for it to come round again in another language. If you can, step to one side, and you'll see that every column has two faces, each carrying words.

Here's a thought to take with you. Much of this building's story is about money and spectacle. Yet in one of its galleries, it keeps words first written to help AIDS research. Whether that's a contrast or a balance is yours to decide.

Holzer has also made a second work for the museum, Like Beauty in Flames, which exists only in augmented reality.

Next, the long gallery that opens off the Atrium and runs under the bridge: gallery 104. Stop just inside its entrance, where you can see down the length of the room: Gallery 104 · The gallery under the bridge.

## The Matter of Time

### 009 · Gallery 104 — The gallery under the bridge
*Gallery 104 · just inside the entrance · 335 words*

**Where:** Just inside the entrance of gallery 104, the long gallery that opens off the Atrium and runs under the bridge, where you can see down the length of the room past the great curved walls of weathering steel.

**What:** Richard Serra (San Francisco 1938 – Long Island 2024), The Matter of Time, 1994–2005, weathering steel, eight sculptures, dimensions variable, about 1,034 tonnes in all, Guggenheim Bilbao Museoa, gallery 104 (about 130 × 30 m, column-free, under La Salve Bridge).

Somewhere above this room runs a road bridge. And yet not one column holds up the ceiling. The museum puts it like this: "Stretching under the bridge, gallery 104—an enormous, column-free space." It's about 130 metres long and 30 wide.

What fills it is The Matter of Time, by Richard Serra. It's eight sculptures of weathering steel, made between 1994 and 2005, just over a thousand tonnes of it in all. Serra was born in San Francisco. He died in March 2024, at his home on Long Island, aged eighty-five.

How are you meant to see it? The museum's answer is that "the entire room is part of the sculptural field". The gaps between the pieces matter as much as the steel. Together they make corridors that are wide, narrow, long, compressed, high and low, "always unexpected", in the museum's words. There's no set route. Each visitor finds their own way through.

And the title is meant literally. There are two kinds of time in here. There's the time it takes you to walk through. And there's the time in which you remember what you felt, and piece it back together. Serra said he wanted this to be "a public space, open, where anyone can come, especially young people".

The eight run in a sequence, "from the relative simplicity of a double ellipse to the complexity of a spiral". They end in pieces made from "sections of toruses and spheres": rings and balls, cut in steel. The oldest, Snake, was here for the opening. The other seven came in 2005.

Now look down the gallery. Find the people walking among the pieces, and see how far the steel rises above their heads. Watch someone disappear into a curve, and come out somewhere you didn't expect. In a minute, that will be you.

Walk in to the first sculptures at this end of the gallery. Step inside the one nearest you and follow its wall as it curves and leans: Gallery 104 · Walls that lean.

### 010 · Gallery 104 — Walls that lean
*Gallery 104 · the first sculptures · 285 words*

**Where:** Inside or beside the first sculptures at the Atrium end of gallery 104, the twisted steel walls nearest the entrance; ideally in the corridor between the two torqued ellipses.

**What:** Richard Serra, The Matter of Time, 1994–2005, Guggenheim Bilbao Museoa: the pieces nearest the Atrium end in David Zwirner's "foreground to rear" order, Torqued Spiral (Closed Open Closed Open Closed), 2003; Torqued Ellipse, 2003–04; Double Torqued Ellipse, 2003–04 (order and labels to confirm at O6).

A wall leans over you. A few steps on, it's falling away from you. And you can't quite say where the change happened.

The pieces at this end of the gallery are the twisted ones. There's Torqued Spiral, with a subtitle you can almost walk: Closed, Open, Closed, Open, Closed. There's Torqued Ellipse, and Double Torqued Ellipse. Serra made them in 2003 and 2004. "Torqued" simply means twisted. You'll feel the twist before you can describe it.

Serra explained it himself, in words the museum prints in English: "They are torqued shapes which, when entering them, allow you to move to understand their torsion." In other words, you don't understand these by looking from outside. You understand them by walking. And he added: "When you move they move too in such a way that you are always trying to get them."

Between the two torqued ellipses there's a gap of about two metres, and that gap makes a corridor. Find it, and walk it slowly. The museum treats the whole room as part of the sculpture, so this corridor is part of the work, just as much as the steel on either side.

Now go inside one of them. As you follow the wall, notice the moment it stops leaning in and starts leaning out. Look up at the rim, where the steel meets the light of the room. And notice whether your body adjusts before your eyes do: a shoulder drops, a step shortens.

You're not looking at a sculpture now. You're inside one, and, just as Serra said, it moves when you move.

Walk on toward the middle of the gallery, to the work called Snake: Gallery 104 · Snake waited eight years.

### 011 · Gallery 104 — Snake waited eight years
*Gallery 104 · Snake · 265 words*

**Where:** Inside gallery 104, beside or at one end of Snake, the piece among the eight whose label reads "Snake"; stand where you can walk its length without blocking its openings.

**What:** Richard Serra (San Francisco 1938 – Long Island 2024), Snake, 1994–97, weathering steel, part of The Matter of Time, 1994–2005, eight sculptures, dimensions variable, Guggenheim Bilbao Museoa.

For eight years, Snake was the only part of The Matter of Time in this room. The rest was still to come.

This is Snake, by Richard Serra, the oldest work in this room. He made it between 1994 and 1997 for the museum's opening. It didn't have the gallery to itself, though. At the opening it shared the room with other works, among them two Lichtensteins and a Warhol, as an Irish critic noted. The other seven Serras came later, and the complete Matter of Time opened in June 2005.

The museum's long-time director, Juan Ignacio Vidarte, said: "This huge gallery was envisioned to hold art of the 21st century." The finished work, he said, "makes the museum a pilgrimage site for anyone interested in the present and future state of sculpture".

The critic Robert Hughes described the room as "one old and seven new rolled steel sculptures".

Snake is the old one. The museum's guide for teachers says it is built from the conical section, "the basic constructive unit": its walls are slices of cones, cut in steel.

So walk its length. Watch how its walls lean, and notice whether the lean stays the same as you go, or slowly changes. Then compare it with the twisted pieces you've just walked through. Cones here. Twisting forms there. One steady idea, and the restless ones that followed.

It was the first. Everything else in this installation came after it.

Walk on along the gallery to the spirals, forms you walk into that keep closing in as you go: Gallery 104 · Lost on purpose.

### 012 · Gallery 104 — Lost on purpose
*Gallery 104 · the spirals · 303 words*

**Where:** Inside gallery 104, beside the two Torqued Spirals, the pieces whose labels read "Torqued Spiral (Right Left)" and "Torqued Spiral (Open Left Closed Right)"; play it at an opening, then walk in.

**What:** Richard Serra (San Francisco 1938 – Long Island 2024), Torqued Spiral (Right Left), 2003–04, and Torqued Spiral (Open Left Closed Right), 2003–04, weathering steel, part of The Matter of Time, 1994–2005, eight sculptures, Guggenheim Bilbao Museoa.

"These pieces have an accelerated time," Richard Serra said. "It's a changed time."

You're among the spirals now. Here are two more Torqued Spirals. One is Torqued Spiral, Right, Left. The other is Torqued Spiral, Open, Left, Closed, Right. Torqued, remember, means twisted. Say those titles again, slowly. They sound less like names than directions for a walk.

The museum describes this gallery as a journey "from the relative simplicity of a double ellipse to the complexity of a spiral". These are the complex ones. And its guide for teachers is blunt about what happens inside these works: "Once inside the works, the viewer loses all reference and does not know what lies ahead."

So go in. Follow a spiral toward its centre. The steel rises well above your head and keeps on curving, and you can't see where the corridor leads until you're in it. Notice whether, at some point, you stop knowing which way you came in.

The same guide says that the spirals, like the twisted ellipses, "seem to be in continuous movement and include the viewer in their movement". Serra put the feeling in his own words: "It's like an abrupt leap through time. It is not linear."

On the way out, test yourself. Do you remember the turns? Or only the feeling of them?

Serra wasn't trying to make you comfortable. His rule was simple: "unless the work is formally innovative, nothing will change." A spiral you can't see out of is one way of making sure something does.

The whole work is called The Matter of Time. In here, time is the material. You've just walked through some of it.

Carry on to the far end of the gallery, to the last two pieces, built from sections of rings and spheres: Gallery 104 · Torus and sphere.

### 013 · Gallery 104 — Torus and sphere
*Gallery 104 · the far end · 335 words*

**Where:** Inside gallery 104, by the last two pieces of the sequence, whose labels read "Between the Torus and the Sphere" and "Blind Spot Reversed"; stand where you can also see along the whole gallery.

**What:** Richard Serra (San Francisco 1938 – Long Island 2024), Between the Torus and the Sphere, 2003–05, and Blind Spot Reversed, 2003–05, weathering steel, part of The Matter of Time, 1994–2005, eight sculptures, Guggenheim Bilbao Museoa.

A torus is a ring, like a doughnut. A sphere is a ball. Richard Serra cut his steel from both.

These are the last two pieces of The Matter of Time: Between the Torus and the Sphere, and Blind Spot Reversed, both finished in 2005. In the museum's words, they are "created from sections of toruses and spheres". The museum's guide for teachers calls them "the two final works of this evolution", and warns that the pieces "transform in unexpected ways while the visitor is going around them".

So go around them. Walk the outside of one slowly, and watch its edge change shape against the room. Then step inside. A wall cut from a sphere curves two ways at once, over you and around you. Feel how different that is from a wall that only leans.

The museum also says the installation "includes a progression in time". This is where that progression arrives.

Serra grew up close to steel. As AP told his story, he was inspired by the time he spent at a shipyard where his father worked as a pipefitter. Later he worked in steel foundries to help pay for his studies. And here his steel stands on an old riverside wharf, in a city that once built ships.

The curator of this installation, Carmen Giménez, called him, as AP reported her words in English, "beyond doubt the most important living sculptor".

Before you leave, look along the whole gallery. Eight pieces in a row, and the people moving among them, small against the steel.

At the far end of the gallery, away from the Atrium, there's an educational space with a video showing how the pieces were installed.

If you're on the full tour, there's a short Extra you can play here or on the walk back: the Serra that vanished. The next main stop is outside. Walk back through the gallery to the Atrium and out onto the terrace on the river side: Atrium terrace · Chillida and the river.

### 014 · Gallery 104 · Extra · The Serra that vanished
*Gallery 104 (anywhere, e.g. the walk back) · 176 words*

**Where:** Anywhere in gallery 104, for example on the walk back to the Atrium; it needs no view.

**What:** Richard Serra, Equal-Parallel: Guernica-Bengasi (Igual-paralelo: Guernica-Bengasi), made for the Museo Reina Sofía, Madrid, 1986, lost; replica 2009. Also Snake, 1994–97, The Matter of Time, Guggenheim Bilbao Museoa.

A Serra really did disappear once. Thirty-eight tonnes of steel, gone. But it wasn't this one, and it wasn't in Bilbao.

The lost one was called Equal-Parallel: Guernica-Bengasi. Serra made it specifically for the Museo Reina Sofía in Madrid, for the exhibition that opened that museum in 1986.

As the Catalan paper Ara told it when Serra died, the work weighed thirty-eight tonnes, and it was lost. It was put into a warehouse, and from there it vanished, without anyone being able to explain what happened.

Thirty-eight tonnes. Mislaid.

The story has a better ending than you might expect. In 2009 Serra made a copy, without charging his fees, so that the work could go back into the museum's galleries.

So if you ever read that Bilbao lost a Serra, or that Snake went missing, you can smile. Snake is here in this gallery, the oldest of the eight pieces around you.

Walk back through the gallery to the Atrium and out onto the terrace on the river side: Atrium terrace · Chillida and the river.
