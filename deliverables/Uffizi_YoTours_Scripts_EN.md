# Yo Tours · Uffizi Gallery · audio guide scripts (EN) · 24 Oct 2026 edition

## Opening

### 001 · Welcome to the Uffizi
*Outside · 333 words*

**Where:** Outside, in the Piazzale degli Uffizi: the long, narrow stone courtyard between the two wings of the building, with statues of famous Tuscans in the niches along the piers and the river at the far end.

**What:** The Uffizi building, designed by Giorgio Vasari for Cosimo I de' Medici, built 1560–1580 and completed after 1574 by Francesco I and Bernardo Buontalenti; the gallery's opening to the public on 24 June 1769; the Family Pact of 1737; how the guide works.

The most famous art gallery in Florence began life as an office block. The word Uffizi simply means "offices".

Around you is the Piazzale degli Uffizi, the long stone courtyard between the two wings, with statues of famous Tuscans standing in the niches. In 1560, Duke Cosimo the First de' Medici began this building to a design by Giorgio Vasari, to bring the offices of the Florentine state under one roof. Vasari and Cosimo both died in 1574. Cosimo's son Francesco and the architect Bernardo Buontalenti finished it.

Francesco filled the top-floor loggia with ancient statues and paintings, a private gallery for himself and his court. Over the next century, a select few visitors from outside the court were let in. Then, on the 24th of June 1769, Grand Duke Peter Leopold opened it to the public. The museum calls it one of the world's first museums created with a modern concept.

Why is it all still here? After the last Medici grand duke died, his sister, Anna Maria Luisa, made a Family Pact with the new ruling house of Lorraine. Nothing was ever to be taken away from Florence and the State. The collections would stay, in the pact's words, for the ornament of the State, the benefit of the public, and to attract the curiosity of foreigners. Which, today, means you.

This guide comes in three lengths. The full tour takes about two and three-quarter hours, the essential version about two hours, and the highlights about an hour and a quarter. Whichever one is on your phone, play it in order. The numbers may jump, and every stop tells you where to go next. We start on the second floor and finish on the first.

Go in at the museum entrance and climb the Granducal staircase all the way to the second floor. There is a lift beside it. At the top, walk through the vestibule, Room A1, into the long corridor lined with marble statues: Room A2, the Eastern Corridor.

## Second floor · The 13th and 14th centuries

### 002 · A2 · The Eastern Corridor
*Room A2 · 284 words*

**Where:** Room A2, the Eastern Corridor, just outside the vestibule at the top of the staircase: a long, bright corridor with tall windows over the courtyard on one side, ancient marble statues and busts along the walls, and a painted ceiling overhead.

**What:** Giorgio Vasari's Eastern Corridor, first of the three top-floor corridors (east, south, west), originally conceived as a loggia open onto the Piazzale; the Medici collection of ancient sculpture, including Roman copies of lost Greek works; the grotesque ceiling, first bay painted in 1581 for Grand Duke Francesco I, the other corridors decorated over the next two centuries.

Before it was a corridor, this was a loggia, open to the air above the Piazzale. And that may be where the word "gallery" comes from.

Vasari designed three of these corridors, east, south and west, running round the top of the building. When Francesco de' Medici began to fill the loggia in the 1580s, this eastern stretch was the whole gallery. It was his private collection, for himself and his court.

He filled it with the family's ancient sculpture. Many of these figures are ancient Roman copies of lost Greek originals, and pairs of statues face each other between the windows. In the eighteenth century this run of marble was one of the great sights of the Grand Tour. Scholars and art lovers came from all over Europe to walk here.

Then look up. The ceiling is covered in grotesques: scrolls, masks, little creatures and garlands. The first bay was painted in 1581 for Francesco, and the date is painted on the vault. The other corridors took the next two centuries. The odd name comes from Rome. Renaissance painters climbed down into the buried rooms of Nero's Golden House and copied what they found. Those underground rooms were called grottoes, so the style became grotesque.

Here's a simple rule that will carry you through the whole museum. The windows look over the courtyard. The painting rooms always open on the other side, away from the windows.

Room A3 is close by, but not down the long corridor. As you leave the vestibule, turn the other way, to the short end of the corridor nearest Palazzo Vecchio. A3 is the doorway there, away from the windows. Look for a tall painted cross on gold.

### 003 · A3 · Painted crosses
*Room A3 · 249 words*

**Where:** Room A3, the first painting room, at the short end of the Eastern Corridor toward Palazzo Vecchio, on the far side of the vestibule from the rest of the route. Look for the big painted cross on a gold ground, nearly three metres tall, with Christ upright and eyes open and small scenes on either side of him.

**What:** Pisan painter, Painted cross, 12th century (dating "highly uncertain"), tempera on wood, gold ground, 276 × 231 cm, Uffizi inv. 1890 no. 432; with the other early gold-ground panels of the room.

This is the oldest room in the gallery. Its great cross is more than eight hundred years old, and nobody knows who painted it, or for which church.

The museum calls its maker simply a Pisan painter, perhaps working in the middle of the twelfth century, though even that date is uncertain. Pisa was then a rich sea power.

Look at Christ. His eyes are open and his head is raised. He isn't suffering. He is triumphant over death, which was the usual way to paint him in Italy until the early twelve hundreds. Around him, small scenes tell the story of the Passion and what came after, from the washing of the disciples' feet to Christ's descent into limbo. They read from top to bottom and left to right, like a strip of pictures.

And everything sits on gold. Here gold isn't decoration. It stands for divine light, for a heaven that never changes, an idea inherited from the Byzantine painting of the Christian East. The figures face you straight on. Nobody has weight. Nobody stands in a space you could step into. These were images for prayer, glowing by candlelight.

Hold on to three things: flat gold, frontal figures, no weight. In the next room, three enormous Madonnas begin to pull that tradition apart.

Go back out to the corridor and walk past the vestibule you came in by. The next doorway on the same side is Room A4, the big room with three giant gold Madonnas.

### 004 · A4 · Three Maestàs
*Room A4 · 262 words*

**Where:** Room A4, "Giotto, Cimabue": the large room with three huge altarpieces of the Virgin and Child enthroned on gold, each over three metres tall. Stand in the middle, where you can see all three.

**What:** Cimabue, Santa Trinita Maestà, c. 1290–1300, 384 × 223 cm; Duccio di Buoninsegna, Rucellai Madonna, c. 1285, 450 × 290 cm; Giotto, Ognissanti Maestà, c. 1300–1305, 325 × 204 cm; all tempera on wood, gold background. Dante, Purgatorio XI, 94–96 (Longfellow translation).

Dante used two of the painters in this room as a warning about pride.

In his Purgatory, on the terrace where the proud are purged, the illuminator Oderisi tells the poet how quickly fame fades. In Longfellow's translation, he says: "In painting Cimabue thought that he should hold the field, now Giotto has the cry, so that the other's fame is growing dim."

Both men are here. So is a third great painter, Duccio, from Siena. All three painted the same subject: the Virgin and Child enthroned in majesty, a Maestà. All three are gigantic, from about three and a quarter metres to four and a half. On the museum's own dates, they were painted within about twenty years of each other, from the mid twelve-eighties to the early thirteen hundreds. And the earliest is not Cimabue's. It's Duccio's.

So don't think of an old master followed by his pupils in a neat line. Think of three different approaches, side by side. To compare them, look at three things. First, the throne: is it a flat shape, or can you feel space inside it? Then the angels: are they a pattern, or a crowd? And last, the bodies: do they float, or do they sit with real weight?

Take your time between them. Much of what you'll see on this floor grows out of what happened in this room.

Dante thought fame was fleeting. Seven centuries on, all three names still draw a crowd.

Stay in Room A4. Start with Cimabue's panel: the one with four prophets in arches under the throne.

### 005 · A4 · Cimabue — Santa Trinita Maestà
*Room A4 · 284 words*

**Where:** In Room A4, in front of Cimabue's Maestà: the tall gold panel, nearly four metres high, with eight angels lifting the throne and four prophets standing in arches beneath it.

**What:** Cimabue, Virgin and Child Enthroned, and Prophets (Santa Trinita Maestà), c. 1290–1300, tempera on wood, gold background, 384 × 223 cm, Uffizi inv. 1890 no. 8343.

For a long time, this was where the story began: Cimabue, the grand old master, and then everyone else. The museum's own dating has quietly changed that.

The Uffizi calls Cimabue the most important Florentine painter of the thirteenth century. He painted this for the Vallombrosan monks of Santa Trinita. Today it's dated to about 1290 to 1300, late in his career. That makes it later than Duccio's Madonna in this same room.

Read it from the bottom up. Under the throne, in four arches, stand four Old Testament prophets: Jeremiah, Abraham, David and Isaiah. Their scrolls carry texts about the Incarnation and Mary's virginity. Above them rises an ivory throne, built up like a piece of architecture. Eight angels with wonderful many-coloured wings lift it gently on either side.

The Virgin's right hand points to her son. It's an old Byzantine formula. She is showing you the way to salvation. The Child is dressed like an ancient philosopher, blessing, with a rolled scroll in his hand. Their robes are traced with fine lines of gold called damascene, and even the gold background is engraved with geometric patterns.

Now look again, at the folds of the cloth, the soft shadows, the gentle faces. That is a new naturalism. The museum links it to younger painters, to Duccio, and above all to Giotto, who was Cimabue's pupil.

Vasari tells how Cimabue found a shepherd boy drawing sheep on a stone and took him on as an apprentice. That boy was Giotto. Whether or not it happened, the pupil's painting is waiting in this same room.

Stay in Room A4 for Duccio's Rucellai Madonna, the largest of the three, with six kneeling angels holding the throne.

### 006 · A4 · Duccio — Rucellai Madonna
*Room A4 · 290 words*

**Where:** In Room A4, in front of the largest of the three Maestàs, four and a half metres tall: the Virgin on a slender throne held by six kneeling angels, three on each side, with small roundels of saints around the frame.

**What:** Duccio di Buoninsegna, Virgin and Child enthroned, surrounded by angels (Rucellai Madonna), c. 1285, tempera on wood, gold background, 450 × 290 cm; on deposit from the church of Santa Maria Novella, Florence.

For about three hundred years, this was thought to be a Cimabue. It isn't.

In 1285 a confraternity called the Laudesi ordered a great altarpiece from a painter from Siena, Duccio di Buoninsegna. They met in the church of Santa Maria Novella to sing the praises of the Virgin. Centuries later, Vasari described it and gave it to Cimabue. The name stuck to the wrong man.

Then the paperwork surfaced. In 1854 the scholar Gaetano Milanesi realised that an old published document was the actual contract, made out to Duccio. Many still refused to believe it. The contract was published again, the argument went on, and only an exhibition here at the Uffizi in 1937 closed the debate for good. And the nickname? From the late fifteen hundreds it hung in the chapel of the Rucellai family.

It's the largest painting on wood from the thirteenth century known today. Six angels kneel around the throne, three on each side, and they seem to float in the gold. Small roundels of saints run round the frame. Near the bottom, look for two figures in black and white: Saint Dominic and Saint Peter Martyr, because Santa Maria Novella was a Dominican church.

Now find the gilded edge of the Virgin's dark mantle and follow it down. It doesn't run straight. It curls and ripples the whole length of her figure, a line drawn for the pleasure of drawing it. Cimabue never painted an edge like that. It's a Sienese delight in line. Even the Child's red cloth is woven with gold.

Three centuries under another man's name, and Duccio's signature was in that line all along.

Stay in Room A4 for Giotto's Maestà, where angels kneel holding vases of roses and lilies.

### 007 · A4 · Giotto — Ognissanti Maestà
*Room A4 · 282 words*

**Where:** In Room A4, in front of Giotto's Maestà, the smallest of the three at just over three metres: the Virgin seated in a pointed marble tabernacle, with two angels kneeling at her feet holding vases of flowers.

**What:** Giotto, Virgin and Child enthroned, surrounded by angels and saints (Ognissanti Maestà), c. 1300–1305, tempera on wood, gold background, 325 × 204 cm, Uffizi inv. 1890 no. 8344.

Some of the halos in this painting are partly hidden, by other heads and by the throne itself.

That small fact is the revolution. If one thing can hide another, there is space between them. Giotto painted this around 1300 to 1305 for the church of Ognissanti, All Saints, run by the religious order of the Humiliati. Nobody knows exactly where it stood inside the church.

Mary sits like a queen. Around her rises a pointed tabernacle of inlaid, many-coloured marble, a piece of Gothic architecture you could almost walk into. The saints and angels stand inside it, and they overlap. Now look at her body. Giotto keeps the decoration on her clothes to a minimum, so that light and shade can do the work. You can sense her knees and her lap, and the weight settling into the seat.

The angels have jobs. The two kneeling at the foot of the throne offer vases of roses and lilies, for charity and purity. The angels beside the throne hold out a crown and a pyx, a small lidded vessel that probably points to Christ's Passion.

Vasari tells us that Giotto brought painting back to nature. That's a writer looking back two and a half centuries later. The museum's own verdict is just as strong: for over a century, this composition was a model for Florentine painters. You can almost date modern painting from this panel.

Dante wrote that Giotto now "has the cry", the fame that had been Cimabue's. Standing here, it's hard to argue.

Room A5 opens off this room, through the doorway near the corridor in the wall toward the staircase you came up. Look for a tall gold Annunciation.

### 008 · A5 · Siena: the Lorenzetti brothers
*Room A5 · 207 words*

**Where:** Room A5, "Lorenzetti, Simone Martini": a room of Sienese gold-ground paintings. Ambrogio's Presentation in the Temple is the tall panel showing a church interior with columns and a starry vault; Pietro's smaller Madonna shows mother and child gazing at each other among angels.

**What:** Ambrogio Lorenzetti, Presentation at the Temple, 1342, tempera on wood, gold background, 257 × 168 cm, inv. 1890 no. 8346; Pietro Lorenzetti, Virgin and Child enthroned, with angels, c. 1340, tempera on panel, 145 × 122 cm, inv. 1890 no. 445.

If Florence chose weight, Siena chose grace. This room is Siena's answer, from the same decades as Giotto.

Siena was Florence's great rival, and its painters kept faith with gold, flowing line and jewel-bright colour. Two of the finest were brothers, Pietro and Ambrogio Lorenzetti. Ambrogio alone painted the famous frescoes of Good and Bad Government in Siena's town hall. Both brothers vanish from the records in 1348, the year of the Black Death. They probably died in the plague.

Find Ambrogio's Presentation in the Temple, the tall panel with a church interior. It's signed and dated 1342, and it was made for an altar in Siena Cathedral. He shows the temple inside and out at the same time, with columns and starry vaults. The lines of the coloured marble floor run back into depth. Then come closer. The women are wearing earrings. And the baby Jesus is sucking his thumb.

Pietro's Madonna, from about 1340, is quieter. Mother and son exchange a long, tender look. The Child strokes her face and holds on to her fingers.

Pietro has one more painting nearby, his Saint Humility, in Room A6.

Stay in Room A5 for Simone Martini's Annunciation, with the angel's words written in gold across the air.

### 009 · A5 · Simone Martini and Lippo Memmi — Annunciation
*Room A5 · 322 words*

**Where:** In Room A5, in front of the great gold Annunciation, nearly two metres high: the angel kneeling on one side, the Virgin drawing back on the other, a vase of lilies between them and words in raised gold running across the gold ground, with a standing saint in each side panel.

**What:** Simone Martini and Lippo Memmi, Annunciation with St. Maxima and St. Ansanus, 1333, tempera on wood, gold background, 184 × 168 cm, Uffizi inv. 1890 nos. 451, 452, 453.

Simone Martini didn't leave the angel's greeting to your imagination. He wrote the words themselves across the air, in raised gold.

The greeting begins "Ave gratia plena": hail, full of grace, the Lord is with you. Gabriel has just arrived, his cloak still fluttering and his wings spread. The greeting runs from his lips towards the Virgin, so your eye follows the path of the sound.

Now look at Mary. This isn't calm acceptance. She is distressed. She draws back and wraps herself in her dark blue cloak. Her book is still in her hand. It's a very human flinch, at the most solemn moment in Christian art.

Simone was a star. He was a friend of the poet Petrarch, and he worked for kings and for the popes in Avignon. He painted this in 1333 for Siena Cathedral, with Lippo Memmi. They shared a workshop, and Simone married Lippo's sister. Both names are in the signature, but critics give most of the work to Simone. The two side saints are often assigned to Lippo.

One of those saints is Ansanus, a patron saint of Siena, holding the city's banner. The other is a puzzle. She may be Maxima, his mother, or Saint Margaret. The name written at her feet says Judith, and that is wrong.

Between angel and Virgin stands a vase of lilies, for her purity. The angel holds an olive branch, a sign of peace.

Almost seven hundred years on, the greeting still hangs in the air.

Room A6 has a short Extra on Florence in the fourteenth century: it's through the doorway at the back of this room, away from the corridor. The next main stop is Room A7: go back into the Maestà room, A4. On its far side, away from the corridor, are two doorways side by side. Take the one on the Room A5 side, into Room A7, with a huge gold procession of kings.

### 010 · A6 · Extra · Florence in the fourteenth century
*Room A6 · 214 words*

**Where:** Room A6, "14th Century", through the doorway at the back of Room A5. Look for Pietro Lorenzetti's tall gold altarpiece of a nun in a dark habit, full length in the centre, surrounded by small story scenes.

**What:** Pietro Lorenzetti, Saint Humility and scenes from her life, c. 1335–1340, tempera on wood, gold background, 257 × 168 cm, inv. 1890 nn. 6120–6126, 6129–6131, 8347. Not on display (restoration, no return date): Giovanni da Milano, Ognissanti Polyptych, and Giottino, Pietà di San Remigio.

This room is about Florence after Giotto. But its most striking painting today is by a Sienese, and its heroine came from Faenza.

Pietro Lorenzetti painted Saint Humility and the stories of her life around 1335. Humility was a noblewoman who became a nun late in life, and came to Florence to found a monastery. She stands in the centre, full length, with a book and a palm of glory. On her head is a sheepskin, the emblem of humility. The small kneeling woman at the bottom left is probably the person who paid for the painting.

The little scenes around her follow her life, from taking the habit, through miracles in her convent, to the journey to Florence and her funeral. When an imperial army besieged Florence in 1529, the nuns left their convent and carried this painting with them.

Two of this room's key works are away for restoration: Giovanni da Milano's Ognissanti Polyptych and Giottino's Pietà of San Remigio.

Room A7, Gentile da Fabriano, is next door. If the doorway at the back of this room, away from Room A5, leads into A7, take it. Otherwise go back through A5 into A4, and take the one of the two far-side doorways that leads into A7. Look for the huge gold procession.

### 011 · A7 · Lorenzo Monaco — Coronation of the Virgin
*Room A7 · 238 words*

**Where:** Room A7, "Lorenzo Monaco, Gentile da Fabriano". Lorenzo Monaco's Coronation is the huge gold altarpiece, four and a half metres high, with Christ crowning the Virgin above a striped, starry rainbow and two saints in white kneeling at the sides.

**What:** Lorenzo Monaco, Coronation of the Virgin, 1414 (inscribed February 1413, Florentine calendar), tempera on wood, gold background, 450 × 350 cm, inv. 1890 n. 885. Also in the room: Gentile da Fabriano, Adoration of the Magi, 1423.

Around the year 1400, painters from Paris to Prague, Milan and Florence shared one courtly style. We call it International Gothic: rich fabrics, swaying figures, jewel colours, and a love of detail for its own sake.

In Florence, one of its great voices was a monk. Lorenzo Monaco simply means Lorenzo the Monk. He belonged to the Camaldolese abbey of Santa Maria degli Angeli, here in Florence. He painted this Coronation of the Virgin for the high altar of his own abbey church. The inscription says February 1413. But Florence began its new year in late March, so by our calendar it's February 1414.

It's enormous, four and a half metres high. Christ crowns his mother, high on a starry rainbow that stands for the heavenly spheres. Angels and crowds of saints look on. Kneeling at the sides are Saint Benedict and Saint Romuald, in the white habit of Lorenzo's own order. He had trained as a painter of miniatures, and you can see it in the slender figures and the long, clean lines of their robes. Along the bottom, small scenes show the Nativity, the Adoration of the Magi, and the life of Saint Benedict.

The other great painting in this room is one of the most spectacular works of the whole International Gothic style.

Stay in Room A7 for Gentile da Fabriano's Adoration of the Magi, with the youngest king having his golden spurs taken off.

### 012 · A7 · Gentile da Fabriano — Adoration of the Magi
*Room A7 · 309 words*

**Where:** In Room A7, in front of the great gold Adoration, three metres high and almost as wide, under a frame of three pointed arches: a long procession of kings, horses and animals winding down to the Holy Family on the left, with a row of small scenes along the base.

**What:** Gentile da Fabriano, Adoration of the Magi, signed and dated May 1423, tempera on wood, 300 × 282 cm, Uffizi inv. 1890 n. 8364; the predella Presentation in the Temple is a modern copy of the original in the Louvre.

Gentile da Fabriano signed this painting with the month as well as the year: May 1423. Perhaps he knew it was something special.

It was ordered by Palla Strozzi, a banker and refined lover of art, one of the richest men in Florence, for his family's chapel in the sacristy of Santa Trinita. And it is a performance of wealth.

Read it like a story. Up in the three arches at the top, the journey of the Magi unfolds in three separate episodes. Then the procession winds down through the hills into the foreground. It's a crowd from many lands, with exotic animals to show how far the kings have come. At the left, the eldest king kneels at the Child's feet. The youngest, in the most dazzling clothes of all, waits while his golden spurs are taken off.

Come close to those spurs. Gentile embossed the metal leaf so that it stands up in relief from the surface, on the spurs and on a sword hilt. And the Strozzi themselves may be somewhere in that crowd.

Now drop your eyes to the row of small scenes along the base: the Nativity, the Flight into Egypt and the Presentation in the Temple. Here the gold sky gives way to blue. The Nativity is a night scene, and the light seems to come from the Child. One more thing. The Presentation, the last scene on the right, is a modern copy. The original is in the Louvre.

The museum calls this the most important example of International Gothic painting in Italy. It's also a farewell. In the next room, the gold starts to come down.

Go back into Room A4. Room A8 is the other doorway on A4's far side, next to the one you came through. Look for Masolino and Masaccio's Saint Anne, with the Virgin on her lap.

## Second floor · Early Renaissance

### 013 · A8 · Masolino, Masaccio and Fra Angelico
*Room A8 · 234 words*

**Where:** Room A8, "Masaccio, Beato Angelico, P. Uccello". The Saint Anne is the gold panel with Saint Anne seated high behind the Virgin, who holds the Child on her lap; Uccello's wide battle scene is also in this room.

**What:** Masolino and Masaccio, Saint Anne, Madonna and Child, five angels ("Sant'Anna Metterza"), c. 1424–1425, tempera on wood, 175 × 103 cm, inv. 1890 no. 8386; Fra Angelico, Glorification of Mary, 1431–1435, with its predella, inv. 1890 no. 1612; Fra Angelico, Madonna and Child Enthroned with Angels, 1435, from Pontassieve, Deposits no. 143.

In this room the gold begins to come down, and a new kind of space comes in. It starts with two painters who were both called Tommaso.

Masolino and Masaccio painted the Saint Anne with the Virgin and Child together, around 1424, for the church of Sant'Ambrogio, according to Vasari. Its old name, Sant'Anna Metterza, means Anne "in third place": grandmother, mother and son, one above the other. Vasari gave the whole panel to Masaccio. Today the stern Saint Anne is given to Masolino, and the Virgin and Child to Masaccio. Look at that Child. He's solid and heavy, almost a little Hercules. Masaccio was in his early twenties. He died at twenty-six.

Fra Angelico, a Dominican friar, takes you somewhere gentler. His Glorification of Mary is crowded with more than forty saints, each one an individual. Christ isn't crowning his mother. He's adding a precious stone to her diadem. Its predella was separated from it more than four centuries ago, and was reunited with it here in 2024. Look too for his Madonna from Pontassieve, of 1435, where the Child's pale skin almost glows.

And Paolo Uccello? Vasari tells that he sat up all night over his perspective. When his wife called him to bed, he answered, "What a sweet thing this perspective is."

Stay in Room A8 for Paolo Uccello's Battle of San Romano, the wide panel of horses and broken lances.

### 014 · A8 · Paolo Uccello — The Battle of San Romano
*Room A8 · 308 words*

**Where:** In Room A8, in front of the wide battle panel, more than three metres across: armoured horsemen, a thicket of raised and broken lances, fallen soldiers on the ground and small orange trees filling the top corners.

**What:** Paolo Uccello, Battle of San Romano, c. 1435–1440, tempera on wood, 182 × 323 cm, Uffizi inv. 1890 n. 479, signed "PAULI UGIELI OPUS"; the central panel of a set of three, with the others in the National Gallery, London, and the Louvre, Paris.

Those orange trees in the top corners were never part of the original plan.

The panel first had an arched top, to fit a room in the palace of Lionardo Bartolini Salimbeni. He ordered three battle scenes after Florence beat the Sienese and their allies, led by the Duke of Milan, at San Romano near Pisa in 1432. Later his heirs sold the set to Lorenzo the Magnificent, who hung it in the Medici palace on Via Larga. The shape had to change. The arches were squared off, probably by Uccello himself, and the new corners were filled with sour oranges. In Latin they're mala medica, a pun on the family name.

This is the middle of the story. The first panel is in London and the last is in Paris. Here, Niccolò da Tolentino, commander of the Florentine army, unseats the enemy leader, Bernardino della Carda.

Now read the lances. The Florentines' lances and crossbows point forward. The enemy's tilt back. The battle is already decided in the geometry. Uccello was a virtuoso of perspective, and the bodies on the ground are foreshortened, shrinking away into the picture. They suggest a low viewpoint, for a panel made to be seen from below.

The armour and the bridles were covered in metal leaf. It once glittered, and it has darkened with time. Down at the bottom left, he signed it in Latin: the work of Paolo Uccello.

A battle where hardly anyone bleeds, and the real winner is perspective.

The Botticelli rooms come next. Follow the museum's Botticelli signs from Room A8. If the way through is closed, go back through Room A4 to the corridor, walk on to Room A13, with its big Flemish altarpiece, and enter the Botticelli rooms from the far end. Either way, begin in the room lined with painted Virtues in gilded frames.

## Second floor · The Botticelli rooms and the Tribune

### 015 · Botticelli rooms · Pollaiolo and Botticelli — Fortitude
*Botticelli rooms · 250 words*

**Where:** In the first of the Botticelli rooms, the room of painted Virtues: a row of seated women in tall gilded frames along one wall. Botticelli's Fortitude is the young woman wearing armour over her dress, with a sceptre in her hand.

**What:** Sandro Botticelli, Fortitude, 1470, tempera grassa on poplar, 167 × 87 cm, inv. 1890 n. 1606; with Piero del Pollaiolo's Virtues for the Tribunale di Mercanzia, among them Charity, 1469–1470, tempera grassa on wood, 168 × 90.5 cm, inv. 1890 no. 499; also in the room, Portrait of a Lady, attributed to Piero del Pollaiolo, c. 1475–1480.

Botticelli's story in these rooms begins with a job that was never supposed to be his.

In 1469 the merchants' court of Florence, the Tribunale di Mercanzia, ordered a set of seven Virtues for its hall on Piazza della Signoria. The commission went to Piero del Pollaiolo. But one of the men in charge, Tommaso Soderini, steered one panel to the young Botticelli. Pollaiolo protested, and Botticelli was allowed this single figure. The museum calls the protests understandable.

Find her: Fortitude, the young woman wearing armour over her graceful dress, with a ruler's sceptre in her hand. She's dated 1470, Botticelli's first documented work. Her body is long and sinuous, her face languid and melancholy. You'll meet that face again. Even the wood is different. Pollaiolo's Virtues are on cypress, hers is on poplar. ### 018 · Botticelli rooms · Botticelli — The Birth of Venus
*Botticelli rooms · 308 words*

**Where:** In the Botticelli rooms, in front of the Birth of Venus, facing the Primavera across the passage: a wide, pale canvas almost three metres across, the goddess standing on a scallop shell between the winds on the left and a woman holding out a flowered cloak on the right. **What:** Sandro Botticelli, Birth of Venus, c. 1485, tempera on canvas, 172.5 × 278.5 cm, Uffizi inv. 1890 n. 878; flanked by the Madonna of the Magnificat and the Madonna of the Pomegranate.

She's only just arrived. The wind is still blowing, the roses are still falling, and her hair hasn't settled.

This is Venus, goddess of love and beauty, coming ashore on the island of Cyprus. She was born from the foam of the sea, and here she stands on a giant scallop shell. On the left, the wind Zephyr blows her in, wrapped together with a companion, perhaps the breeze Aura. On the right, a young woman hurries to cover her with a cloak scattered with flowers. She may be the Hora of spring, or one of the Graces.

A near-life-size nude goddess was still a rarity in Florence in the 1480s. The subject was perhaps suggested by the poet Agnolo Poliziano. The painting is often read through the Neoplatonic ideas of the Medici circle. But Botticelli also borrowed. Venus's modest pose comes from ancient statues. And the pair of winds is based on a Hellenistic carved gem that belonged to Lorenzo the Magnificent.

Now notice the surface, pale and matte. This is painted on canvas, not on wood. That wasn't a daring first: canvas was widely used in fifteenth-century Florence for decorative pictures in noble houses, and it helps explain that soft, chalky look. Vasari saw it in 1550 in the Medici villa at Castello, beside the Primavera.

Now the two are together again, facing each other, in new airtight cases instead of the old glass. Look from the goddess to the Madonna of the Magnificat, the round painting beside her where Mary is writing in a book, and compare the faces. They are strikingly alike.

### 020 · A13 · Hugo van der Goes — The Portinari Triptych
*Room A13 · 308 words*

**Where:** Room A13, "Hugo van der Goes". Stand back from the one enormous three-part altarpiece that fills the room, more than six metres wide when open: a newborn Child on the bare ground at the centre, a kneeling family with towering saints on the wings, and a vase of flowers at the front.

**What:** Hugo van der Goes, Adoration of the Shepherds with angels and Saint Thomas, Saint Anthony, Saint Margaret, Mary Magdalen and the Portinari family (recto); Annunciation (verso), known as the Portinari Triptych, c. 1477–1478, oil on wood, 274 × 652 cm open, 1890 nn. 3191, 3192, 3193.

In 1483 a painting arrived in Florence from Flanders, shipped by sea to Pisa, and it made every painter in the city stop and stare. This is the painting.

Tommaso Portinari ran the Medici bank in Bruges, and there he ordered this huge altarpiece from Hugo van der Goes, who painted it around 1477 or 1478. It was for his family's chapel in Sant'Egidio, the church of the hospital of Santa Maria Nuova, here in Florence. Florentine painters found it extraordinarily new, and the museum says they especially praised its realism.

The family kneel on the wings, in Flemish clothes. On the left are Tommaso and his sons, Antonio and Pigello, with Saint Thomas and Saint Anthony looming behind them. On the right are his wife, Maria Baroncelli, and their daughter Margherita, with Saint Margaret and Mary Magdalene. Notice the sizes. The saints tower over the family. Size here means rank, not distance.

Now the centre. The Child lies on the bare ground in rays of light, the Light of the World. In front of him stands a still life. A white iris and a red lily, for purity and for Christ's blood. Purple columbines, for the Virgin's sorrows. Red carnations, for the Trinity. Every petal is exact.

Then look to the right of the centre, at the shepherds. Rough, open-mouthed working men, hurrying in from the fields. Florence noticed them. Ghirlandaio's Adoration in the Sassetti Chapel, painted two years later, seems to borrow these very shepherds.

A landmark in Florence's encounter with Flemish oil painting, and it began as one family's chapel picture.

### 022 · A16 · The Tribune
*Room A16 · 326 words*

**Where:** At a doorway of the Tribune, Room A16: a small octagonal room with red velvet walls, a dome shining with mother-of-pearl and a floor of coloured marble. The life-size marble goddess covering herself with her hands is the Medici Venus.

In 1802, the most admired woman in Europe was packed up and carried off to Paris. She stands in this room. In 1816, she came home.

Bernardo Buontalenti built the Tribune in the early 1580s for Grand Duke Francesco the First, as a place for his jewels and treasures. It's an octagon, because eight was the number that draws near to Heaven. And Francesco planned it as a whole cosmos of the four elements.

For two centuries this little room was the high point of the Grand Tour. Titian's Venus of Urbino hung near her for years. You'll meet her downstairs.

### 025 · A25 · Piero della Francesca — The Duke and Duchess of Urbino
*Room A25 · 286 words*

**Where:** Room A25. Stand in front of the two small panels in one frame, each about the size of a large book: a pale woman on the left and a man in a red cap on the right, facing each other in strict profile, with one wide landscape running behind them both.

**What:** Piero della Francesca, The Duke and Duchess of Urbino Federico da Montefeltro and Battista Sforza, c. 1473–1475, oil on wood, 47 × 33 cm each, 1890 nn. 1615, 3342; reverses painted with triumphs, the Virtues and Latin inscriptions.

These two have faced each other for more than five hundred years, and neither has shown a flicker of feeling. That was the point.

Piero della Francesca painted Federico da Montefeltro, Duke of Urbino, and his wife, Battista Sforza, around 1473. He set them in strict profile, like the rulers on ancient coins. The museum explains why. A profile gives a good, faithful likeness of the face, without letting the sitter's feelings show through. These are not private people. They are a state.

There was a practical reason too. Federico had lost an eye and part of the bridge of his nose. So he is shown from his good side, and look at that nose, with its sharp dent at the top. Piero leaves every mole and wrinkle in place.

Now turn to Battista, on the left. See how pale she is, almost white against the sky. She died in 1472, at twenty-six. So this portrait was very probably painted after her death. The museum suggests her pallor may allude to it. A wife remembered, facing her husband for ever.

Behind them runs one continuous landscape, the hills and rivers of the Marche, the lands they ruled. Notice how the far hills soften into blue haze. Piero was also a mathematician, and he measured even the air.

If you can see the backs of the panels, they show the couple riding in triumph on ancient carts, with the Virtues and Latin verses in their praise.

The pair came to Florence much later, in the dowry of Vittoria della Rovere, when she married into the Medici.

### 026 · A27 · Perugino
*Room A27 · 234 words*

**Where:** Room A27, "Pietro Perugino". Two big, almost square altarpieces, each about one and three-quarter metres across: the Pietà, with the dead Christ held by his mother under a grey stone portico, and the Oration in the Garden, with Christ kneeling on a rock and three apostles asleep below.

That balance was his great gift. If Vasari is right, it also became his trap.

### 029 · A35 · Leonardo: the Baptism
*Room A35 · 240 words*

**Where:** Room A35, "Leonardo Da Vinci". Stand in front of the tall panel, almost two metres high, of John the Baptist pouring water over Christ, who stands in a shallow river; two angels kneel on the bank at the left.

In a busy Florentine workshop, the master designed the picture and the assistants filled in parts of it. This painting is famous because one of those assistants was Leonardo da Vinci.

The master was Andrea del Verrocchio, sculptor, goldsmith and painter. Between about 1470 and 1475 his workshop painted this Baptism, which comes from the Vallombrosan monastery of San Salvi in Florence. Leonardo was then somewhere between eighteen and twenty-three.

Vasari tells the story. He says Leonardo painted the angel on the far left so well that he upset the older Verrocchio. And he claims Verrocchio was so outdone that he gave up painting altogether. It's a legend, but a telling one.

Today, scholars think Leonardo did more than the angel. The museum gives him perhaps the river landscape behind, the golden light, and even the body of Christ. And there may have been an older hand as well. Look at the weaker hands of God at the top, and the dove.

So this is not one artist's picture. It's a workshop caught in the act, with a genius growing up inside it.

**Where:** Room A35. Stand in front of the long, low panel, more than two metres wide and less than one high: the angel kneels on a flowery lawn at the left, the Virgin sits at a carved marble lectern at the right, and dark trees and a misty distance open between them.

**What:** Leonardo da Vinci, Annunciation, c. 1472, oil on wood, 90 × 222 cm, 1890 n. 1618; at the Uffizi since 1867, from San Bartolomeo a Monteoliveto.

Nobody knows who ordered this painting. It reached the Uffizi in 1867, from a small church just outside the city gates. What we do know is that Leonardo was about twenty, and still working in Verrocchio's studio.

The scene is the Annunciation. The angel Gabriel kneels on the left and holds out a lily. The Virgin, on the right, looks up from her book. Between them is an enclosed garden, the old symbol of her purity, in front of a palace.

Now look the way Leonardo looked. Start with the angel's wings. They aren't the usual decorative feathers. The museum says they're based on the wings of a mighty bird of prey. Then the lawn under the angel, full of separate flowers, each one observed. Then the far distance, past the dark trees, where everything fades into a pale, misty blue.

Look at the marble lectern in front of the Virgin. It copies a design by his master Verrocchio, inspired by the tomb of Piero de' Medici, known as Piero the Gouty, in the church of San Lorenzo. A real Florentine monument, slipped into a sacred scene.

Then the famous problem. Look at the Virgin's right arm, reaching for the book. It seems too long. Was the young painter careless? Perhaps not. The museum suggests it was planned to be seen from the right and from lower down, over a side altar. So take a few steps to your right, and look again. See whether the arm settles.

Barely twenty, he was already asking his own questions about how our eyes really see.

Stay in Room A35 for Leonardo's unfinished Adoration of the Magi, the big brown panel.

### 031 · A35 · Leonardo — Adoration of the Magi
*Room A35 · 323 words*

**Where:** Room A35. Stand back from the big, almost square panel, nearly two and a half metres each way, drawn and painted mostly in browns: the Virgin and Child at the centre, a crowd swirling round them, ruins with two staircases on the left and horsemen fighting behind.

**What:** Leonardo da Vinci, Adoration of the Magi (San Donato in Scopeto), c. 1482, drawing in charcoal, watercolour ink and oil on wood, 244 × 240 cm, 1890 n. 1594; restored at the Opificio delle Pietre Dure, 2011–2017.

A document of July 1481 records that Leonardo had taken on this altarpiece, and promised to finish it within thirty months. He never finished it.

It was for the high altar of San Donato a Scopeto, a monastery of the Augustinians just outside the walls. That September he was still at work on it. Then he left Florence for Milan, and the court of Ludovico Sforza. The Augustinians finally hired Filippino Lippi. His Adoration hangs in Room A28, back along this floor.

What's left is charcoal, ink and oil, straight on the wood. The restoration showed that, unusually for his day, he worked the composition out directly on the panel.

Look at the centre. The Virgin and Child sit calmly, and the kings kneel with their gifts. Everyone else leans in, reaches out, turns in wonder. Compare Gentile da Fabriano's orderly procession, earlier on this floor. Here a whole crowd reacts at once.

Behind them, the picture boils over. On the left, a ruined building with two staircases climbing to nothing. On the right, horsemen clash.

For a long time it looked almost brown all over. In 2011 it went to the conservators of the Opificio delle Pietre Dure. Earlier restorers had coated it in glue, varnish and egg white, and those layers were pulling the paint away from the panel. The cleaning took off only what was not original, and under a tinted old varnish, a landscape appeared, typically Leonardo in its depth and atmosphere.

It came back to the Uffizi in 2017. Look into the background now: visitors before then could hardly make it out.

The next main stop is Room A38, the next doorway along the corridor, with Michelangelo's round Doni Tondo.

## Second floor · Raphael, Michelangelo and the Niobe Room

### 032 · A37 · Extra · The Cabinet of Marbles
*Room A37 · 226 words*

**Where:** Room A37, the Cabinet of Marbles, beside the top of the Buontalenti staircase: a deep red room with ancient reliefs set into the walls. Look for the marble boy, a little under a metre tall, sitting on a rock and pulling a thorn from his foot.

**What:** Boy with Thorn, also known as Spinario, 1st century BC, Greek marble with additions in Carrara marble, h. 84 cm. Room rearranged in July 2024: twelve Roman reliefs back in their original places, and other ancient marbles.

This boy has been trying to get a thorn out of his foot for about two thousand years.

The room began as an open terrace, built in 1658, and in 1825 it was roofed over to make space for more antiquities. In the summer of 2024 the museum rearranged it again, and set twelve ancient reliefs, almost all Roman, back into their original places on the walls.

The Spinario is a Roman marble from the first century BC, copying a Greek idea. He sits on a rock, pulling at the sole of his foot. The museum suggests the thorn came from treading grapes at the harvest. Seven copies of him are known. The most famous version, perhaps even the original, is a bronze in the Capitoline Museums in Rome, admired there since the Middle Ages.

Look at how ordinary the moment is. No god, no hero, just a boy and a sore foot.

The 2024 rearrangement also brought out a small seated figure of the Greek poet Menander, one of only three known copies. It also brought out a dark green basalt torso, perhaps the finest copy of a famous Greek spear-bearer by Polykleitos.

Back to the corridor and turn left. The next doorway is Room A38: look for the round painting of the Holy Family in its carved gold frame. Don't take the Buontalenti staircase.

### 033 · A38 · Raphael and Michelangelo
*Room A38 · 236 words*

**Where:** Room A38, "Raphael and Michelangelo". Michelangelo's round Holy Family in its carved gold frame is the work to find first; Raphael's Madonna of the Goldfinch and his large canvas of the boy Saint John in a cave, in a leopard skin, are in the same room, with two tiny panels by Fra Bartolomeo.

**What:** Overview of Room A38. Raphael, St John the Baptist as a Boy, c. 1518, oil on canvas, 163 × 147 cm. Fra Bartolomeo, Adoration of the Child and Presentation in the Temple, c. 1497, oil on wood, about 20 × 9 cm each, 1890 no. 1477. Also the Doni Tondo (034) and the Madonna of the Goldfinch (035).

Michelangelo was born in 1475, Raphael eight years later. In Florence they were rivals. In this room, they hang a few steps apart.

Michelangelo always thought of himself as a sculptor first. The round Holy Family here, the Doni Tondo, is the only finished panel painting by him to survive. Raphael is the opposite temperament: calm, gracious, a brilliant absorber of everything he saw. His Madonna of the Goldfinch is in this room too. Each has its own stop.

Before those, two works that people walk past. The big canvas of a boy in a cave is Raphael's Saint John the Baptist, from about 1518, made in Rome. Vasari says a cardinal, Pompeo Colonna, ordered it. John sits in a leopard skin and points to a glowing cross tied to an oak. Some critics see the hands of Raphael's pupils in it as well.

And look for two very small panels, each about the height of your hand. Fra Bartolomeo painted them around 1497 as the doors of a little shrine, with the Adoration of the Child and the Presentation in the Temple. Vasari says a marble Madonna by Donatello once sat between them. It is now identified with a relief in London's Victoria and Albert Museum.

Raphael's own self-portrait waits downstairs, in the first self-portrait room.

Stay in Room A38 for the Doni Tondo, the round Holy Family with nude figures behind a low wall.

### 034 · A38 · Michelangelo — The Doni Tondo
*Room A38 · 315 words*

**Where:** Room A38. Stand in front of the round painting, 120 centimetres across, in its deep carved and gilded frame: Mary twisting round to take the Child from Joseph, in hard bright colours, with a row of nude young men behind a low wall.

**What:** Michelangelo Buonarroti, Holy Family, known as the "Doni Tondo", about 1505–1507 (page: 1505–1506), tempera grassa on wood, diameter 120 cm, 1890 n. 1456; frame carved by Francesco del Tasso, probably to Michelangelo's design.

Michelangelo carved in marble. When he did agree to paint, he painted like a sculptor. Here you can see it, in the only finished panel painting by him that survives.

It was made for Agnolo Doni, a rich Florentine merchant, who married Maddalena Strozzi in 1504. The museum links it to their marriage and to the birth of their first child. Michelangelo had already carved round reliefs of the Virgin in marble. Now he did it in paint.

Look at the family. Mary kneels in front and twists round over her shoulder. The Child is passed between her and Joseph, and the three bodies lock together like a dome. Her bare arm is as strong as an athlete's. The colours are hard and bright, like polished stone.

Then look behind them. A low wall runs across the picture, and beyond it lounge young nude men. They seem to stand for the pagan world, before Christ. The wall may stand for original sin. And there, between the two worlds, is the little John the Baptist, perhaps a hint that the painting celebrated a christening. Signorelli's Madonna in Room A32 also has nudes in its background. Michelangelo had looked closely.

One of those nudes gives a date. The famous ancient statue of the Laocoön was dug up in Rome in January 1506, and its echo appears among these figures. That fits the birth of the couple's daughter, Maria, in September 1507.

Now the frame. It was probably designed by Michelangelo himself, and carved by Francesco del Tasso. Christ's head and four prophets look out from among the leaves. And at the top left, hidden in the carving, are half-moons, the emblem of the bride's family, the Strozzi.

A sculptor's painting for one family's happiest years.

Stay in Room A38 for Raphael's Madonna of the Goldfinch: the little Saint John holding out a small bird to the Child.

### 035 · A38 · Raphael — Madonna of the Goldfinch
*Room A38 · 276 words*

**Where:** Room A38. Stand in front of the upright panel, about a metre high: the Virgin in red and blue, seated in a soft landscape with a small book, and two little boys at her knees, one holding out a tiny bird to the other.

**What:** Raphael, Mary, Christ and the young John the Baptist, known as the "Madonna of the Goldfinch", before February 1506, oil on wood, 107 × 77.2 cm, 1890 n. 1447.

This was a wedding present. Vasari tells us Raphael painted it for Lorenzo Nasi, who married in Florence in February 1506.

Raphael was in his early twenties, newly arrived in the city and taking in everything. Look at the shape of the group. The Virgin sits at the top, and the two children settle at her knees, so the three make a calm, steady pyramid. The museum traces that shape to a lost drawing by Leonardo of the Virgin with Saint Anne, shown in a Florentine church, and to a marble Madonna by Michelangelo. And the softness, the way the faces melt into shadow and the hills dissolve into haze, is Leonardo's sfumato.

Now the bird. The little Saint John holds out a goldfinch, and the Christ Child reaches to touch it. It looks like a game. But the goldfinch was linked to the Passion, so this fragile bird foreshadows Christ's sacrifice. Even the small book in Mary's hand points the same way.

Then disaster. In 1547 a landslide destroyed the Nasi family house, and this panel broke into seventeen pieces. It was probably given to the painter Michele di Ridolfo del Ghirlandaio to put back together. After that, it vanishes from the record for a century, until it turns up in the collection of a Medici cardinal.

It was restored again early in this century. Someone, long ago, gathered up the pieces of a wedding present and refused to let it go.

A painting about a fragile future, which very nearly had none.

Back in the corridor, turn left. The next doorway, Room A39, is the long Niobe Room: a hall of marble figures fleeing.

### 036 · A39 · The Niobe Room
*Room A39 · 316 words*

**Where:** Room A39, the Niobe Room: a long white and gold hall with a coffered ceiling, lined with marble statues of young people running, falling and looking up. Find the tallest group, more than two metres high: a mother pulling her small daughter close.

**What:** The Niobe Room, inaugurated 20 Feb 1780, architects Zanobi del Rosso and Gaspare Maria Paoletti. The Niobids, Roman marbles of the 2nd century AD after a celebrated Greek group, found in Rome in 1583. Niobe with her younger daughter, first half of the 2nd century AD, Pentelic marble, h. 228 cm, 1914 no. 294.

Niobe, queen of Thebes, had fourteen children, and she boasted about it. She mocked the goddess Latona, who had only two. Latona's two were Apollo and Artemis, the archers. They killed every one of Niobe's children.

That is the moment all around you. Sons and daughters run, stumble, fall and look up at arrows you can't see. The tallest group is Niobe herself, pulling her youngest daughter close, trying to shield her. The myth says that afterwards Niobe was turned to stone, and her tears became a spring.

These figures were dug up together early in 1583, in a vineyard near Saint John Lateran in Rome. The Wrestlers you saw in the Tribune came out of the same ground. They are Roman marbles of the second century AD, after a celebrated Greek group. For almost two hundred years they stood in the gardens of the Villa Medici in Rome. Then Grand Duke Peter Leopold had them brought to Florence, and this hall was built for them. It opened in 1780, all white and gold, with gilded roses in the ceiling.

There is a second story in this room. At four minutes past one in the morning, on the 27th of May 1993, a car packed with explosives blew up in Via dei Georgofili, the street just behind this wing. It killed five people: four members of the Nencioni family, Fabrizio, Angela, Nadia and Caterina, and a student, Dario Capolicchio. In the museum, 173 paintings and 56 sculptures were damaged. This room was badly hit.

What you see now has been repaired. Take a moment with Niobe and her daughter. A mother shielding her child. And, just outside these walls, a family that no one could shield.

Back in the corridor, turn left and walk to the far end, past the closed rooms A40 to A42, to the top of the Lanzi staircase. Stop before the stairs.

**What:** The Western Corridor (Third Corridor), ancient statues in facing pairs, ceilings painted over the two centuries after the first bay of 1581; the Lanzi staircase down to the first floor; the panoramic terrace with the café, second floor.

Before 1769, visitors came into the gallery through a small door in this corridor. When the Grand Duke opened it to the public that year, the route was turned round and a new grand entrance was built, the one still in use.

## First floor · Contini Bonacossi collection

### 038 · B2 · The Contini Bonacossi collection
*Room B2 · 246 words*

**Where:** Room B2, at the near end of the long hall of the Contini Bonacossi collection, Room B1, on the side away from the Info Point, beside Room B4. Stand in front of the big, square gold altarpiece, about two and a half metres each way, of the Virgin enthroned with angels and saints. The tall fresco of another enthroned Madonna, with two children offering flowers, is in the same room.

**What:** Stefano di Giovanni, known as Sassetta, Madonna of the Snow Altarpiece, 1432, tempera on panel, 240 × 256 cm, Contini Bonacossi no. 1. Also: Andrea del Castagno, Madonna and Child enthroned with Saints Jerome and John the Baptist, two angels, and two children from the Pazzi family, c. 1449–1450, detached fresco, 290 × 212 cm, Contini Bonacossi no. 2.

Somewhere in this golden altarpiece, an angel is making a snowball.

This is a private collection inside a public museum. Alessandro Contini Bonacossi gathered it in the first half of the twentieth century. His heirs honoured his wish, and in 1969 its 144 paintings, sculptures, ceramics and pieces of furniture passed to the State. Since 2018 they have filled eight rooms here, the long hall among them.

Sassetta painted this altarpiece in 1432 for Siena Cathedral. It tells the legend of the Madonna of the Snow. One August, the story goes, snow fell on a hill in Rome, and the pope traced the plan of a new church in it, Santa Maria Maggiore. Now find the two angels behind the Virgin's throne. One holds a tray of snow. The other is pressing it into a ball.

The other giant in this room is a fresco, lifted off a castle wall more than a century ago. Andrea del Castagno painted it for the chapel of the Pazzi family's castle at Trebbio. The two children offering flowers and a crown of roses are probably young Pazzi. Sienese gold on one side, Florentine figures as solid as sculpture on the other.

Next is Room B4, Giovanni Bellini, right beside this room on the same side of the hall. Look for a large painting of a saint in a rocky desert with his lion. Careful: a second, much smaller Saint Jerome, by Cima da Conegliano, hangs in the same room.

### 039 · B4 · Giovanni Bellini — Saint Jerome in the Desert
*Room B4 · 292 words*

**Where:** Room B4, "Giovanni Bellini". Stand in front of the larger of the two Saint Jeromes: a tall panel, about one and a half metres high, of an old saint reading on a rocky ledge, a lion in its den and a walled city in the distance. Cima da Conegliano's Saint Jerome in the same room is tiny, about a third of a metre high, with a skull at its centre.

**What:** Giovanni Bellini, St. Jerome in the Desert, c. 1480, oil on panel, 151.7 × 113.7 cm, Contini Bonacossi no. 25. Also: Giovanni Battista Cima da Conegliano, St. Jerome in the Desert, c. 1510–1520, oil on panel, 33 × 27.5 cm, Contini Bonacossi no. 19, same room.

There's a lizard in this painting. And a city that never existed.

Saint Jerome was two men in one. He was a scholar, the man who translated the Bible into Latin. And he was a hermit, doing penance in the desert. Giovanni Bellini, the great painter of Venice and traditionally the teacher of Giorgione and Titian, gives you both. Jerome sits reading on a ledge of bare rock. Below him, in its den, rests his lion. The legend says Jerome pulled a thorn from its paw, and it never left him.

Bellini painted it in oil, around 1480, and it's big, about one and a half metres tall. Now look at the city in the distance. It looks real, and it isn't. Bellini built it from pieces of real places. Two famous buildings of Ravenna, the Roman bridge of Rimini, a bell tower from Verona. He probably sketched them himself, then put them together like a dream.

Then come back to the foreground. A thin cross stands upright on the rock, and behind it a lizard is crawling. Lizards sleep through the winter and wake again, so they stood for death and resurrection. A tiny creature, carrying the biggest idea in the picture.

Before you leave, find the other Jerome in this room, by Cima da Conegliano. It's small enough to hold in two hands, with a skull at its centre.

Wild rock on one side, a city on the other, and between them a man reading in peace. Perhaps that was Bellini's hope for both.

Room B5, across the hall, has a short Extra on Bramantino's altarpiece of eight saints. The next main stop is Room B8, at the far end of the hall: Bernini's marble saint lying on his grill.

### 040 · B5 · Extra · Bramantino — Madonna and Child with eight saints
*Room B5 · 207 words*

**Where:** Room B5, "Bramantino", across the hall from Room B4. The large altarpiece, about two metres tall: the Virgin and Child seated on steps under a loggia of columns, with men gathered around them and sky-blue drapery echoing the sky.

**What:** Bartolomeo Suardi, known as Bramantino, Madonna and Child with eight saints, catalogue date c. 1512–1514, oil on panel, 205 × 166.5 cm, Contini Bonacossi no. 3.

Bramantino is a nickname. It means "little Bramante", after the man who trained him in Milan, Donato Bramante, the architect and painter.

You can feel the architect in this picture. The Virgin and Child sit on steps under a loggia of plain columns, and the sky-blue drapery behind them seems to be cut from the sky itself. Then the people. The museum names most of them. Saint Ambrose of Milan stands behind the Virgin with his scourge and his bishop's staff. Close examination shows his bishop's mitre was painted out. The white-bearded Jerome strikes his bare chest with a stone. John the Baptist kneels and looks straight at you. On the right, the saint with the long sword is probably Martin of Tours.

Now look at the front. A poor man, with his back to us, is adoring the Madonna. And a young bearded man in a white shirt and green tunic gazes at the baby. He may be the patron, painted in among the saints.

Bramantino changed his mind often as he worked. Look closely and you can see many of those changes on the surface of the paint.

Walk on to the far end of the hall, Room B8: Bernini's Saint Lawrence on the grill.

### 041 · B8 · Bernini — Saint Lawrence
*Room B8 · 290 words*

**Where:** Room B8, "Bernini", at the far end of the Contini Bonacossi hall. The white marble saint lies stretched out, about a metre long, on a carved wooden base shaped like a tree wrapped in flames.

**What:** Gian Lorenzo Bernini, Saint Lawrence, 1617, white marble with a carved wooden base, 66 × 108 cm, Contini Bonacossi Collection, room B8.

To get this face right, the story goes, the young sculptor burned himself.

Saint Lawrence was a deacon of the early Church in Rome, put to death on an iron grill over a fire. Gian Lorenzo Bernini carved him in 1617, when he was only eighteen or nineteen. Many years later, Bernini's son Domenico wrote his father's life, and he tells the story. To understand the saint's pain, Bernini stood bare-legged on one leg beside smouldering coals. He held a mirror, and with a pencil he drew the expression on his own face.

It's a wonderful story. Now look at the face. It isn't screaming. It's relaxed, turned upward, almost dreaming. Whatever the boy saw in his mirror, what he carved is a man already beyond the fire. That rapt look returns decades later in his Ecstasy of Saint Teresa in Rome.

Then look at the body. Bernini had been studying Michelangelo. The museum sees three of his figures in this one. There's the limp dead Christ of the Pietà in Saint Peter's, a reclining river god, and Adam on the Sistine Chapel ceiling. Bernini takes the abandon of one and the twisting strength of another, and fuses them.

It's smaller than people expect, about a metre long, well under life-size.

And look underneath. The wooden base is carved as a tree wrapped in flames, probably from a design by Bernini himself.

A teenager, quoting the greatest sculptor of the century before him, and already sounding like no one but himself.

Walk back along the hall to the foot of the Lanzi staircase. Don't take the stairs. Go into the long room right beside them, Room C1, where the self-portraits begin: look for a marble cardinal seated near the entrance.

## First floor · Self-portraits

### 042 · C1 · The self-portraits
*Room C1 · 276 words*

**Where:** Room C1, "From the origins to the 17th century", the long room beside the foot of the Lanzi staircase. Stand near the seated marble statue of a cardinal in lace, about 1.6 metres high, twisting in his chair near the entrance. Raphael's small self-portrait, a young man in a dark cap and dark clothes, hangs in this room.

**What:** Giovan Battista Foggini, Cardinal Leopoldo de' Medici, 1697, marble, 160 cm high, inv. 1914 no. 350. Also: Raphael, Self portrait, 1506, oil on panel, 47.3 × 34.8 cm, inv. 1890 n. 1706, room C1.

In these rooms, for once, the artists are the ones looking at you.

Cardinal Leopoldo de' Medici, in the seventeenth century, was known as the Prince of Collectors. Among much else, he collected painters' faces, painted by their own hands. The collection never stopped growing, and today it holds more than two thousand self-portraits. A selection is on view in these rooms.

The cardinal himself is here, in marble. His nephew, Grand Duke Cosimo the Third, ordered it in his honour. Look at the lace on the cassock, the curls, the way the body twists in the chair.

From 1973 to 2016, part of the collection hung inside the Vasari Corridor, where few visitors could see them. Since 2023 they have been here on the main route. When these rooms opened, the walls were painted pink, a nod to the cardinal's robes.

Now find a small panel of a young man in a dark cap and plain dark clothes. That's Raphael, at about twenty-three, the painter of the Goldfinch Madonna upstairs. It's the same face he later gave himself in his School of Athens. The plain clothes are a statement: this is a man at work.

### 043 · C2 · Extra · Bill Viola — Self portrait, Submerged
*Room C2 · 166 words*

**Where:** Room C2, "Water and Light". Look for the tall vertical screen showing a man lying on a riverbed with his eyes closed while water flows over him, in slow motion.

**What:** Bill Viola, Self portrait – Submerged, 2013, high-definition video on a vertical plasma screen with stereo sound, about 11 minutes in slow motion, 121.2 × 72.4 × 9 cm, inv. 1890 no. 10644.

When Bill Viola was a small boy, he nearly drowned in a lake. He couldn't swim. What he remembered afterwards was not the fear. It was the beauty of the light and the colours under the water.

This is his answer to the old question of the self-portrait. No mirror, no brush. Viola lies on the bed of a river, his eyes closed, his face completely relaxed. The water slides over him and now and then lifts the edge of his shirt. Everything moves in slow motion, for about eleven minutes.

Give it a minute. Most people walk straight past a screen.

Viola made it in 2013 and gave it to the Friends of the Uffizi, who gave it to the museum the same year. He died in 2024. So the man under the water, calm and still, is now a portrait of someone who has gone. It feels less like drowning than like rest.

### 044 · C3 · Extra · Painters at work
*Room C3 · 220 words*

**Where:** Room C3, "17th century". Look for a small canvas, about half a metre high, of a painter at his easel in a bare studio, with an old bare-chested man kneeling at a lectern in front of him and a brazier of coals on the floor.

**What:** Michelangelo Cerquozzi, Self-portrait in his studio, c. 1640–1646, oil on canvas, 51 × 42.5 cm, inv. 1890 n. 10721. Rooms C3 "17th century", C4 "Showing the Artwork", C5 "Early 18th century".

On the back of this little canvas, an old inscription gives the painter's nickname: "Michelangelo of the Bambocciate".

Michelangelo Cerquozzi worked in Rome. A bambocciata was a small scene of everyday life, a fashion named after a painter from the north nicknamed Il Bamboccio. Here, in the 1640s, he turns the same eye on himself.

He's at his easel in a bare studio. In front of him an old man, stripped to the waist, kneels at a lectern. He's a model, posing as Saint Jerome. Now look round the room. A marble bust lies on the floor, there to be copied. A brazier of glowing coals warms the bare room. And on the wall hangs a painting of the Good Thief on his cross. He liked ordinary Romans as models, the way Caravaggio did.

It's a self-portrait that shows you not just a face, but a working day.

The next rooms follow the same thread: C4 is called Showing the Artwork, and C5 reaches the early eighteenth century.

## First floor · The 16th century

### 045 · D4 · Correggio and Parmigianino
*Room D4 · 226 words*

**Where:** Room D4, "Correggio and Parmigianino", one of the two rooms at the back of Room D1. ### 046 · D4 · Parmigianino — Madonna with the Long Neck
*Room D4 · 358 words*

**Where:** Room D4, "Correggio and Parmigianino", reached through Room D1. The contract gave Parmigianino five months to paint this altarpiece. He took the rest of his life, and still didn't finish it.

A noblewoman, Elena Baiardi Tagliaferri, ordered it in 1534 for a church in Parma. He died in 1540, at thirty-seven, with the panel still in his studio. When it was set up on its altar, a line in Latin was added at the base of the column. In English it reads, "Adverse destiny prevented Francesco Mazzola from Parma from completing this work". An apology, painted into the picture.

Vasari tells us that in his last years Parmigianino was lost in alchemy. Whether or not that's fair, the picture stopped where he stopped, and that single foot is all there is of a saint.

### 048 · D12 · Pontormo and Rosso

Jacopo Carucci was born in 1494 in Pontorme, near Empoli, and took the village as his name, Pontormo. Giovanni Battista di Jacopo, a year younger, was called Rosso, the redhead. They are among the first painters of what we now call Mannerism: elegant, unsettling, deliberately strange.

Rosso's altarpiece here shows what the fuss was about. In 1518, the prior of the hospital of Santa Maria Nuova ordered it for the altar of a Catalan widow, buried in the church of Ognissanti. Rosso sued him for the money he was still owed.

Now look at Saint Jerome, gaunt and scowling. You can guess what upset the prior. Then look lower, at the two child angels on the steps of the throne, lost in their book. Rosso could be tender too.

### 049 · D12 · Rosso Fiorentino — Moses Defends the Daughters of Jethro
*Room D12 · 311 words*

**Where:** Room D12, "Pontormo - Rosso Fiorentino". Look for a canvas about 1.6 metres high packed with naked, struggling men in the foreground, bodies flung across one another, and a young woman standing behind them.

**What:** Rosso Fiorentino, Moses Defends the Daughters of Jethro, 1523–1527, oil on canvas, 160 × 117 cm, inv. 1890 n. 2151.

This painting was a gift for a king. It may have been commissioned by an enemy of the Medici. And in the end it came home to a Medici prince.

The story is from the Book of Exodus. Moses has killed an Egyptian and fled into the desert. At a well, shepherds drive away the seven daughters of a priest who have come to water their flock. Moses fights the shepherds off, and then draws water for the women. One of them, Zipporah, will become his wife.

Rosso Fiorentino turns it into a brawl. Look at the foreground. Naked bodies are thrown across each other, heads down, limbs everywhere. The space is sliced into tall vertical strips, and that makes the violence feel even closer. The bodies echo the tangle of soldiers in Michelangelo's lost cartoon for the Battle of Cascina.

Now find Zipporah, standing behind the fight. Against all that hard muscle, she's soft and supple, almost calm.

Rosso painted it in the 1520s, probably for Giovanni Bandini, a man of the party opposed to the Medici. Then it was sent to France, as a gift for King Francis the First. Decades later it was back in Florence, in the collection of Don Antonio de' Medici, the son of a grand duke.

Rosso went to France too. After the Sack of Rome in 1527 he left Italy, and ended up working for the king at Fontainebleau. There he reused the poses of some of these very nudes.

The picture came home. The painter never did. He died in Paris.

### 050 · D12 · Extra · Rosso Fiorentino — Angel Playing the Lute
*Room D12 · 216 words*

**Where:** Room D12, "Pontormo - Rosso Fiorentino". A small panel, under half a metre across, against a dark background: a child angel with red and white wings bent over a large lute. It is the room's picture on the museum's map.

This little angel is having trouble with his lute.

He's a child, bent over a lute that seems far too big for him. The museum describes him as apparently unconfident in his effort to handle it, and that's exactly the charm. His fingers are on the strings. His head is down. He is concentrating as hard as any child at a first music lesson.

Rosso Fiorentino painted him in 1521. But this was never meant to be a picture on its own. It's a fragment, cut from a much larger altarpiece that is now lost.

How do we know? Infrared studies beneath the dark background have found part of a building, so there was once architecture behind him. And around 1600, a Sienese painter, Francesco Vanni, seems to have repeated the whole composition in an altarpiece near Siena. That later altarpiece lets us guess what the lost original looked like.

The panel carries an inscription with Rosso's name and the year. Even that is uncertain. It may be his own hand, or it may have been added when the altarpiece was cut up.

A lost altarpiece, a borrowed memory, and the one small piece of it everyone knows.

### 052 · D15 · Bronzino — Eleonora di Toledo with her son Giovanni
*Room D15 · 314 words*

**Where:** Room D15, the Room of the Dynasties, on the corridor next to Room D14. This is a portrait of a head of state. Three times, while her husband was away, Eleonora di Toledo governed in his place.

She was Spanish, the wife of Duke Cosimo the First. The museum calls her a stateswoman, a capable administrator, and the duke's most important collaborator. Agnolo Bronzino painted her around 1545 with their second son, Giovanni, then about two years old.

Look at the dress first. It's silk brocade, a speciality of Florence's own textile industry, so the gown is also an advertisement. Follow the pattern: pomegranates and pinecones. They were Christian symbols of resurrection and new life, and here they also speak of her fertility.

The true ending is sadder. In the winter of 1562, malaria struck the family. Giovanni died in November. His brother Garzia died in December, and Eleonora died at Pisa that same month, within weeks of her son.

So this picture of a secure future became, in the end, a memorial to the mother and the boy beside her.

### 057 · D23 · Titian — Venus of Urbino
*Room D23 · 344 words*

**Where:** Room D23, Titian – Venus of Urbino. Stand in front of the wide canvas, about one and two-thirds metres across, of a nude woman reclining on white sheets and looking straight out at you, a little dog asleep at her feet and two maids at a chest in the background.

**What:** Titian, Venus of Urbino, 1538, oil on canvas, 119 × 165 cm, inv. 1890 no. 1437 (page "D22" in the old numbering = map D23).

In 1538 a young nobleman wrote a letter about a painting he had bought from Titian. He didn't call it Venus. He called it "the naked woman".

He was Guidobaldo della Rovere, the heir to the duchy of Urbino. That is how she got her name. The Venus of Urbino.

She lies on white sheets and looks straight out at you. The museum calls her gaze flirtatious and allusive. In one hand she holds a bunch of roses, the flower of Venus. A dog sleeps at the foot of the bed, a sign of fidelity in marriage. On the windowsill stands a pot of myrtle, another plant of Venus, and of constant love. Behind her, two maids are busy at a painted chest, taking out a wedding dress.

So is she a goddess or a bride? Titian took his idea from a Venetian custom called the toccamano. At home, not in church, a young woman touched the hand of the man who had asked to marry her, to show that she consented. Out of that small private gesture, Titian made a seductive Venus.

He knew Giorgione's Sleeping Venus, now in Dresden, which he may even have helped to finish. Giorgione's goddess sleeps. Titian's is wide awake, and she knows you are there. More than three centuries later, Manet answered her with his Olympia.

Later she came to Florence, in the dowry of Vittoria della Rovere, the last of her family, who married a Medici grand duke. And for many years she hung in the Tribune, close to the marble Medici Venus. Ancient statue and modern painting, side by side, as if someone had set up a contest.

And unlike the statue, she still looks you in the eye.

### 058 · D23 · Extra · Titian — Flora
*Room D23 · 226 words*

**Where:** Room D23, near the Venus of Urbino. Look for a smaller canvas, about 80 centimetres high, of a young woman with long, loose copper-blonde hair, a white robe slipping off her right shoulder, holding a few flowers.

**What:** Titian, Flora, about 1517, oil on canvas, 79.7 × 63.5 cm, inv. 1890 n. 1462 (page "D22" in the old numbering = map D23).

Long before the Venus of Urbino, Titian painted this young woman. She looks out of the dark, around 1517, with a few spring flowers in her hand.

Her hair is the colour of so many of Titian's women that it carries his name. That copper-blonde, falling loose over her shoulders, is the shade the world still calls Titian red. Her white robe, cut in an ancient style, is slipping off her right shoulder. In her hand are violets, rosebuds and jasmine.

She is called Flora, the nymph who became the bride of Zephyr, the west wind. But the museum reads her less as a goddess than as an image of beauty itself, modest and sensual at the same time. Venetian painters loved this kind of picture. They are known simply as the Beauties.

She also travelled. Around 1635 she was in Amsterdam, in the collection of a Spanish ambassador. He sold her to an archduke of Austria. Then in 1793 the imperial gallery in Vienna exchanged her with the grand dukes of Tuscany, and she came to Florence. Engravers had been copying her since the sixteenth century.

**Where:** Room D26, Veronese. Look for the tall canvas, about two and a half metres high, of a nobleman in a black fur-lined tunic with a sword, one glove off, his bare hand on the shoulder of his small son.

**What:** Paolo Caliari, known as Veronese, Portrait of Count Giuseppe da Porto with his son, Adriano, about 1555, oil on canvas, 247 × 133 cm, Contini Bonacossi no. 16 (page "D25" = map D26). Also: Veronese, Holy Family with Young St John and St Catherine, about 1565, oil on canvas, 86 × 122 cm (page "D26. Veronese Corridor" = map D27).

In 1573 Paolo Veronese was called before the Inquisition in Venice. Part of the trouble was a dwarf with a parrot.

He had painted an enormous Last Supper for the dining hall of a Dominican convent. The inquisitors objected to what he had crowded in around Christ: a jester-dwarf with a parrot, a servant with a nosebleed, and halberdiers dressed like Germans. He was told to correct it within three months. Veronese didn't repaint it. He changed the title. The Last Supper became the Feast in the House of Levi, a different banquet from the Gospel of Luke. That canvas, about thirteen metres long, is in Venice, at the Gallerie dell'Accademia.

Here you meet him on a quieter day. Count Giuseppe da Porto stands with Adriano, his first-born son, one of seven children. The count wears a heavy black tunic lined with fur, and a sword. Look at his hands. He keeps one glove on. He has taken the other off, so that his bare hand can rest on his boy's shoulder. Adriano wears a jacket lined with ermine and embroidered in gold. The portrait once had a partner, of the count's wife and their daughter. That one is now in Baltimore.

Veronese's Holy Family with Saint Catherine, from about 1565, is listed in the Veronese Corridor, D27, which you walk through next. In 1654 two Medici princes bought it in Venice for what the museum calls an astronomical sum, a thousand scudi.

Go out into the Veronese Corridor, Room D27, and up the long corridor beside the river to the Verone, Room D28. Look for the statue of Silenus cradling the baby Bacchus.

### 060 · D28 · The Verone
*Room D28 · 236 words*

**Where:** Room D28, the Verone: the long covered loggia at the river end of the first floor, with a row of statues. Look for the statue of the old Silenus cradling the infant Bacchus in his arms.

**What:** The Verone, the covered loggia on the Arno, with ancient and Renaissance statues, among them three monumental works from the Villa Medici in Rome. Jacopo del Duca, Silenus cradling Bacchus as a child, made between 1571 and 1574, a copy after a Roman original.

After so many rooms of paint, here is a space with hardly any paintings at all. That's rather the point.

The Uffizi calls the Verone a covered loggia on the Arno, with walls of a very pale, elegant green, a place for ancient and Renaissance statues. Three of the biggest came from the Villa Medici in Rome, the family's grand house in that city. This is a good place to rest your eyes, and your feet.

Look for Silenus cradling the infant Bacchus. In the myths, Silenus was the old companion and teacher of the wine god, usually shown drunk and cheerful. Here he is something gentler: a foster father holding a baby. Its theme fits in a single word, fatherhood. The Sicilian sculptor Jacopo del Duca made this version between 1571 and 1574, copying an ancient Roman original.

Keep that baby in mind. In a few minutes you'll meet him again, grown up, in a painting by Caravaggio, holding out a glass of wine.

Behind you now is the whole sixteenth century: Lombardy, Ferrara and Bologna, the Florentine court of Bronzino, and Venice with Titian and Veronese. Ahead is the seventeenth century, and a painter who changed everything with a beam of light and a great deal of darkness.

At the end of the Verone, turn left along the corridor. The first room, E4, is Caravaggio's. Look for the round shield with Medusa's screaming head.

## First floor · The 17th century

### 061 · E4 · Caravaggio's room
*Room E4 · 270 words*

**Where:** Room E4, Caravaggio – The Medusa, the first room off the corridor after the Verone. Find the wide canvas, about 1.35 metres across, of an angel seizing an old man's wrist as he is about to sacrifice a boy, with a calm hilly landscape behind. Guido Reni's tall David in a red feathered hat is in the same room.

**What:** Caravaggio, Sacrifice of Isaac, about 1603, oil on canvas, 104 × 135 cm, inv. 1890 no. 4659. Also: Guido Reni, David with the Head of Goliath, about 1605, oil on canvas, 222 × 147 cm. Artemisia Gentileschi's Judith and Caravaggio's Medusa are in this room (062, 063).

In Rome in 1606, Caravaggio killed a man named Ranuccio Tomassoni. He spent the rest of his life on the run, and he died in 1610, aged thirty-eight.

In a career that short, he changed European painting. He took real faces, ordinary and lined, and struck them with a hard light out of deep darkness. That way of painting is called tenebrism, from the Italian word for darkness. Painters all over Europe copied it, and they became known as the Caravaggisti. This room and the three after it carry the story through the seventeenth century.

Start with the Sacrifice of Isaac. God has ordered Abraham to kill his son, and the old man has the boy pinned down. At the last instant an angel grips his wrist, hard, and points to the ram that will die instead. Look at that hand on the wrist. The whole story turns on it. Behind them stretches a wide, calm landscape of hills and a village. The subject was chosen by a powerful churchman, Maffeo Barberini, who later became Pope Urban the Eighth.

It came to the Uffizi as a gift from the collector John Fairfax Murray. He had bought it believing it was by another painter entirely, Gherardo delle Notti. You'll meet him two rooms from here.

Guido Reni was in Rome in the same years, and he borrowed Caravaggio's darkness without his violence. His David poses like a dandy in a red feathered hat, calmly considering the giant's head. Artemisia Gentileschi's Judith hangs in this room too.

Stay in Room E4 for Caravaggio's Medusa, the screaming head painted on a round shield.

### 062 · E4 · Caravaggio — Medusa
*Room E4 · 309 words*

**Where:** Room E4, Caravaggio – The Medusa. Look for the round painted shield with the severed head of Medusa, mouth wide open in a scream, snakes for hair and blood pouring from the neck.

**What:** Caravaggio, Medusa, 1595–1598, oil on canvas applied to a wooden shield (a parade shield), inv. 1890 no. 1351. No uffizi.it artwork page; placement from the October 2026 map, "E4 Caravaggio The Medusa".

For more than thirty years, this was not a famous Caravaggio. It was a piece of parade equipment in the Medici armoury, and nobody had written down who painted it.

In 1598 Cardinal Francesco Maria del Monte, Caravaggio's patron in Rome, gave the painted shield to Grand Duke Ferdinando the First. It went into the Uffizi armoury without any attribution. Only in 1631 was it recorded as a work by Caravaggio. And it was put on show as part of a suit of Persian armour, worn by a mannequin sitting on a wooden horse. Picture walking into the armoury and meeting that rider, with this head on his arm.

It was a well-chosen gift. Medusa was the Gorgon whose gaze turned people to stone. The hero Perseus cut off her head, and the goddess Athena set it on her shield, so its power would freeze her enemies. The Medici liked the image as a sign of their own military strength.

Now look at the face. Caravaggio has painted the moment between life and death. The head is already cut off, and she is still alive enough to be horrified by it. Her mouth is wide open. Her eyes stare. Blood pours from the neck, and the snakes in her hair are still writhing. He painted it on canvas stretched over a wooden shield, so the head sits on the curve.

There's a long tradition that the face is Caravaggio's own, studied in a mirror. Nobody can prove it. But remember how Perseus survived. He never looked at Medusa directly, only at her reflection in his polished shield. A painter watching his own scream in a mirror, and putting it on a shield, would be a very Caravaggio kind of joke.

Stay in Room E4 for Artemisia Gentileschi's Judith Beheading Holofernes: two women holding a general down on a bed.

### 063 · E4 · Artemisia Gentileschi — Judith Beheading Holofernes
*Room E4 · 333 words*

**Where:** Room E4. Stand in front of the tall dark canvas, almost one and a half metres high, of two women holding a man down on a bed while one drives a sword into his neck, blood running onto the white sheets.

**What:** Artemisia Gentileschi, Judith Beheading Holofernes, about 1620, oil on canvas, 146.5 × 108 cm, inv. 1890 n. 1567 (page "D29" in the old numbering; the map puts Artemisia in E4).

When this painting reached Florence, it caused a stir. According to the museum, it was denied the honour of being shown in the Gallery. Today it hangs at the heart of it.

The story is from the Bible. Judith, a young Jewish woman, saves her besieged town by going into the enemy camp. After a banquet, the Assyrian general Holofernes falls into a drunken sleep, and she kills him. As the Book of Judith puts it, "The Lord has struck him down by the hand of a woman."

Look at how Artemisia Gentileschi stages it. This is no graceful heroine holding a tidy sword. The general lies across the bed, his head gripped by the hair. Judith braces her arms and works the blade into his neck. Her maid leans on him to hold him down. And the blood spurts so hard it stains Judith's own dress.

Artemisia was the daughter of the painter Orazio Gentileschi, who worked in Rome. In 1612 a court in Rome convicted Agostino Tassi, a painter who worked with her father, of raping her. During the trial she herself was tortured to test her word, with cords wrapped round her fingers and pulled tight. She held to her account. "I have told the truth and I always will, because it is true." Many people read that ordeal into this picture. Perhaps. But it was her painting, not her suffering, that made her name.

She spent seven years in Florence, and she became the first woman admitted to the city's Academy of Drawing. She agreed the fee for this canvas with Grand Duke Cosimo the Second. He died in 1621, soon after it was finished, and the money stalled. To get paid, she needed help from her friend Galileo Galilei.

Two women working together, and a painter who refused to look away. Florence wasn't ready to hang it. The world has caught up.

Next room along, Room E5: Caravaggio's Bacchus, the young god holding out a glass of wine.

### 064 · E5 · Caravaggio — Bacchus
*Room E5 · 314 words*

**Where:** Room E5, Caravaggio – Il Bacco. Stand in front of the canvas, about a metre high, of a young man crowned with vine leaves who holds out a glass of red wine, with a bowl of fruit in front of him.

**What:** Caravaggio, Bacchus, about 1598, oil on canvas, 95 × 85 cm, inv. 1890 n. 5312 (page "D32" in the old numbering; the map says E5).

For years, this painting sat in the Uffizi's storerooms, and nobody knew what it was. Then in 1913 it was found, and the art historian Roberto Longhi gave it back to Caravaggio.

It's an early work, from his first years in Rome, when he lived under the protection of Cardinal del Monte, the man who sent the Medusa to Florence. According to the museum, the cardinal gave this one to Grand Duke Ferdinando for the wedding of his son Cosimo.

So here is Bacchus, god of wine, crowned with vine leaves. He holds out a glass toward you, as if inviting you to drink. Now look a little closer. The fruit in the bowl is not all perfect. Some of it is already bruised and turning. And look at his hands. The fingernails are dirty. He is a god. He is also, quite clearly, a real young man posing, with a heavy, slightly drunken look.

The body follows classical models, the ancient portraits of Antinous, the beautiful youth loved by the emperor Hadrian. Some critics read the wine and fruit as an invitation to friendship and simple pleasures. Others see a celebration of the freedom of the senses. And the fruit on the turn may be a quiet reminder that ripeness does not last.

There is one more secret, and you won't be able to see it. When the painting was cleaned in 1922, a tiny reflection appeared in the glass carafe on the table. In 2009, infrared imaging showed what it was: a man in the clothes of the time, very probably the painter himself. It cannot be seen with the naked eye, so don't strain for it.

Caravaggio offers you the glass. And somewhere in the carafe, he stays to watch you take it.

Next room along, Room E6, Painting by Candlelight. Look for the Nativity lit only by the glowing Child.

### 065 · E6 · Painting by candlelight
*Room E6 · 227 words*

**Where:** Room E6, Painting by Candlelight. Look for the wide night scene, about 1.3 metres across, of the Virgin, Joseph leaning on his stick and two angels bending over a manger, all lit by the newborn Child.

**What:** Gerrit van Honthorst, Adoration of the Child, about 1619–1620, oil on canvas, 95.5 × 131 cm, inv. 1890 no. 739.

In this room, the light doesn't come in through a window. It comes from inside the pictures.

Gerrit van Honthorst was a Dutch painter from Utrecht. Early in the seventeenth century he went to Rome, and there he saw what Caravaggio had done with darkness. He took that hard spotlight and made it warmer and more intimate: candles, lamps, faces glowing in the night. The Italians gave him a nickname for it, Gherardo delle Notti, Gerard of the Nights. You've already met his name once, on the Caravaggio that a collector bought thinking it was his.

His Adoration of the Child was painted around 1619 or 1620. There is no candle in it at all. The light pours out of the newborn's body. It softens the Virgin's face as she leans over him. It catches Joseph, leaning on his stick, and two angels bending over the manger. Everything outside that small circle sinks back into the night.

Grand Duke Cosimo the Second bought some of Honthorst's works in 1620, and this was probably one of them. So it may have reached the Medici almost as soon as it was painted.

Caravaggio used darkness to shock. In this room it is used to hold people close together.

Next room along, Room E7. Look for Rembrandt's portrait of an old bearded man, lost in thought against a dark background.

### 066 · E7 · Rembrandt, Rubens and Van Dyck
*Room E7 · 254 words*

**Where:** Room E7, Rembrandt – Rubens – Van Dyck. Look for the canvas, just over a metre high, of an old bearded man, half-length, who seems lost in thought as he emerges from a dark background.

**What:** Rembrandt Harmenszoon van Rijn, Portrait of an old man (The Old Rabbi), 1665, oil on canvas, 104 × 86 cm, inv. 1890 no. 8435 (page "D34" in the old numbering; the map's E7 highlight).

Rembrandt painted this old man in 1665, four years before his own death. By then he had lost a great deal.

His wife Saskia had died in 1642, and in 1656 he went bankrupt. He kept on painting, and his late portraits are among his deepest.

The picture is known as the Old Rabbi. The face has been identified as a rabbi's, and a name has been suggested, though the documents don't confirm it. Rembrandt lived for many years in Amsterdam's Jewish quarter, and he often asked neighbours and acquaintances to sit for him. So this may simply be a man he knew.

Look at how it's painted. The colour is laid on thick, and you can see the texture of the paint. The old man isn't posing for you. He seems lost in his own thoughts, almost swallowed by the dark around him.

The painting has travelled too. Napoleon's officials noticed how good it was, and it was carried off to Paris. It stayed there until his fall, and only years later came back to Florence.

The room also carries two Flemish names. Peter Paul Rubens was the great painter of Catholic Antwerp, all movement and warm flesh. Anthony van Dyck began as his brilliant assistant and went on to paint the courts of Europe.

After so much splendour, it's fitting to end the paintings with a quiet face.

From Room E7, follow the route out to the Magliabechi staircase. The last words of this guide are for the top of the stairs.

## Closing

### 067 · The way out
*Magliabechi staircase · 312 words*

**Where:** At the top of the Magliabechi staircase, where the route ends beyond Room E7, by the exit sign. There is a lift close by.

**What:** The end of the route. Down the Magliabechi staircase to the ground floor, past the cloakroom and the audioguide desk, and out by the back way out of the building, on the Palazzo Vecchio side.

This is where the route ends. Before the look back, the way out.

Go down the Magliabechi staircase to the ground floor. If you'd rather not take the stairs, there is a lift close by. At the bottom you pass the cloakroom and the audioguide desk, so collect anything you left there. Once you are through the exit, you can't come back in for it. The way out is at the back of the building, on the Palazzo Vecchio side.

Now think back to where you started. You climbed to the top floor of a building that Vasari designed as offices for the Medici state. That's all "Uffizi" means. You began with three gold Madonnas, where the gold was the light. You end in rooms where the light is a single beam in the dark, or a newborn child, or the face of an old man.

Halfway along, you passed the Tribune, the little octagonal room with the red walls and the mother-of-pearl dome. That was where it started. A grand duke set his finest treasures in one room, to be admired. In time, the whole floor became a gallery. And on the 24th of June 1769 it opened to the public, as one of the world's first museums created with a modern concept, in the museum's own words.

It's all still here because of a promise. When the Medici line died out, the last of the family, Anna Maria Luisa, agreed with the new rulers that nothing would leave the capital and the State. That is why you could stand in front of the Venus of Urbino today, in the city that kept her.

Outside, the Ponte Vecchio is a short walk away. Take one picture with you as you go, the one that stopped you. You'll find it's the one you remember. Thank you for walking the Uffizi with me.
