**What:** Metropol Parasol, known as las Setas, by Jürgen Mayer H. (J. MAYER H., Berlin), engineered by Arup, opened 27 March 2011. Somewhere under this square lies a Roman house with a mosaic of Medusa. Close to thirty metres above it, people stroll across a giant timber canopy. This guide connects the two.

Officially it's Metropol Parasol. Sevillanos took one look and called it las Setas, the mushrooms. It was designed by the Berlin architect Jürgen Mayer H., and opened on the twenty-seventh of March, 2011.

It's one building in four layers. Roman and Moorish remains lie under the street. The market is at street level. Above it is a raised public square, the Plaza Mayor. On top, a walkway winds across the parasols. You'll start at the bottom, go to the very top, and finish in the middle.

## Antiquarium

### 002 · Antiquarium — Found under a car park
*Level −1 · Antiquarium entrance · 337 words*

**Where:** On level minus one, at the entrance to the Antiquarium beside the taquilla, or on the first stretch of walkway inside, where the excavated remains first come into view.

**What:** The Antiquarium, the archaeological museum under the Setas, opened with the Setas in 2011; about 4,879 m² of excavated remains from the 1st century AD to the 13th century: Roman houses with mosaics, a Moorish (Almohad) house; remains of a 1st-century Roman fish-salting works also found on the site. NEW in V5.1: the dig history in one line (1991 traders' test dig, 1998 rescue excavation linked to the car-park plan, the big excavation 2003–2009, archaeology directed by Fernando Amores Carredano); the museum space by the architect Felipe Palomino González: a glass "membrane" around the ruins, transparent to translucent; one zone of direct daylight only; two lighting systems, for the architecture and for the remains. The look of the space.

In the 1990s, Seville planned a car park under this square. What it got instead was the Romans.

The plan was to remodel the Plaza de la Encarnación with an underground car park. But the ground here kept giving up its past. First came a small test dig in 1991, paid for by the market traders. Then a rescue dig in 1998, when the car park was planned. Then the big excavation, from 2003 to 2009, its archaeology directed by Fernando Amores. The car park was never built. What they uncovered is what you're about to walk through.

This is the Antiquarium, the museum of the remains under the Setas. It opened together with the Setas, and it covers nearly five thousand square metres. The city calls it Seville's most important surviving Roman site.

The remains span some twelve centuries. The oldest go back to the emperor Tiberius, in the first century. The latest are from the 1200s. The Roman houses lay deepest. A house from Moorish Seville came much later, and much nearer the surface.

Remains of a Roman fish-salting works were found here too. Their story comes later, when you can look toward the river.

The space was designed as carefully as the dig. Its architect, Felipe Palomino, wanted a membrane wrapped around the ruins, and he made it of glass. The glass can be clear, or frosted, or anything in between. Almost no daylight reaches down here. There is just one area of direct natural light. So the lighting comes in two separate systems, one for the architecture and one for the remains.

Walkways carry you over the excavation, with remains underfoot and behind glass. Read the labels as you go. This was a neighbourhood, where people lived and worked for century after century.

A car park that was never built became a window into Roman and Moorish Seville.

Walk on along the walkway in the Antiquarium, and play the next stop at the first good view down into the remains: Seville stacked up.

### 003 · Antiquarium — Seville stacked up
*Level −1 · Antiquarium, over the dig · 250 words*

**Where:** Inside the Antiquarium, on the walkway at the first good view down into the remains, ideally where you can see how deep they go.

**What:** The 1991–92 test dig on the site of the old Mercado de la Encarnación (Verdugo Santos, Larrey Hoyuelos and Murillo Díaz, commissioned by the market traders' cooperative): convent remains, an Almohad level about 3 m down, a late-1st-century Roman building about 7 m down, Roman deposits to about 9 m; groundwater and a shored pit; the Setas designed as "a structure that sits on the Roman ruins". V5.1: kept from V5 003; the opening now follows on from 002, which names the 1991 dig.

That first test dig, the one the market traders paid for, was tiny. It still found everything.

In the autumn of 1991, the traders' cooperative of the Encarnación market commissioned it on the site of their old market hall. What the archaeologists found was Seville stacked like a layer cake. Near the top, the remains of the convent that once stood here. About three metres down, Moorish houses. Then Roman Seville, seven to nine metres below the street.

Getting down there was the hard part. As soon as they dug deep, they hit groundwater. They had to shrink the dig to a pit about three metres across and shore up its sides, just to reach the Roman levels.

It was a tiny, waterlogged window. But it showed that everything was there, one age on top of another.

Years of bigger excavations followed. And when the Setas came to be designed, the building had to make room for all of this. Its architect, Jürgen Mayer H., has described it as "a structure that sits on the Roman ruins".

Wherever you stand in here, you are already below the street, inside those layers. Somewhere above your head are the market, the raised square and the parasols. The whole history of the city, in one cut through the ground.

Follow the walkway on into the Roman houses, and look for the label for the Casa de la Columna, the House of the Column. The next stop in the Antiquarium is Medusa, the guardian.

### 004 · Antiquarium — Medusa, the guardian
*Level −1 · Antiquarium, Roman houses · 360 words*

**Where:** Inside the Antiquarium, among the Roman houses, at the label for the Casa de la Columna, the House of the Column, with its Medusa mosaic.

**What:** Roman Hispalis under the Plaza de la Encarnación: houses with mosaics built and rebuilt in several phases from the mid-1st century AD. NEW in V5.1: 47 floors found, 33 with mosaics, about a dozen on show when the specialist study came out (López Monteagudo 2015, as reported by KRAMER; spoken in the past tense, audit 004.1), some in situ on the floor, some as vertical panels (KRAMER); the Casa de la Columna with its Medusa mosaic (VS-ANT), "laid out on the floor" (ACOM); KRAMER: "the warning and protective Medusa in a geometric composition", "depicted with an unhappy facial expression", at "the first stop, building D". Guardian line kept general (Romans put Medusa's head on floors as a guard), the specific one attributed to "her study" (López Monteagudo, via Kramer's review; one consistent attribution across 004–006, audit 004.2); no claim that she protects this house. In passing: Casa Romana, Casa de las Basas, Casa de la Noria (house attribution of the noria left open, audit row 25). Originals, replicas, photographs.

Seville was an important Roman city, yet it has kept surprisingly few Roman mosaics. The best group it has is down here.

The Romans called the city Hispalis. Under this square stood a quarter of its houses, and they were never finished in one go. The archaeologists traced phase after phase of building, over five or six centuries, starting in the first century.

They found forty-seven floors, thirty-three with mosaics. Guadalupe López Monteagudo wrote the specialist study of them. When it came out, only about a dozen were on show. Some lie on the floor where they were found. Others hang upright, as panels. A review of her study puts it plainly: in a city where few have ever been found, these are unmatched for quality and variety.

The house to find is the Casa de la Columna, the House of the Column. Look for its Medusa, the snake-haired Gorgon of Greek myth. In the old stories, a single look from her turned people to stone, until the hero Perseus cut off her head.

So why would anyone want her underfoot? Because that stare could work for you, too. Romans put Medusa's head on their floors as a kind of guard, a face to turn bad luck away. The specialist study calls this one a warning and protective Medusa.

If you find her, notice her setting. She sits inside a geometric pattern. Then look at her face. The same study notes that she looks unhappy. Perhaps she knows how her story ends.

Along the way, the labels give other house names too. The Casa Romana, the Roman House. The Casa de las Basas, the House of the Column Bases. The Casa de la Noria, the House of the Water Wheel. They are modern names, not Roman ones. And the labels tell you which pieces are originals, which are replicas, and which survive only in photographs taken during the dig.

For a city that kept so little of its Roman floors, this is where Hispalis still shows its face.

Walk on through the Roman houses, and play the next stop in the Antiquarium as you go: Bacchus and the dinner party.

### 005 · Antiquarium — Bacchus and the dinner party
*Level −1 · Antiquarium, walking through the Roman houses · 356 words*

**Where:** Inside the Antiquarium, heard while walking on through the Roman houses after the House of the Column. There is no fixed spot: play it as you go and read the labels. It names what to look for, never where it is.

**What:** POSITION-FREE WALK-ALONG (lead decision 30 Oct 2026, kept in V5.1). NEW in V5.1: the Casa de Baco, whose triclinium's main mosaic showed the triumph of Bacchus with Ariadne (KRAMER); the version with Ariadne in the carriage was "the most common motive in Baetica for a long time" (KRAMER; not claimed to be this floor, audit 005.1); drinking vessels at the corners of the mosaic floor of the Casa de Baco (ACOM; not tied to the triumph floor, audit 005.2); the Casa del Sigma and the sigma, the C-shaped curved dining couch (stibadium), label-dependent (TTS, general archaeological terminology); the Hospitium de los Delfines and its rare "unswept floor" (asarotos oikos) motif in a geometric composition, food scraps "remnants of a particular event" (KRAMER); marine fauna among the mosaics found, dolphins, fish and water birds (KRAMER; "found", not "on show", audit 005.6). Attributed throughout to "the study" (audit 004.2). Names only, as in V5: the Casa de la Ninfa, the Patio del Océano with a three-metre mural (VS-ANT; what it shows is not described). Check every name against the O4 label photos before release; whether each floor is on show is a label question.

Wine, couches and leftovers. Keep an eye on the labels as you walk. Some of them read like the plan for a Roman dinner party.

Start with the Casa de Baco, the House of Bacchus, the Roman god of wine. Romans dined lying on couches, in a room called the triclinium. In this house, the main mosaic of the dining room showed the triumph of Bacchus. With him was Ariadne, the princess he married in the myth. The study of these floors says one version, with Ariadne riding in his chariot, was long the most common in Roman southern Spain. When you find the mosaic floor of the Casa de Baco, look at its corners for the drinking vessels.

Then the couch. Look for the Casa del Sigma, the House of the Sigma. Sigma is a Greek letter, and in Roman times it was often written like a C. The name passed to a curved dining couch, where guests lay in a half-circle around the food. If the label speaks of a stibadium, that's the same curved couch.

Now the strangest mosaic of the evening. Hospitium is Latin for hospitality, or for taking in guests. In the Hospitium de los Delfines, the Hospitium of the Dolphins, the study describes a rare motif set in a geometric pattern: an unswept floor. Food scraps, scattered as if a feast has just ended. Which party it was, nobody can know. The study says that's exactly what wakes up your imagination.

Two more names to watch for. The Casa de la Ninfa, the House of the Nymph, a spirit of springs and rivers. And the Patio del Océano, the Ocean Courtyard, where the city's tourism office lists a three-metre mural. Among the mosaics found here, the study counts many sea creatures: dolphins, fish and water birds.

The guests are long gone. Their dinner party was set down in stone.

Keep walking and reading the labels in the Antiquarium. Next, look for a mosaic hung upright on a wall, with two birds at its centre. Play the next stop when you find it, or as you walk on: The two birds.

### 006 · Antiquarium — The two birds
*Level −1 · Antiquarium, on the wall · 258 words*

**Where:** Inside the Antiquarium, wherever you find a mosaic hung upright on a wall with two birds at its centre, the museum's emblem; if you haven't spotted it, play it as you walk on and keep looking. It never says where the mosaic is.

**What:** NEW TRACK in V5.1. The emblem: the "kissing birds" mosaic, the symbol of the Antiquarium, "at the centre of a large mosaic" and "reconstructed on the wall of the museum" (ACOM), "pieced together … displayed on the wall" (ACOM-B). Only these SEC facts are spoken as fact; neither source mentions a bowl. Dropped: "It began as part of a floor", "underfoot", "sharing a drink", "small" birds (all inferred). No species, no century, no "kissing" as fact. Retitled from "Two birds and a wine bowl"; 005's cue updated.

Of all the gods and monsters found under this square, the museum chose two birds as its emblem.

Somewhere along the way, look for a mosaic hung upright on a wall, with two birds at its centre. It was pieced back together and set on the wall, so you see it standing up.

Some people call them the kissing birds. Kissing, or just keeping each other company? You decide.

The study of these mosaics describes a panel with two birds on a crater, the big bowl Romans mixed their wine in. It may be the same mosaic. That panel comes from the Hospitium de los Triclinia, the Hospitium of the Dining Rooms. If the label matches, look for more. On that panel, the birds sit in a small square at the centre of a geometric design. Around it, two borders are laid to look like moving water, and smaller zigzag panels add to the ripple. See if the water seems to move.

Why birds at all? Romans liked mosaics that brought nature indoors, plants and animals, especially in the parts of a house people walked through. What one image meant to the family who lived with it, the study admits, always eludes us.

Out of all of it, the museum picked the gentlest image to stand for the whole.

Walk on, and look for the label for the casa almohade, the Almohad house. Play the next stop in the Antiquarium there: The Almohad house. If you passed it earlier, play that stop now, before you leave the Antiquarium.

### 007 · Antiquarium — The Almohad house
*Level −1 · Antiquarium, Almohad house · 260 words*

**Where:** Inside the Antiquarium, at the label for the casa almohade, the Almohad house, the Moorish part of the excavation.

**What:** An Islamic house of the Almohad period, 12th–13th centuries, with traces of the surrounding Almohad neighbourhood; found about 3 m below the street in the 1991–92 test dig, against about 7 m for the Roman level. Context: the Almohad Great Mosque of Seville (ordered 1172) and its minaret, begun 1184, today's Giralda. NEW in V5.1: the Torre del Oro, ordered by the Almohad governor Abu l-'Ula, built in 617 AH (1220–21) (Ministerio de Defensa 2023); "it survived because it was buried" (audit §2, OPINION-safe), tied to the layers above it (convent, market; AAA91). The house is not named "Casa de la Noria" (audit row 25, unresolved).

Down here, a thousand years can be just a few metres of earth.

At the label for the casa almohade, you reach Moorish Seville. The house comes from the Almohad era, the 1100s and 1200s, when Seville was a capital of Muslim Spain. The excavators found a Moorish house on this site, and traces of the Almohad neighbourhood around it.

When the first test dig went down, the archaeologists met this Moorish level about three metres below the street. The Roman floors lay about twice as deep. So the house stood high above the Roman city, on ground that the centuries had slowly built up over it. If you've just come from the Roman houses, you've walked through a thousand years in a few minutes.

Those were great decades for Seville. The Almohad caliph Abu Ya'qub Yusuf built the city's Great Mosque. In 1184, work began on its minaret. Today we call it the Giralda. Down by the river, the Torre del Oro, the Tower of Gold, is Almohad work too. An Almohad governor had it built around 1220.

Those towers stood in the open air, and they stand there still. You'll see the Giralda from the top. The house had a different fate. Later, a convent rose over this ground, and later still a market. The house survived because it was buried.

That's the end of the Antiquarium. Follow the exit signs, then go to the lifts beside its entrance, the Mirador access. The next stop plays by the lifts, before you go up: A plaza with many lives.

## Going up

### 008 · Plaza de la Encarnación — A plaza with many lives
*Level −1 · by the lifts · 337 words*

**Where:** On level minus one, by the lifts that go up to the Mirador, beside the Antiquarium entrance. Play it while you wait; there is nothing you need to look at.

**What:** The Plaza de la Encarnación before the Setas: the Convento de la Encarnación (founded 1591), demolished under the Napoleonic decree of 1810; a wooden market by 1820; Melchor Cano's permanent market hall, built in the 1830s; the cut of 1948; demolition in 1973; the surface car park; the 1990s underground car-park plan that ran into Roman Seville. NEW in V5.1, both attributed to the operator (S-HIST): "more than 400 stalls of fresh products" and "the first known food market in the city of Seville" (audit #9); the 1973 reasons as the operator gives them: structural problems in the vegetable and fish stalls, the Tamarguillo flood, the first supermarkets (audit #8; supersedes the PLAN 007 NOT line by PLAN_V5_1).

This square exists because of Napoleon's brother.

It takes its name from the Convent of the Incarnation, the Encarnación, a nunnery founded here in the late 1500s. The nuns stayed for more than two centuries. Then came the French. During the occupation, in 1810, the government of Napoleon's brother, Joseph Bonaparte, ordered the convent pulled down to open up a square.

A square is rarely empty for long in Seville. Soon, traders had a wooden market here. Then the city built a proper one, a permanent market hall designed by the architect Melchor Cano, which went up in the 1830s. For well over a century, this was Seville's central market. By the operator's account, it had more than four hundred stalls, and it was the first known food market in the city.

It didn't last. Around the middle of the last century, a new traffic artery sliced off part of the market. In 1973 the rest was demolished. The operator gives three reasons. There were structural problems in the fish and vegetable stalls. There was the Tamarguillo flood, when a stream on the edge of the city burst its banks. And the first supermarkets had arrived.

The traders were moved out to temporary stalls, and for decades the square was a fenced, dusty car park. Later, the city planned an underground car park beneath it. The digging ran into Roman Seville, and that plan was dropped. What they found is in the Antiquarium, beside these lifts.

So the gap that Napoleon's brother cleared has been filled again and again. First by a market, then by parked cars, then by archaeologists, and finally by the Setas.

And the traders? Their own story waits for you near the end, above the market.

The lift you're about to take rises through every chapter of it, from below the old streets to high above the rooftops.

Take the lift up. Start the next stop as the doors close. It carries on as you step out onto the walkway: Mushrooms of wood.

### 009 · Setas — Mushrooms of wood
*Lift and first walkway · 347 words*

**Where:** Start it in the lift as the doors close; the second half plays as you step out onto the first stretch of walkway, among the timber of the parasols. Keep moving with the flow and stop at the rail only where you're out of the way.

**What:** Metropol Parasol by J. MAYER H. (Jürgen Mayer H., born 1965), winner of the 2004 international competition (65 entries). NEW in V5.1: two phases, ten projects in the second, winner made public on 12 June 2004; the brief: a public space for the city, compatible with the conservation and display ("puesta en valor") of the archaeological remains (IAPH-PH50, CL), so the ruins were in the brief from the start (corrects V4's "brief rewritten"); Mayer still in his thirties (SOLITUDE + IAPH-PH50); the fig trees named by the city (Cristo de Burgos), "centennial" per the operator (S-HIST says the ficus "in the Plaza de San Pedro", which adjoins it; age to confirm at O20 / city guide p. 114), listed by the City among Seville's "árboles singulares" (SEV-AS), spoken as "remarkable trees" (audit S21, 009.1); this is the fig-tree passage's only full telling (010 and 021 call back to it, audit M3); the timber named once as Kerto-Q. Kept from V5: engineered with Arup; Kerto laminated veneer lumber made in Finland, about 3,400 elements made at Aichach near Munich, 2–3 mm polyurethane coating; concrete trunks with the lifts; the "largest wooden structure" claim and what the engineers say. No visual reference before "By now you're out of the lift" (was "As you step out", route audit S1).

In 2004 Seville put a question to architects worldwide. What should it do with a square that had been a car park, then a dig, for some thirty years? Sixty-five entries came in.

The brief asked for a public space for the city, and for a design that would conserve the Roman remains and put them on show. So the ruins were no surprise. They were in the brief from the start.

The competition ran in two rounds, and ten projects reached the second. That June, the winner was made public: Jürgen Mayer H., from Berlin, still in his thirties. His project, Metropol Parasol, was a huge shade over the square, standing on a handful of trunks, with a walkway across the top.

He didn't set out to build mushrooms. But the nickname stuck, and even the architect came round to it.

So where did the shapes come from? Mayer has said he took cues from the cathedral's vaulting and from the great shade trees of a neighbouring square. The city names them as the giant fig trees of the Plaza del Cristo de Burgos. The city lists them among its remarkable trees. Trees and vaults, then, not mushrooms.

By now you're out of the lift, among the timber. Engineered with the firm Arup, it's laminated veneer lumber made in Finland, a type called Kerto-Q: thin sheets of wood glued into big panels. Some 3,400 pieces were milled in Bavaria and trucked to Seville. And every beam wears a waterproof skin of polyurethane a few millimetres thick.

But it isn't all wood. The lift you just rode runs inside a concrete trunk.

Long billed as the largest wooden structure in the world, it is certainly one of the largest. Its engineers say "one of the largest timber structures ever built".

A competition, a nickname nobody planned, and thousands of pieces of Finnish wood. That's what's all around you now.

## Walkways and Mirador

### 010 · Walkway — The Cathedral and the Giralda
*Walkway · south side (where the Giralda is in clear view) · 331 words*

**Where:** Anywhere on the walkway, or at the Mirador, where the Giralda, the cathedral's tall tower, and the cathedral beside it are in clear view above the rooftops. Stop at the rail where you're out of the way.

**What:** The Giralda: minaret of the Almohad Great Mosque, begun 1184, the cathedral's bell tower since the conquest of 1248, about 97.5 m to the top of the weathervane. NEW in V5.1: the Giraldillo, the weathervane on top, a cast-bronze figure of Faith holding a banner (lábaro) and a palm, 1565–1568, 348 cm tall, symbolising the triumph of Christianity over Islam (IAPH-GIR); the name-origin story is NOT used (no official source found). Seville Cathedral: new church decided in 1401 (spoken "early in the 1400s" to keep to three dates); called by UNESCO the largest Gothic building in Europe; World Heritage with the Alcázar and the Archivo de Indias (the Alcázar is not claimed as visible). Link to the Setas: the cathedral's vaulting as a design cue is told in 009 and only called back here (hook, and the timber-vaults line; audit M3); MoMA's 1:200 model (2004–07) and its "cathedral without walls". The Almohads glossed as the Moorish rulers, with the Moorish remains under the square stated for every version (audit M2, lead decision 3).

One of the ideas behind the Setas is in view from here. It's the cathedral, less than a kilometre from where you stand.

That way is south. The tall tower is the Giralda, and the cathedral stands beside it.

The Giralda began as a minaret. Work on it started in 1184, for the Great Mosque of the Almohads, the Moorish rulers of the city. This was the same Moorish Seville whose remains lie under this square. When King Fernando the Third took the city in 1248, the mosque became the cathedral, and the minaret became its bell tower. It rises close to a hundred metres to the tip of its weathervane. The Setas are close to thirty metres high. The tower is more than three times as tall.

That weathervane has a name, the Giraldillo. It's a bronze figure of Faith, holding a banner and a palm, cast in the 1560s. From here it looks tiny. In fact it's about three and a half metres tall. It stands for the triumph of Christianity over Islam, on top of a tower that began as a minaret.

The cathedral itself came later. Early in the 1400s, the cathedral's clergy voted to pull down the old mosque and build a new church. What they built is vast. UNESCO calls it the largest Gothic building in Europe, and lists it as World Heritage, together with the Alcázar and the Archive of the Indies.

Now, the link. The Museum of Modern Art in New York keeps a model of the Setas. The museum describes the design as a democratic "cathedral without walls".

Inside the cathedral, stone vaults close out the sky. Up here the vaults are timber, and the sky comes straight in.

Two cathedrals, then, centuries apart. One of stone, with walls. One of wood, without.

Now find a spot on the walkway facing west, toward the river: stand with the Giralda on your left. The next stop is The river, Triana and the Aljarafe.

### 011 · Walkway — The river, Triana and the Aljarafe
*Walkway · west side (the Giralda on your left) · 326 words*

**Where:** On the walkway, at a spot on the rail where the Giralda, the cathedral's tall tower, is on your left: you are then facing west, toward the river and Triana. Stand close to the rail so others can pass.

**What:** The view west toward the Guadalquivir; NEW in V5.1: toward the river, the rooftops of Triana on its far bank (STRIP, seville-tickets, SEC; PLAN_V5_1 views rule), with one general line on Triana, pottery as its main trade for centuries (JA-TRI, OFF; sailors dropped: UPO-TRI is a student article, SNIP, audit 011.1); beyond, the Aljarafe, the high ground west of the city (Triana itself is low riverside ground, not the high ground).
