## Antiquarium

### 002 · Antiquarium — Found under a car park
*Level −1 · Antiquarium entrance · 297 words*

**Where:** On level minus one, at the entrance to the Antiquarium beside the taquilla, or on the first stretch of walkway inside, where the excavated remains first come into view.

**What:** The Antiquarium, the archaeological museum under the Setas, opened 27 March 2011; about 4,879 m² of excavated remains from the 1st century AD to the 13th century: Roman houses with mosaics, a Moorish (Almohad) house; remains of a 1st-century Roman fish-salting works also found on the site.

In the 1990s, Seville wanted somewhere to park. It dug under this square, and it found the Romans.

The plan was to remodel the Plaza de la Encarnación with an underground car park. The digging ran into Roman Seville, and the car park was never built. Instead, the excavations went on, on and off, for more than a decade, the later ones directed by the archaeologist Fernando Amores. What they uncovered is what you're about to walk through.

This is the Antiquarium, the museum of the remains under the Setas. It opened together with the Setas on 27 March 2011, and it covers nearly five thousand square metres. The city calls it Seville's most important surviving Roman site. It is run by Seville City Council.

What makes it unusual is the span of time. The remains here cover some 1,200 years of the city's life, from about the time of the emperor Tiberius, in the first century, to the 1200s. In the ground, the Roman houses lay deepest. A house from Moorish Seville came much later, and much nearer the surface. You'll meet both.

Inside, walkways carry you over the excavation, with remains underfoot and behind glass. Take your time, and read the labels as you go. Most of what's here is houses and streets. This was a neighbourhood, where people lived and worked for century after century.

### 004 · Antiquarium — The House of the Column and its Medusa
*Level −1 · Antiquarium, Roman houses · 290 words*

**Where:** Inside the Antiquarium, among the Roman houses, at the label for the Casa de la Columna, the House of the Column, with its Medusa mosaic.

**What:** Roman Hispalis under the Plaza de la Encarnación: houses with mosaics built and rebuilt in several phases from the 1st century AD; the Casa de la Columna with its Medusa mosaic; in passing, the Casa Romana, Casa de las Basas, Casa del Sigma and Casa de la Noria; originals and replicas.

Seville was an important Roman city, yet it has kept surprisingly few Roman mosaics. The best group it has is down here.

The Romans called the city Hispalis. Under this square stood a quarter of its houses, and they were never finished in one go. The archaeologists traced phase after phase of building, over five or six centuries, starting in the first century.

One scholarly review of the mosaics puts it plainly. In a city where few have ever been found, these are unmatched for their quality and their variety.

The house to find here is the Casa de la Columna, the House of the Column. Its mosaic shows Medusa, the snake-haired Gorgon of Greek myth. In the old stories, a single look from her turned people to stone, until the hero Perseus cut off her head. Someone in Roman Hispalis wanted her on their floor, here, under what is now a Seville street.

The other houses have names too. There is the Casa Romana, the Roman House, and the Casa de las Basas, the House of the Column Bases. There is the Casa del Sigma, the House of the Sigma, the Greek letter. And there is the Casa de la Noria, the House of the Water Wheel. They are modern names, not Roman ones.

Read the labels as you go. Some pieces here are originals, some are replicas, and some mosaics may appear only in photographs taken during the dig. The labels tell you which is which.

For a city that kept so little of its Roman floors, this is where Hispalis still shows its face.

## Going up

### 007 · Plaza de la Encarnación — A plaza with many lives
*Level −1 · by the lifts · 278 words*

**Where:** On level minus one, by the lifts that go up to the Mirador, beside the Antiquarium entrance. Play it while you wait; there is nothing you need to look at.

**What:** The Plaza de la Encarnación before the Setas: the Convento de la Encarnación (founded 1591), demolished under the Napoleonic decree of 1810; a wooden market by 1820; Melchor Cano's permanent market hall, built in the 1830s; the cut of 1948; demolition in 1973; the surface car park; the 1990s underground car-park plan that ran into Roman Seville.

This square exists because of Napoleon's brother.

It takes its name from the Convent of the Incarnation, the Encarnación, a nunnery founded here in the late 1500s. The nuns stayed for more than two centuries. Then came the French. During the occupation, in 1810, Joseph Bonaparte's government ordered the convent pulled down to open up a square.

A square is rarely empty for long in Seville. Soon, traders had a wooden market here. Then the city built a proper one, a permanent market hall designed by the architect Melchor Cano, which went up in the 1830s. For well over a century, this was Seville's central market.

It didn't last. Around the middle of the last century, a new traffic artery sliced off part of the market. In 1973 the rest was demolished, largely because of structural problems. The traders were moved out to temporary stalls, and for decades the square was a fenced, dusty car park.

Later, the city planned an underground car park beneath it. The digging ran into Roman Seville, and that plan was dropped. What they found is in the Antiquarium, beside these lifts.

So the gap that Napoleon's brother cleared has been filled again and again. First by a market, then by parked cars, then by archaeologists, and finally by the Setas.

And the traders? Their own story waits for you at the end, above the market.

The lift you're about to take rises through every chapter of it, from below the old streets to high above the rooftops.

### 008 · Setas — Mushrooms of wood
*Lift and first walkway · 296 words*

**Where:** Start it in the lift as the doors close; the second half plays as you step out onto the first stretch of walkway, among the timber of the parasols. Keep moving with the flow and stop at the rail only where you're out of the way.

**What:** Metropol Parasol by J. MAYER H. (Jürgen Mayer H.), winner of the 2004 international competition (65 entries); engineered with Arup; Kerto laminated veneer lumber made in Finland, about 3,400 elements made at Aichach near Munich, 2–3 mm polyurethane coating; concrete trunks with the lifts; the "largest wooden structure" claim and what the engineers say.

In 2004 Seville asked architects from around the world what to do with a square that had been a car park and then a dig for some thirty years. Sixty-five entries came in.

The winner was Jürgen Mayer H., from Berlin. His project was called Metropol Parasol: a huge shade over the square, standing on a handful of trunks, with a walkway across the top.

He didn't set out to build mushrooms. But Sevillanos called it las Setas anyway. The nickname stuck, and even the architect came round to it.

So where did the shapes come from? Mayer has said he took cues from the cathedral's vaulting and from the great shade trees of a neighbouring square. The city names them as the giant fig trees of the Plaza del Cristo de Burgos. Trees and vaults, then, not mushrooms.

As you step out, the timber is all around you. It was engineered with the firm Arup, and it's laminated veneer lumber made in Finland: thin sheets of wood glued into big panels. The pieces were milled in Bavaria and trucked to Seville, some 3,400 of them. And every beam wears a waterproof skin of polyurethane a few millimetres thick.

But it isn't all wood. The lift you just rode runs inside a concrete trunk.

You may have heard a bigger claim. Long billed as the largest wooden structure in the world, it is certainly one of the largest. Its engineers say "one of the largest timber structures ever built".

A competition, a nickname nobody planned, and thousands of pieces of Finnish wood. That's what's all around you now.

That way is south. The tall tower is the Giralda, and the cathedral stands beside it.

The Giralda began as a minaret. Work on it started in 1184, for the Great Mosque of the Almohads, the same Moorish Seville as a house found under this square. When King Fernando the Third took the city in 1248, the mosque became the cathedral, and the minaret became its bell tower. It rises close to a hundred metres to the tip of its weathervane. The cathedral itself came later. In 1401 the cathedral chapter voted to pull down the old mosque and build a new church. What they built is vast. UNESCO calls it the largest Gothic building in Europe, and lists it as World Heritage, together with the Alcázar and the Archive of the Indies.

Now, the link. Mayer has spoken of the cathedral's stone vaulting as one of his references. The Museum of Modern Art in New York keeps a model of the Setas, and it describes the design as a democratic "cathedral without walls".

Take a moment with that idea. Inside the cathedral, stone vaults close out the sky. Up here the vaults are timber, and the sky comes straight in.

Two cathedrals, then, centuries apart. One of stone, with walls. One of wood, without.

For the Romans, this city was Hispalis, and the river made it a port. Ships came up the river from the sea. One academic study by a University of Seville archaeologist is titled "Hispalis, Roman port of Baetica". Baetica was the Roman province of the south.

Now the find. Under this square, archaeologists uncovered a fish-salting works from the first century AD. A long corridor, floored with small Roman bricks, led to five tanks. Four have been excavated. When the works was abandoned, they were filled with rubbish, mostly compacted fish remains. Judging by that fill, they went out of use somewhere between 70 and 120 AD.

Was garum made here, the famous Roman fish sauce? It's tempting to say so. The careful answer is that this was the kind of works that produced salted fish and fish sauces like garum. Either way, it belonged to a city that lived by its river.

Long before the convent, the market or the parasols, the river had already put this square to work.

### 011 · Walkway — The glue that had to be baked
*Walkway · 242 words*

**Where:** Anywhere on the walkway on the way to the steps up to the Mirador, walking slowly or stopped at the rail out of the way of others, with the timber panels of the parasols around you.

**What:** The timber structure of the Setas: Kerto laminated veneer lumber panels by Finnforest, engineered by Arup; more than 3,000 joints with glued-in steel bars, about 35,000 holes drilled; the Fraunhofer WKI heat study and the tempering of the glued parts; the 2007 redesign; the largest panel 16.5 m tall.

Architect magazine once called this the world's largest building held together primarily by glue. And in Seville, glue has an enemy: the summer sun.

Wherever two timber panels cross around you, there's a joint, and there are more than three thousand of them. Inside each, steel bars are glued into the wood. The timber makers say about thirty-five thousand holes were drilled for those bars, by hand.

The question was heat. Scientists at Germany's Fraunhofer institute for wood research simulated what the sun would do to the joints. Dirk Kruse, of the institute, put it plainly: "the temperatures in the adhesive could potentially reach almost 60 degrees." That was right at the limit the glue was rated for.

The fix sounds like baking. After gluing, the parts were heated again, a step called tempering, so the resin set harder. Every glued joint was baked a second time to toughen it.

It wasn't the only scare. By one account, in 2007 the engineers warned it could not be built as drawn, and it was largely re-engineered. Mika Kallio, of the timber firm Finnforest, later said: "It is perhaps the most complex timber structure ever built."

The biggest single panel is sixteen and a half metres tall. And every one of them is still holding on, glue and steel, all around you.

### 013 · Walkway — Love it or hate it
*Walkway · toward the way down · 282 words*

**Where:** On the walkway after you come back down from the Mirador, heading toward the way down; walking, or stopped at the rail out of the way of others.

**What:** The controversy over Metropol Parasol: protests at the opening on 27 March 2011, the critics' verdict "como a un Cristo dos pistolas", Rowan Moore's review as relayed by Dezeen, Mayor Alfredo Sánchez Monteseirín's "moderna catedral urbana"; the four-year delay and the cost the city reported to Spain's Ombudsman, without the museum figure; Red Dot "Best of the Best" 2012, Mies van der Rohe Award finalist 2013, over a million visitors in the first year by one count.

When the Setas opened, in March 2011, some Sevillanos came out to protest. They argued that its cost had swallowed money their own neighbourhoods needed.

For years it was the most argued-about building in Seville. People objected to the money, and to the look of it in the middle of the old town. Its critics said the Setas suited the old town "like two pistols on a Christ", a Spanish way of saying wildly out of place.

The British critic Rowan Moore was dry about it: "Oh my God, it's an icon. How very last decade." The mayor who opened it, Alfredo Sánchez Monteseirín, saw something else entirely: "a modern urban cathedral".

Then there was the delay, and the bill. Meant to open in 2007, it took four more years. The city told Spain's Ombudsman that the cost had roughly doubled, from a budget of about 51 million euros to a final bill of about 102 million. Changes to the timber structure, the archaeology and disputes with the builder all played a part.

And yet. It won a Red Dot "Best of the Best" design award. It was a finalist for the European Union's Mies van der Rohe Award for architecture. And by one count, over a million people went up to the walkway in its first year.

You're standing on it now, so you get a vote too. Icon, folly, or a bit of both?
