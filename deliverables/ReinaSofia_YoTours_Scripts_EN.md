# Yo Tours · Museo Reina Sofía · audio guide scripts (EN) · 17 Oct 2026 edition

## Opening

### 001 · Welcome — a hospital that became a museum
*Sabatini entrance · Floor 1 · 351 words*

**Where:** Play this just inside the Sabatini entrance on Calle Santa Isabel, Floor 1, between the two glass lift towers that frame the doorway. You don't need to stand anywhere in particular.

**What:** The Sabatini Building: Madrid's eighteenth-century Hospital General, largely designed by Francesco Sabatini, opened as the Centro de Arte Reina Sofía in 1986 and established as the Museo Nacional Centro de Arte Reina Sofía in 1988. Route of this guide: Floor 1, Floor 2, Floor 4, Nouvel roof terrace.

This museum began with a king's wish to gather all of Madrid's hospitals under one roof. Work on a great General Hospital finally began here in the eighteenth century.

The name to remember is Francesco Sabatini, an architect born in Palermo. He gave the hospital its long, grand front. He also thought hard about health: air flowing right through the wards, high ceilings, whitewashed walls. The museum calls these ideas ahead of their time.

Then the money ran out. When Charles the Third died, near the end of the century, building stopped, and only about a third of the plan was ever finished. Even so, it worked as a hospital for nearly two centuries, until it closed some sixty years ago.

Most of the plans for it after that meant knocking it down. Critics fought back, and the old hospital was declared a historic monument. It opened as an art centre in 1986. The two glass and steel lift towers you walked between were added to frame this entrance. And in 1992, Picasso's Guernica came to live here.

Here's how the guide works. Every stop gives you a room number and the room's title, the way the museum names them, so you always know where you are. You begin on this floor, Floor 1, with Richard Serra's steel sculpture in Room 102. Then up to Floor 2, for the avant-gardes of the early twentieth century and Guernica. Floor 3 is closed for now, so you skip it. Floor 4 holds art from 1975 to today, in Rooms 1 to 21. And you finish on the roof terrace of the Nouvel building.

Some stops are marked Extra. They are short and optional. Skip them whenever you like.

One plain fact before you start. The museum is rehanging its whole collection, in a process due to finish in 2028.

First, stay on Floor 1. The garden in the middle of the building, reached from the cloister, has a short Extra. The next main stop is Room 102, with Richard Serra's steel sculpture. If you can't see a sign, ask at the information desk.

## Floor 1 · Serra and the Garden

### 002 · Garden · Extra · Gargallo's Great Prophet and Chillida
*Sabatini Garden · Floor 1 · 230 words*

**Where:** In the garden in the middle of the Sabatini Building, reached from the cloister on Floor 1. Look for a tall dark bronze figure, well over two metres high, if it is on display here, and for two rust-brown steel crosses curving into each other, about three metres long.

**What:** Pablo Gargallo, Grand prophète (Great Prophet), 1933, cast 1936, sand-cast bronze, 236 × 75 × 45 cm. Eduardo Chillida, Toki egin, Homenaje a San Juan de la Cruz (Toki egin, Homage to San Juan de la Cruz), 1989–1990, cast Corten steel, 142 × 295 × 213 cm, Exhibited in Sabatini Building, Garden.

Sabatini planned this courtyard for a hospital. He imagined two fountains here, and trees like those in Madrid's Royal Botanical Garden. It was the largest courtyard in his design, and one of the few parts that was actually built.

Today it's a garden, with sculpture among the plants. Look for a tall bronze figure, well over two metres high. This is Pablo Gargallo's Great Prophet, moved into this garden in 2025. If you can't see it, it may be shown elsewhere today. He modelled it in plaster in 1933, meaning it for bronze. Money was short, and the bronze was only cast after his death. The figure is too big and too thick for the sheet metal he often used. Yet light still passes right through it. The critic Pierre Cabanne saw it as a study of volume and emptiness, finished by your own eye.

Now find two rust-brown steel crosses, bent towards each other in a curve. This is Eduardo Chillida's Toki egin, a homage to the Spanish mystic San Juan de la Cruz. Toki egin is Basque for "make way". The two crosses embrace and seem to push against gravity, like the union of the soul with God in the saint's poetry. Chillida wanted space itself to be the subject, not only the steel around it.

Back inside on Floor 1, Room 102 holds Richard Serra's steel sculpture.

### 003 · Room 102 · Richard Serra — Equal-Parallel: Guernica-Bengasi
*Room 102 · 321 words*

**Where:** Room 102 on Floor 1, a long white gallery with a barrel-vaulted ceiling. On the floor stand four solid blocks of dark weathering steel, about one and a half metres high, two square and two long, set in a line with an empty gap in the middle.

**What:** Richard Serra, Equal-Parallel: Guernica-Bengasi, 1986, cast Corten steel, four parts, each 148.5 cm high (two 500 cm long, two 148.5 cm), made for the Reina Sofía's opening exhibition. Also in the room: Richard Serra, Inversion, 2001, two forged steel blocks.

The title of this sculpture names two air raids, almost half a century apart. Guernica, the Basque town bombed by the Condor Legion in April 1937. And Benghazi, the Libyan city attacked by the United States Air Force in April 1986, while Serra was making this work. That raid was a reprisal for a bomb in a Berlin discotheque, blamed on Libyan agents.

It's an unusual title for Richard Serra. He generally said his works don't refer to particular people, places or events. Here he set two moments side by side, as a parallel, on the premise that they are equal.

The sculpture belongs to this building. Serra made it for the exhibition that opened the Reina Sofía art centre, and it entered the collection two years later.

Now look at what's actually in front of you. Four solid blocks of weathering steel. Two are square and two are long, and all have the same depth. Each one is about one and a half metres high, the height of the windowsills in this building. Serra called them equal elevations. They alternate along the floor, and in the middle he left an empty space about the size of a block.

Take a slow walk past them. Above you is a smooth barrel vault, full of white light. The steel sharpens the symmetry of the old hospital architecture, and you start to feel the room as much as see it. The empty gap is part of the work too.

Before you go, look for two smaller blocks of forged steel in the same room. That's Inversion, made fifteen years later.

Two bombings, four blocks, and a space between them that nothing fills. You'll meet the first of those bombings again upstairs, in Picasso's Guernica.

Take one of the glass lift towers by the entrance up to Floor 2. The collection rooms begin there, between the two lift towers, at Room 201.01, 'Holy Bohemia'.

## Floor 2 · Bohemia, Cities and Cubism

### 004 · Room 201.01 · Holy Bohemia — From Slum to Gran Vía
*Room 201.01 · 213 words*

**Where:** Room 201.01 on Floor 2, the first collection room, titled 'Holy Bohemia. From Slum to Gran Vía'. Look for a black-and-white photograph of King Alfonso XIII opening the Madrid Metro, and Ricardo Baroja's small, dark etchings of the city's edges.

**What:** Room 201.01, 'Holy Bohemia. From Slum to Gran Vía'. Alfonso Sánchez García, Alfonso XIII inaugura el primer tramo del metropolitano de Madrid (Alfonso XIII Opens the First Section of the Madrid Underground System), 17 October 1919, posthumous print 1984, gelatin silver print. Ricardo Baroja, El suburbio (The Suburb), 1909, etching and aquatint. Floor 2 presentation: 'Avant-garde Territories: City, Architecture and Magazines'.

On the seventeenth of October 1919, King Alfonso the Thirteenth opened the first stretch of the Madrid Metro. A photographer, Alfonso Sánchez García, was there to record it. His photograph hangs in this room.

Welcome to Floor 2. The museum calls this floor Avant-garde Territories, and it tells the story of modern art through three things: cities, architecture and magazines. Paris, Madrid and Barcelona are the main stages. This floor is due to be rehung in 2028.

This first room shows Madrid with two faces. Around 1900, Spain was in crisis, made worse by the loss of its last colonies in 1898. Writers such as Miguel de Unamuno and Pío Baroja looked hard at poverty and backwardness. Painters followed them into slums and taverns, among beggars and people on the margins.

Look for Ricardo Baroja's small, dark etchings, such as The Suburb. The city's edges, drawn in black and grey.

Then, in the 1920s, a different Madrid takes shape. Reinforced concrete made room for towers like the Telefónica building. New leisure brought new buildings, a racecourse at Zarzuela, a court for the ball game of pelota. From slum to Gran Vía, as the room's title says.

Next, Room 201.02, 'Holy Bohemia. Paris was a party'. Look for Picasso's tall portrait, Woman in Blue.

### 005 · Room 201.02 · Picasso — Woman in Blue
*Room 201.02 · 306 words*

**Where:** Room 201.02, 'Holy Bohemia. Paris was a party'. Find Picasso's large portrait, over a metre tall, of a woman dressed in blue, standing and filling the canvas.

**What:** Pablo Picasso, Mujer en azul (Woman in Blue), Madrid, 1901, oil on canvas, 133 × 100 cm.

Picasso painted this woman in Madrid, sent her to a national exhibition, and then left town. The painting was forgotten for decades.

In 1901 he was not yet twenty. He had already been to Paris once, and now he spent the first months of the year in Madrid. He met the writers of the Generation of '98. He became artistic director of a small magazine called Arte Joven, Young Art, and drew its illustrations.

That spring he painted a series the museum calls courtesan portraits. This is one of them. And you can watch him borrowing from everywhere. From the Modernisme he had known in Barcelona. From Van Gogh and Toulouse-Lautrec, whom he had seen in Paris. And from the old Spanish masters in the Prado.

Look at the way she fills the frame, upright and composed. For that, Picasso took the composition of a royal portrait, Velázquez's Queen Mariana of Austria. Then look at the brushwork, and think of El Greco. A queen's pose and an old master's hand, given to a woman of Madrid's night life.

He sent the painting to the General Exhibition of Fine Arts in Madrid that same year. It attracted very little notice. And before the show had even closed, Picasso was gone, back to Paris to prepare his first solo exhibition there. Nobody seems to have missed the picture.

It surfaced again decades later, when it entered what was then Spain's national museum of modern art. In time, it came here.

So take a last look at her. A canvas its young painter walked away from, at the very start of the career that would change modern art.

Stay in Room 201.02 for a short Extra on Ramon Casas's posters. Or go on to Room 201.03, 'Ramón Gómez de la Serna, the improbable artist', with Solana's crowded café gathering.

### 006 · Room 201.02 · Extra · Ramon Casas and Isidre Nonell — Barcelona, Paris and the poster
*Room 201.02 · 203 words*

**Where:** Stay in Room 201.02. Look for Ramon Casas's tall Anís del Mono posters, more than two metres high, each with a large woman against flat, bold colour, and for Isidre Nonell's heavy, thickly painted figures of women.

**What:** Ramon Casas, Anís del Mono. «Mono y mona» (Anís del Mono. "Male Monkey and Female Monkey"), 1898, lithograph on paper, 212 × 103 cm, and other Anís del Mono posters. Isidre Nonell, Mujer (Woman), 1907, oil on canvas, 63 × 52 cm.

Around 1900, some of the most modern art in Barcelona was on the street wall. Ramon Casas understood that better than anyone.

Catalonia was an industrial society, and it was learning from the French poster. Magazines and poster competitions drove the new style, and colour lithography caught the eye of people hurrying past. For the Anís del Mono competition, Casas put a monumental woman at the centre, against flat, strongly contrasting colour. He was aiming at buyers all over Spain.

Look for his tall Anís del Mono posters. The one called Mono y mona, Male Monkey and Female Monkey, is from 1898, and it stands more than two metres high.

Now find Isidre Nonell, who went the other way. Instead of glamour, he painted the people Barcelona preferred not to see, among them Roma women. His figures are heavy and twisted, their faces thickly painted. The museum reads them as people denied an identity, shut out of history. His Woman, from 1907, is here.

Two artists from the same city. One sold to the street. The other painted the people who lived on it.

Next, Room 201.03, 'Ramón Gómez de la Serna, the improbable artist', with Solana's Gathering at the Café de Pombo.

### 007 · Room 201.03 · José Gutiérrez Solana — The Gathering at the Café de Pombo
*Room 201.03 · 267 words*

**Where:** Room 201.03, 'Holy Bohemia. Ramón Gómez de la Serna, the improbable artist'. Find the large group portrait, about two metres wide, of men gathered round a café table, with one man standing in the centre.

**What:** José Gutiérrez Solana, La tertulia del Café de Pombo (The Gathering at the Café de Pombo), 1920, oil on canvas, 161.5 × 211.5 cm, donated by Ramón Gómez de la Serna, 1947. Also in the room: Rafael Barradas, Pombo.

From 1915, every Saturday, the writer Ramón Gómez de la Serna held court in a Madrid café. The Café de Pombo stood on Calle Carretas, a few steps from the Puerta del Sol. Painters, poets and critics of the avant-garde came to talk, to argue and to be seen.

Gómez de la Serna is hard to put in any box. He invented the greguería, a tiny, witty sentence that mixes humour and metaphor. The museum calls his café a "theatre of the word but also an arena of gazes". He liked to wander the city as a kind of performance, and he wrote about places like the Rastro flea market.

In 1920, the painter José Gutiérrez Solana painted the gathering. The museum calls it the most iconic image of those Saturdays.

Step back and take in the table. The man standing in the centre is Gómez de la Serna, the host. Around him are the regulars, among them Manuel Abril, Mauricio Bacarisse, Tomás Borrás and José Bergamín. And Solana put himself in the picture too. See if you can pick out the painter among his friends.

Rafael Barradas painted the same café. His Pombo is in this room, and you'll meet more of his work two rooms on.

There's a lovely end to the story. The painting belonged to Gómez de la Serna himself, and in 1947 he gave it to the national collection. The host gave away his own party.

Next, Room 201.04, 'José Ortiz Echagüe', has a short Extra on his photographs. The next main stop after it is Room 202.01, 'Madrid, a Diverse City'.

### 008 · Room 201.04 · Extra · José Ortiz Echagüe — photographs of Spain
*Room 201.04 · 209 words*

**Where:** Room 201.04, 'José Ortiz Echagüe', a small room of five works. Look for his black-and-white prints of village life, with deep, velvety blacks, such as a sermon in a village church, and sculpted busts by Julio Antonio.

**What:** José Ortiz Echagüe, Sermón en la aldea (Village Sermon), 1903, direct carbon print on laid paper, image 40.5 × 38.7 cm; also Lagarteranas and Lagarteranas en misa. Julio Antonio, Mujer de la mantilla and Moza de Aldea del Rey.

José Ortiz Echagüe wanted his photographs to give you what he called "the strange feeling of travelling to a different time". Look around this small room and see if it works on you.

He began photographing everyday life in Spain in 1903, and from 1916 he did it systematically, travelling from region to region. He wanted to record ways of life he believed were about to disappear. His aims were documentary. His methods were not.

Find Village Sermon, one of his earliest pictures, taken in 1903 in the parish church of Viguera, in La Rioja. Look at the blacks. They are deep and velvety, and the softer tones break into tiny dots. Ortiz Echagüe printed everything himself, with his own version of the carbon process. He called it Carbondir. The results look closer to an engraving than a photograph.

Not everyone approved. A review in 1923 criticised how heavily he worked his prints by hand.

His portraits of Spanish types and costumes became books, first in Berlin and then in Madrid. The busts beside them are by the sculptor Julio Antonio, who travelled Spain with the same idea, portraying working people with a classical calm.

Next, Room 202.01, 'Madrid, a Diverse City', with Rafael Barradas's small, vibrating painting, Atocha.

### 009 · Room 202.01 · Rafael Barradas — Atocha, and Madrid, a Diverse City
*Room 202.01 · 307 words*

**Where:** Room 202.01, 'Madrid, a Diverse City'. Find Barradas's Atocha, a small canvas just over half a metre wide, its busy street scene broken into overlapping, fragmented shapes. María Blanchard's tall Girl at Her First Communion, almost two metres high, is in the same room.

**What:** Rafael Barradas, Atocha, 1919, oil on canvas, 53 × 66 cm. Also: María Blanchard, La comulgante (Girl at Her First Communion), 1914, oil on canvas, 180 × 124 cm.

What does a modern city feel like when you paint it? Rafael Barradas thought it should vibrate.

He was born in Uruguay. In Italy he saw Futurism first hand, the movement in love with speed and machines. Then he came to Spain, to Barcelona in 1914 and to Madrid the year after. Here he helped to launch a style of his own and gave it a name, Vibrationism. It grew out of Cubism and Futurism, and its aim was to catch the rush of modern life.

Find this small canvas, just over half a metre wide. Its title, Atocha, is the name of a Madrid district, and it shows the bustle of a busy part of the city in the early twentieth century. The museum calls it one of the clearest examples of Vibrationism. It comes close to the French style called Orphism, but Barradas keeps more of the real street in view. Let your eye jump from piece to piece, and see what you can recognise.

The rest of the room asks a bigger question. By the late 1800s, the great cities of the continent were crowded and out of date, grown outward from their old centres. Thinkers and architects such as Ildefonso Cerdá, Arturo Soria and Ebenezer Howard looked for new models. They planned for new transport, separate zones for work and for living, and space for sport and culture. Iron and glass became the materials of a new architecture.

Before you leave, look for a much bigger canvas, almost two metres tall. It's María Blanchard's Girl at Her First Communion, from 1914. You'll meet Blanchard again in the Cubist rooms.

Barradas's Madrid is not a place to stand still. In 1919, neither was the city.

Next, Room 202.02, 'Barcelona, Neutral City'. Look for Sonia Delaunay's colourful Dubonnet, a painting that looks like a poster.

### 010 · Room 202.02 · Sonia Delaunay — Dubonnet, and Barcelona, Neutral City
*Room 202.02 · 298 words*

**Where:** Room 202.02, 'Barcelona, Neutral City'. Find Sonia Delaunay's Dubonnet, a canvas about three-quarters of a metre wide that looks like a poster, built from bold colour and big letters, with a line of small lettering along the bottom. Olga Sacharoff's portrait of Arthur Cravan, with a cat, is in the same room.

**What:** Sonia Delaunay, Dubonnet, 1914, water-based paint on canvas, 61 × 76 cm. Also: Olga Sacharoff, Joven del gato (retrato de Arthur Cravan) (Youth of the Cat [Portrait of Arthur Cravan]), c. 1916, oil and collage on canvas, 111 × 94 cm.

This looks like an advertisement for a drink. But the company that made the drink never asked for it, and it was never meant to sell anything.

Dubonnet was a well-known liqueur, and Sonia Delaunay painted its name in 1914. She borrowed a trick from advertising, where a poster often tells you little about the product except what it's called. Her real subject was the letters themselves, and how they sit among the colours around them.

She and her husband, Robert Delaunay, had developed what they called Simultanism, a version of Cubism that moved towards abstraction. She painted discs of colour that play against each other in your eye, so the surface seems to move with rhythm. Later she carried those ideas into fabrics, clothes and ceramics.

Look along the bottom. The words Atelier Simultané Sonia Delaunay may have been added ten years later, after she founded her own studio to print her fabrics. A painting that pretended to be an advert ended up advertising its maker.

Now the room's title, Barcelona, Neutral City. Spain stayed out of the First World War, and Barcelona became a refuge. When the war began, the painter Olga Sacharoff and her husband left France for Spain. In 1916 they settled in Barcelona, in the home of Francis Picabia. Around them grew what the museum calls a community of exiled avant-garde artists. Robert and Sonia Delaunay were part of it, and so was Albert Gleizes.

Find Sacharoff's portrait of Arthur Cravan, poet, boxer and her husband's brother. Look at the long, stretched forms and the eyes without pupils. They come from her friend Modigliani.

War sent these painters south. For a few years, the avant-garde had a Barcelona address.

Next, Room 202.03, 'Dalmau Gallery'. Look for Joan Miró's farmhouse with a palm tree.

### 011 · Room 202.03 · Joan Miró — House with Palm Tree, and the Dalmau Gallery
*Room 202.03 · 276 words*

**Where:** Room 202.03, 'Dalmau Gallery'. Find Miró's House with Palm Tree, a canvas about three-quarters of a metre wide: a country farmhouse with a palm tree, plants drawn in fine detail and ploughed land kept simple. Picabia's Totalisateur, a watercolour and ink drawing of a puzzling machine, is in the same room.

**What:** Joan Miró, La casa de la palmera (House with Palm Tree), 1918, oil on canvas, 65 × 73 cm. Also: Francis Picabia, Totalisateur (Totalizer), c. 1922, watercolour and ink on paperboard, 55 × 73 cm.

In 1921, this painting went to Paris to launch a young painter's career. Nothing in the show sold.

The painter was Joan Miró. His dealer in Barcelona was Josep Dalmau, who wanted his artists known abroad. Dalmau backed a show of Miró's work at the Galerie La Licorne in Paris, and this canvas was in it. The museum says it plainly. The show failed, because the works didn't sell.

Miró painted it in 1918 at Mont-roig, near Tarragona, where his parents had bought a farmhouse. It was there, after an illness at eighteen, that he had decided to become a painter. The house in this picture stands near his parents' land.

Look at the plants. Each one seems drawn leaf by leaf, with great care. Now look at the ploughed ground. That he keeps much simpler. Fine detail beside simple shapes. The museum points out that those contrasts later fed the signs of his Surrealist paintings. The Miró of stars and floating symbols starts here, in a farmyard.

Dalmau's gallery showed a very different artist the next year. Find Francis Picabia's Totalisateur, a watercolour of a strange machine. Picabia had just stepped away from Dada, and he brought new, sparse, geometric work to Barcelona, built up from the circle. His machines were puzzles, often with a deliberately provocative sexual meaning. He had been reading Freud and looking hard at his friend Marcel Duchamp.

A farmhouse painted leaf by leaf, and a machine that hides a joke. Dalmau's walls had room for both.

Next, turn the corner into the side wing, to Room 204.01, 'Cubism, the First International Language'. Look for Picasso's bronze head of a woman.

### 012 · Room 204.01 · Picasso — Head of a Woman (Fernande), and Cubism
*Room 204.01 · 300 words*

**Where:** Room 204.01, 'Cubism, the First International Language'. Find Picasso's bronze head, about forty centimetres high, its face, hair and neck made of swelling, interlocking forms. A painting with the same title, a head broken into sharp planes in browns, greens and greys, is in the same room.

**What:** Pablo Picasso, Tête de femme (Fernande) (Woman's Head [Fernande]), autumn 1909, patinated bronze, lost-wax cast, 41.3 × 24.7 × 26.6 cm. Also: Pablo Picasso, Tête de femme (Fernande), Paris, 1909–1910, oil on canvas, 61 × 50 cm. Room 204.01 also includes works lent by the Telefónica Collection.

In 1912, the Paris dealer Ambroise Vollard sold a cast of this bronze head to a buyer in New York, the photographer Alfred Stieglitz. Stieglitz published photographs of it in the magazine Camera Work. The next year he put it in the Armory Show, the exhibition that brought modern art to America.

Picasso modelled the head in the autumn of 1909. The sitter was Fernande, who inspired a whole series of his Cubist portraits, in paint and in sculpture. He had been looking at objects from Africa, among them Nimba masks, and he found a new way to build a volume.

Walk slowly round it, if you can. Face, hair and neck are made of swelling forms that lock together. Where you'd expect a hollow, he often pushes the shape outward instead. Watch the neck as you move. It seems to twist. The museum says this head signalled the birth of Cubist sculpture.

Now find a painting with the same title, Woman's Head, Fernande. Here her face is cut into sharp geometric zones, laid out almost like a grid, in browns, greens, greys and blacks. That narrow palette is typical of Analytical Cubism. It keeps your eye on structure, not colour. As one critic put it, a Cubist picture has to be thought as much as seen.

Not everyone was convinced. French newspapers called Cubism cold, geometric, intellectual, monotonous, and foreign. Writers such as Guillaume Apollinaire defended it, and cheap little magazines carried it across borders. Camera Work was one of them. Some works in these Cubist rooms are on loan from the Telefónica Collection.

So the style Paris mocked as foreign went abroad anyway. Part of it travelled as this bronze head.

Next, Room 204.02, also called 'Cubism, the First International Language': Juan Gris's portrait of his partner, Josette.

### 013 · Room 204.02 · Juan Gris — Portrait of Josette Gris, and María Blanchard
*Room 204.02 · 278 words*

**Where:** Room 204.02, 'Cubism, the First International Language'. Find Gris's portrait of a seated woman, over a metre tall, built from calm, clear planes, her hands crossed in her lap. María Blanchard's larger, near-abstract Cubist Composition, in lemon yellow, pink, orange and green, is in the same room.

**What:** Juan Gris, Portrait de Madame Josette Gris (Portrait of Madame Josette Gris), October 1916, oil on board, 116 × 73 cm, donation of Douglas Cooper. Also: María Blanchard, Composición cubista (Cubist Composition), 1916–1919, oil and collage on canvas, 154 × 114 cm.

In October 1916, Juan Gris painted his partner, Josette. His Cubist friends had broken things into pieces. Gris built her, the way you'd build a house.

He had worked in Paris for years, first drawing for magazines, before he turned to Cubism. Around 1916 his painting grew simpler and clearer. He described his pictures as a "flat and coloured architecture".

Look at her shape. Head at the top, body widening down to the lap. That's a pyramid, the classical composition of the French painter Corot. Then look at her hands, resting crossed in her lap. That pose comes from Cézanne's portraits of his own wife, seated in a yellow armchair. Gris was looking back at the masters, as Picasso would too.

It's a calm picture, almost still. Form matters more than colour here, and more than feeling. The painting was a gift from the collector Douglas Cooper.

Now find a larger, much brighter canvas: María Blanchard's Cubist Composition. Blanchard faced prejudice twice over, as a woman and for her disability. She trained in Madrid, won a grant to go to Paris in 1909, and joined the circle of Gris, Jean Metzinger and Jacques Lipchitz. In 1916 she settled in Paris for good.

Her picture is nearly abstract. Planes of lemon yellow, pink, orange and green hide objects you can barely make out. It's meant to unsettle you. Then, in one place, she paints a baroque moulding in careful detail, the one solid thing to hold on to. Can you find it?

Two Spanish painters in Paris, in the same years. One made Cubism calm. The other made it dizzying.

Next, Room 204.03, 'After Cubism': Juan Gris's Open Window.

### 014 · Room 204.03 · Juan Gris — The Open Window, and After Cubism
*Room 204.03 · 308 words*

**Where:** Room 204.03, 'After Cubism'. Find Gris's Open Window, a horizontal canvas a metre wide, where a room and the view through its window meet in one picture. Oskar Schlemmer's works for the Triadic Ballet and the Bauhaus dances, posters, figures and photographs from the 1920s, are in the same room.

**What:** Juan Gris, La fenêtre ouverte (Open Window), January–March 1921, oil on canvas, 66 × 100 cm. Also: Oskar Schlemmer, Das Triadische Ballett, Der Abstrakte (The Triadic Ballet, The Abstract), 1922, and other Triadic Ballet and Bauhaus works, 1922–1928.

Juan Gris moved to a small town on the French coast, Bandol. And there he painted window after window.

This is one of them, from the first months of 1921. Most of the series was made in Bandol, and he finished it a few months later in Céret. The museum credits this way of painting a window entirely to Gris. Other painters loved open windows too, Robert Delaunay and Henri Matisse among them. But in their pictures, the view outside takes over, and the room behind it hardly matters.

Gris does something else. Look at how the inside of the room and the world beyond the window are joined in one picture. Try to find the line where indoors stops and outdoors begins. Gris keeps both spaces in play, and neither one wins.

The title of this room, After Cubism, comes from a manifesto of 1918 by the painter Amédée Ozenfant and the architect Le Corbusier. It launched a movement called Purism. After the broken surfaces of Cubism, they wanted order, clear construction and no decoration for its own sake. Around the same time, Walter Gropius founded the Bauhaus school in Weimar, with shared ideals.

Now look for Oskar Schlemmer's work for the Bauhaus stage. In his Triadic Ballet, the dancers were turned into geometric sculptures, moving through space. Look at the figure called The Abstract, and at the photographs of the Bauhaus dances.

From a window by the sea to a dancer made of geometry. After Cubism, order was the new adventure.

Next, turn the corner into the back wing, where the 205 rooms run along the cloister. Room 205.02, 'GATEPAC, The Urgency of Facilities', has a short Extra on the architects of GATEPAC, and Room 205.04 has one on John Heartfield's photomontages. The next main stop is Room 205.05, 'Speaking Walls', full of Civil War posters.

## Floor 2 · War, Guernica and Surrealism

### 015 · Room 205.02 · Extra · GATEPAC — architecture for a new republic
*Room 205.02 · 216 words*

### 018 · Room 205.06 · Ángeles Santos — A World
*Room 205.06 · 281 words*

### 023 · Room 205.10 · Pablo Picasso — Guernica
*Room 205.10 · 360 words*

**Where:** Room 205.10, 'Guernica'. The painting fills one long wall: almost 3.5 metres high and nearly 7.8 metres wide, entirely in black, white and grey. Stand well back, towards the middle of the room, to take in the whole of it.

**What:** Pablo Picasso, Guernica, 1937 (1 May – 4 June, Paris), oil on canvas, 349.3 x 776.6 cm, registration DE00050, entered the museum in 1992.

In the first months of 1937, Picasso had a commission, and a very different painting in mind. The Spanish Republic, through Josep Renau, had asked him for a large work for its pavilion at the Paris fair. He was thinking of an old theme, a painter and his model.

Then, on the 26th of April, German planes bombed the Basque town of Gernika. Picasso saw the photographs in the French newspaper L'Humanité. He decided to paint something entirely different.

In just under six weeks he made nearly fifty sketches and studies, and reworked the huge canvas as he went. He built it as a mural for the pavilion, and it hung on the ground floor, in a space he chose himself.

Yet look closely, and you won't find Gernika itself in it. No planes, no named place, no date. The museum calls it a generic plea against the barbarity and terror of war.

Let your eyes travel across it. On the left, a bull. Below it, a mother wails over her dead child. In the centre, a wounded horse screams. On the ground, a dead soldier. A bird, in the background on the left. From a window at upper right, a woman leans out holding a lamp. Another rushes in from the right. And at the far right, a woman cries out with her arms raised, in front of a burning house.

He painted it in greys, like a press photograph or a newsreel, and built it as a pyramid, like the history paintings of the old masters.

The Republic bought the painting. During the Second World War, Picasso had it kept safe at the Museum of Modern Art in New York, and later extended the loan until democracy returned to Spain. It came back in 1981.

One last thing. This building was a hospital. During the war, more than thirty thousand people wounded in the bombing and fighting were treated here. And in this very room, survivors of air raids by the Condor Legion recovered. The same Nazi air force that bombed Gernika.

The magazine printed Rationalism and Surrealism side by side, and it kept in touch with the modern architects of GATEPAC, whom you met at the start of these rooms.

Surrealism was there from the first issues, partly through the reports a young painter from the island sent back from Paris. His name was Óscar Domínguez. Thanks to these links, Breton came to support the Second International Exhibition of Surrealism in Tenerife. He gave two lectures, and he arranged a screening of L'âge d'or, the controversial film by Buñuel and Dalí that was later banned.

Now find Domínguez's small painting, Butterflies Lost in the Mountain. He painted it in Paris, and it's full of homesickness for Tenerife. In the case are his father's butterflies, carefully preserved. Two women in clothes that recall the dress of the Guanches, the islands' ancient people, stand high on a mountain. It may be Teide. They gaze at the case almost in worship.

There's another reading. Some say Domínguez gave the picture to Westerdahl, to mark a day of flying kites shaped like black paper birds. It was staged by a group of island poets, actors and artists called Pajaritas de Papel, Little Paper Birds.

Either way, it's a small picture of a very long distance, from a Paris studio back to a mountain at home.

### 031 · Room 205.17 · La Barraca and the Pedagogical Missions — Dalí's portrait of Luis Buñuel
*Room 205.17 · 338 words*

**Where:** Room 205.17, 'Culture's Proletarians. La Barraca and the Pedagogical Missions'. Find Dalí's portrait of Buñuel, about 70 by 60 centimetres: a dark-haired young man, head and shoulders, set sharply against a landscape of tiny buildings under a cloudy sky.

Also in the room: Benjamín Palencia, Boceto para decorado de «La Barraca» (Sketch for a "La Barraca" Set) and Máscara. Cartel de «La Barraca» (Mask. That was the idea of the Pedagogical Missions. They began in 1931, under the Second Republic, led by the educator Manuel Bartolomé Cossío. In just under five years they reached almost seven thousand towns and villages.

A year later came a second project. La Barraca was a travelling university theatre, directed by the poet Federico García Lorca and Eduardo Ugarte. Here, though, Dalí is in a classical mood. Buñuel's head and shoulders stand out hard and solid, like blocks, against a landscape of tiny buildings. And the clouds behind him come from an old master. Dalí had seen Mantegna's Death of the Virgin at the Prado, and borrowed its clouds.

### 032 · Room 206.01 · Maruja Mallo and the School of Vallecas
*Room 206.01 · 311 words*

**Where:** Room 206.01, 'They say city and I reply… countryside', a small room of earthy, dark-toned paintings and sculpture. Find Maruja Mallo's Tierra y excrementos, a small picture on paperboard, about 43 by 55 centimetres, in sombre browns.

"They say: city. And I reply: countryside." The sculptor Alberto Sánchez said that, and it sums up this whole room.

Most of the avant-garde fell in love with the big city. Alberto and the painter Benjamín Palencia went the other way. They walked out to the edges of south-east Madrid, to the bare hills, the empty lots and the dry, unwatered fields, and made them the ground of a new art.

Their walks crossed the Almodóvar hill in Vicálvaro, which they renamed Cerro Testigo, Witness Hill. Poets joined their outings: Federico García Lorca, Rafael Alberti, Miguel Hernández. And so did the painter Maruja Mallo. People came to call the group the School of Vallecas.

Palencia had seen the avant-garde first-hand in Paris, and brought Surrealist ideas home. But the Vallecas artists kept their distance from André Breton's rules. For them, Surrealism was a way to look beneath the surface of the land itself. Earth, rock, worn and eroded shapes, things picked up on their walks, all became images full of poetry. The room text calls it a telluric poetics, a poetry of the earth.

Now find Mallo's small painting, Earth and Excrement, from 1932. Think back to her fair in Room 205.06, all bright colour and noise. That was her first manner. In the 1930s her palette turned muted and sombre. The subject here is unsettling, and the paint seems to become earth itself. It belongs to the series Sewers and Bell Towers.

Mallo was a member of this circle, close to Alberto and Palencia both as a painter and as a friend. Around her hang works from the circle: Alberto's Maternity, Palencia's compositions, Rodríguez Luna's Birds in the Melon Field.

From a city fair to a handful of dark earth. That's the journey this room asks you to make.

### 033 · Room 206.02 · Documents — Picasso, Miró, Dalí and the magazine of Georges Bataille
*Room 206.02 · 292 words*

**Where:** Room 206.02, 'Documents Magazine', hung like a collage of paintings, sculpture and photographs. Find Dalí's Millet's Architectonic Angelus, an upright canvas about 73 by 60 centimetres, and Miró's large Painting (The Red Spot), almost one and a half metres tall.

Georges Bataille's magazine was described as "a war machine against pre-conceived ideas." It was called Documents, and around 1929 and 1930 it gave a home to a group of artists who had had enough of André Breton.

You met Breton in Room 205.13, as the leader of Surrealism. For Bataille and his friends, Breton's version was too rigid and too narrow, too focused on literature and on the West. Documents mixed everything. Poets and painters like Michel Leiris, André Masson and Joan Miró shared its pages with experts on coins, on jazz, on archaeology and on the peoples studied by ethnographers.

And Bataille went after the very idea of beauty. He was interested in the distorted, the monstrous and the low. He wanted forms broken open and bodies pulled out of shape. For him, the so-called normal body was something society had invented.

Because the magazine loved photographs, montage and the clash of picture and text, this room is hung like a collage. Let your eyes jump around.

Dalí had a method for this sort of thing. He called it the paranoiac-critical method, a spontaneous way of knowing things through the irrational. His starting point was Millet's famous painting of two peasants praying in a field, a picture of sober nineteenth-century piety that Dalí admired enormously. Then he piled his own erotic meanings on top of it, some hidden, some not hidden at all.

A pious image turned inside out. That is the spirit of this whole room.

### 034 · Room 206.03 · Joan Miró — Pastorale, and Carl Einstein
*Room 206.03 · 332 words*

**Where:** Room 206.03, 'Carl Einstein. The Masses Are the Artist', the last room on this floor, with African masks, Cubist paintings and Civil War photographs. Find Miró's Pastorale, a wide canvas about 60 by 92 centimetres, in black chalk and oil, with a scatter of small signs.

"The act of looking is always equivalent to combat." Carl Einstein wrote that, and he meant it.

He was a poet, critic and theorist of the avant-garde. He believed that finding new ways of seeing carried a political responsibility. He had no time for the idea of the lone genius. And in the Spanish Civil War, he fought with the Durruti Column. Look for Agustí Centelles's photographs here of Buenaventura Durruti leading volunteers to the Aragón front, and of Durruti's burial. He looked at African sculpture the same way, as something shared, social and political. Some of the masks he collected are in this room, Yoruba, Senufo, Baule and Punu. In a studio on the Rue Blomet in Paris, which he shared with André Masson, Miró was working toward what the two of them called painting-poetry. The country theme is still there. In 1975 an artist cut holes in an old building on a New York pier. The police closed it on the day it opened.

The artist was Gordon Matta-Clark. The building stood on Pier 52, on the Hudson River, a relic of the city's industrial past at a time when the port was emptying out. Her starting point was the Abakuá, an Afro-Cuban secret society open only to men. From its myths she took the figure of Sikán, a woman sacrificed by the men of her own community. Ayón saw Sikán as her alter ego.

This print is called Perfidia, Treachery, and dates from 1998. Knowing Sikán's story, it's hard not to think of her as you look. Ayón died the following year, in her early thirties.

Then turn to Julie Mehretu's canvas from 2023, painted in ink and acrylic. Where Ayón tells a myth, Mehretu builds with marks and layers, and leaves you to find your own way in.

Around them, Juan Roberto Diago's self-portrait is made on jute cloth. Agnes Essonti's photo-essay is called A Journey. And the room text gives Pocho Guimaraes the credit of a pioneering voice.

The slogan puts the African at the centre. For a few minutes, this room does exactly that.

Last room on this floor: Room 21, 'Gender Practices', next to the lift. Look for Catherine Opie's colour portraits, each sitter facing straight out.

### 055 · Room 21 · Gender Practices — Pilar Albarracín and Catherine Opie
*Room 21 · 310 words*

**Where:** In Room 21, 'Gender Practices: Social Choreographies for the New Century', the long room beside the lift tower. Find Catherine Opie's colour portraits, each sitter facing you straight on, and Pilar Albarracín's round photographs of different sizes.

**What:** Catherine Opie, Crystal Mason, 1994, and four more works from the series Portraits, chromogenic prints. Pilar Albarracín, Mujeres (Women), 1993, later prints 2024, digital prints on photographic paper, round, 37.7 to 59 cm in diameter. Also: Regina José Galindo, El dolor en un pañuelo (Pain in a Handkerchief), 1999.

Here's a number the museum puts in its own room text. Women artists make up less than fifteen percent of the work it holds. And the next sentence is just as plain: equality at the Reina Sofía "remains a work in progress."

That honesty sets the tone for this last room on Floor 4. It's about the decades since the mid-nineties, when feminism and the fight for LGBTIQ+ rights reshaped what art could show, and who it could show. Queer theory treated gender as something built by society, not fixed by biology.

Pilar Albarracín's photographs date from 1993 and are titled simply Women. They are round, and of different sizes, like portholes onto other lives.

Then come Catherine Opie's portraits. In the mid-nineties she made a series of fifty colour portraits of people in San Francisco: members of the leather community, drag queens, performance artists, transgender people, close friends. Each one is named, by their real name.

Now look at how they're made. Opie borrowed the language of Hans Holbein, the great Renaissance portrait painter. Frontal poses. Restrained gestures. Rich detail. The museum says she builds "dignity, intimacy and permanence."

That's the turn. For centuries, this kind of portrait was kept for white, heterosexual, well-off men. Opie gives the same seriousness to people who were usually pushed out of the frame, and in doing so, the text says, affirms their right to exist and be seen.

Find the portrait of Crystal Mason. The gaze comes back at you as calmly as any sitter's in a Holbein.

That's the end of Floor 4. To finish, take the glass lift tower next to this room down to Floor 1, and follow the signs to the Nouvel building. There, take a Nouvel lift up to the roof terrace on Floor 3. If the terrace is closed today, the last two stops work anywhere.

## Nouvel Roof Terrace

### 056 · Nouvel roof terrace · Soto, Chirino and Negret — A Different Order
*Nouvel roof terrace · Floor 3 · 325 words*

**Where:** On the roof terrace of the Nouvel building, Floor 3. Three sculptures: Soto's Penetrable, a structure almost five metres high hung with flexible tubes; Chirino's long steel form, over four metres across; and Negret's ten red-painted aluminium columns.

**What:** Display 'A Different Order. Utopian Geometry and Kinetic Art'. Jesús Soto, Penetrable, 1982, painted wood and flexible PVC tubes, 485 × 787 × 867 cm. Martín Chirino, Mediterránea (10) (Mediterranean [10]), 1971, welded and polished stainless steel sheet, 180 × 425 × 110 cm. Edgar Negret, Vigilante rojo (Red Watchman), 1979, painted aluminium sheet, 182 × 222 × 163 cm, donated by the artist in 1983.

"There are no longer spectators. There are only participants." Jesús Soto said that about his work in the early eighties, and up here, in the open air, you can test it.

This terrace display is called A Different Order. The curators tell the story of geometric sculpture from the sixties to the eighties. It was a sculpture that needed your body to move around it before it could work. Soon its clean forms spread into city squares and corporate plazas, beside the mirrored glass of new office towers. And then, the curators say, it hardened into a cliché that hid what these sculptors had really achieved.

Start with Soto's Penetrable. Soto was one of the key Latin American artists of kinetic art, and in the late sixties he began a series of spaces like this one: frames hung with flexible tubes, made to be walked through. For him, empty space was not nothing. It was matter in another state, like a jelly you could push aside. Follow the staff's guidance on whether you can step inside today.

Martín Chirino's long steel form is from 1971. He was one of the founders of the El Paso group, with Antonio Saura and Manolo Millares. Here, hollow volumes rise and fall like a wave. The title, Mediterranean, comes from his journeys to Italy and Greece. Its polished finish has a watery shine the museum links to his native Canary Islands, and the whole thing seems to float, defying gravity.

Last, Edgar Negret's Red Watchman. Ten painted columns, each one turned a little further than the one before, as if the figure were swinging round to look at you. A giant version has guarded the presidential palace in Bogotá since 1978. Negret was thinking of Gaudí, and of the warrior-like chimneys on the roof of the Casa Milà in Barcelona. On a rooftop, he's right at home.

Stay here on the terrace for the closing words of the guide.

## Closing

### 057 · Closing — from Guernica to now
*Nouvel roof terrace · Floor 3 · 304 words*

**Where:** Anywhere on the Nouvel roof terrace, Floor 3, or anywhere in the museum if the terrace is closed. Nothing to find: a moment to look back over the visit.

**What:** No single work. Recalls Picasso, Guernica, 1937, Room 205.10; Tino Calabuig, Guernica, 1992, Room 16; Room 15, 'Institutional Genealogy'; Room 21's room text; Jesús Soto, Penetrable, Nouvel roof terrace.

Here's a date that quietly runs this whole museum. 1881, the year Pablo Picasso was born.

In 1992, Guernica left the Casón del Buen Retiro and came here. It travelled in a crate built so the canvas could never roll up, and a lift was built just to bring it in. A few years later, a royal decree drew a line between the two great collections of Madrid. Roughly speaking, artists born before Picasso belong to the Prado. Picasso, and those who came after him, belong here.

So in a sense, the Reina Sofía grew up around one painting. The museum calls Guernica a focal point for the whole collection, meant from the start to be in conversation with other artists of its time and after.

You've heard that conversation today. On Floor 2, the cafés, magazines and posters of the avant-garde, and then a war, painted in grey on a canvas almost eight metres wide in Room 205.10. On Floor 4, the half-century since the dictatorship ended. The Transition. The years of AIDS and grief. Feminism, photography, the African diaspora. And in Room 15, the museum turning the lens on itself, and admitting in Room 21 that equality is still a work in progress.

None of it is finished. The floor in between, the fifties to the seventies, is being rehung, and the avant-garde rooms will change after that. A museum like this is a debate, not a verdict.

Which brings us back to Jesús Soto and his Penetrable. "There are no longer spectators," he said. "There are only participants."

That's you. You walked the route, you looked, you argued with some of it. Thank you for spending this time with us. When you're ready, the Nouvel lifts will take you back down, and the exit signs will lead you out into Madrid.
