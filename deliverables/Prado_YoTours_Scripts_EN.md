# Yo Tours · Museo del Prado · audio guide scripts (EN) · 10 Oct 2026 edition

## Opening

### 001 · Welcome to the Prado
*Jerónimos entrance hall · 345 words*

**Where:** In the Jerónimos entrance hall, the modern hall where you came in, near the signs marked Collection.

**What:** Opening: the royal collection behind the Prado, the Villanueva building and the 1819 opening, how room numbers work, and the shape of today's route.

This museum began on the walls of one family. For centuries, the kings and queens of Spain bought the paintings they loved and hung them in their palaces and monasteries. They answered to no curator. They simply knew what they liked.

The old building next door was designed by Juan de Villanueva in 1785, on the orders of Charles the Third. It was meant for a cabinet of natural history. Instead, his grandson Ferdinand the Seventh filled it with the royal pictures, and in November 1819 the Prado opened to the public. You're standing in its newest part, the extension built beside the old church of the Jerónimos.

That royal taste explains almost everything you're about to see. It's why there is so much Velázquez, Titian, Rubens and Goya. It's why Bosch's strangest dreams ended up in Spain, because Philip the Second collected them eagerly. And it explains the gaps. This is not a tidy history of art. It's a royal collection, gloriously lopsided.

Now, how we'll find our way. Every room has a number, and it's shown at its doorway. Each stop in this guide gives you the number to look for, so the numbers are your best friend. When in doubt, look for the number.

The route has a simple shape. We start here on Floor 0, with the Flemish and Italian masters: Bosch, Bruegel, Dürer and Raphael. Then we go up to Floor 2 for Rembrandt and the Dauphin's Treasure, a glittering hoard of jewelled cups. Next we come down to Floor 1, the heart of the museum, for Titian, El Greco, Velázquez, Murillo, Rubens and Goya. At the far end of the building we follow Goya's story, up to his bright tapestry designs and down to his darkest paintings. And we finish back here, where we began.

Take your time, and sit whenever you need to. Nobody enjoys a masterpiece on tired feet.

To begin, follow the signs marked Collection from this hall to Room 55A, the first room of Flemish painting on this floor. Look for 55A at the doorway.

## Floor 0 · Flemish, Italian & Medieval

### 002 · Room 55A · Patinir — Charon Crossing the Styx
*Room 55A · 302 words*

**Where:** In Room 55A, find the wide panel, about a metre across, of a blue-green landscape split down the middle by a broad river, with a tiny boatman at the centre.

**What:** Joachim Patinir, Charon crossing the Styx, 1520–1524, oil on panel, 64 × 103 cm.

There's a man in a boat in the middle of this painting, and he's about the size of your thumbnail. Yet the whole picture turns on him, and on the passenger he's carrying.

Welcome to the painters of the Low Countries. Around 1500, artists in Flanders began letting the landscape take over the picture. Joachim Patinir was one of the first to make landscape the star, and this panel, painted around 1520, shows how far he could take it. It was probably owned by Philip the Second.

The boatman is Charon, who in Greek myth ferries the dead across the river Styx. But Patinir mixes myth with Christian belief. On the left bank, angels walk with souls through a soft green paradise. On the right, the three-headed dog Cerberus guards the way into hell. The river runs between them like a road with two exits.

Now find the passenger. Charon has reached the middle of the river, the point where the choice is made. The soul sits in strict profile, and the museum says he's facing the easy road, the one that leads to perdition. The decision, it seems, is already taken.

It's a reminder to prepare for death, painted only a few years after Luther shook the Church in 1517. Patinir borrowed ideas from Bosch, whom you'll meet in Room 56A. But X-rays show he took things out as he worked. A giant demon by the riverbank was painted out. Devils and damned souls were thinned out and made smaller. Little by little, the landscape won.

So the real drama here is in the scenery. Still water, blue mountains fading into the distance, and one small soul who has to choose.

Stay in Room 55A. Bruegel's Triumph of Death hangs in this same room, a big brownish panel swarming with skeletons.

### 003 · Room 55A · Bruegel the Elder — The Triumph of Death
*Room 55A · 312 words*

**Where:** Stay in Room 55A and find the large brownish-red panel, wider than your outstretched arms, crowded with skeletons overrunning a burnt landscape.

**What:** Pieter Bruegel the Elder, The Triumph of Death, 1562–1563, oil on panel, 117 × 162 cm.

Nobody gets out of this painting alive. Not the soldier, not the priest, not the lovers. That's the whole point.

Pieter Bruegel the Elder painted it around 1562. An army of skeletons sweeps across the earth. Behind them the land is bare and burning. In front, Death rides a rust-red horse and drives the living into an enormous coffin, with no way out. Every rank of society is here. Some people fight back. Others simply give in. Neither power nor piety saves anyone.

The idea is an old one. In the medieval dance of death, Death leads everyone away in one long line, the rich and the poor together. Bruegel joined that northern tradition to Italian paintings of the triumph of death. The crowds and the moral sting owe a lot to Bosch, next door.

Now listen with your eyes, because this picture is full of music. At the lower left, a skeleton riding a cart piled with skulls plays a hurdy-gurdy. Above it, two skulls haul on ropes to toll bells hung from a dead tree. Three skeletons blow trumpets to announce their mistress. It's a whole band, playing for a funeral.

Then look to the lower right. A young man plays the lute and sings to his companion. She sings from a little music book and beats time. Just behind them, a skeleton has picked up a bowed fiddle and joined in. They are the only two people in the picture who haven't noticed.

The painting passed through Italian and Spanish noble collections before a Spanish queen, Isabella Farnese, bought it in the eighteenth century. She hung it in her palace at La Granja. A queen chose this for her walls.

And the lovers keep singing.

Next door is Room 56A, the Bosch room. Look for 56A at the doorway, and the crowd around a huge painting in three panels.

### 004 · Room 56A · Bosch — The Garden of Earthly Delights
*Room 56A · 331 words*

**Where:** In Room 56A, the enormous open triptych, more than three metres wide: a green Eden on the left, a pink-and-blue crowd of naked figures in the centre, a dark hell on the right. It is usually crowded, so stand back a few paces or come round to one side.

**What:** Hieronymus Bosch, The Garden of Earthly Delights Triptych, 1490–1500, grisaille and oil on oak panel, 185.8 cm high, central panel 172.5 cm wide.

Philip the Second ruled a vast empire from a granite monastery-palace, the Escorial. He was famously serious and devout. And he could not get enough of Hieronymus Bosch.

He kept one Bosch in his private apartment, and he kept buying more. In 1591 he bought this one at a sale, and two years later he sent it to the Escorial. That passion is why this room exists. It's usually busy here, so step back a few paces, or come round to one side.

Bosch painted it in the 1490s, most likely for the Nassau family in Brussels. In 1568 the Duke of Alba seized it from William of Orange, the leader of the Dutch revolt.

Read it from left to right. On the left is paradise. God presents Eve to Adam. Bosch first painted the creation of Eve, then changed his mind. In the centre, the same landscape and the same high horizon carry on, so at first it looks like paradise continued. The museum calls it a false paradise, given over to lust. Hundreds of naked figures play among giant red and blue fruit. Riders on fantastic animals circle a pool of bathing women. And at the edges, two owls stare straight out at you.

On the right is the reckoning, often called the musical hell. Instruments have become instruments of torture. A bird-headed creature, perched on what looks like a child's potty chair, devours the greedy. And inside the hollow body of the tree-man, there's a tavern.

When the wings are closed, all that colour disappears. On the outside, in shades of grey, is the world on the third day of creation, before any of this began.

It's a pessimistic picture about how fragile and fleeting pleasure is. Five centuries on, the pleasure of looking at it hasn't faded at all.

Stay in Room 56A for two more by Bosch. Look for the Haywain, with its huge cart of hay, and a panel painted like a great round eye.

### 005 · Room 56A · Bosch — The Haywain and The Table of the Seven Deadly Sins
*Room 56A · 344 words*

**Where:** Stay in Room 56A. The Haywain is the three-panelled painting with a towering cart of hay in the centre. The Table of the Seven Deadly Sins is the wide panel with a great round eye at its centre, and the small panel with gold lettering is The Extraction of the Stone of Madness.

**What:** Hieronymus Bosch, The Haywain Triptych, 1512–1515, oil on panel; Table of the Seven Deadly Sins, 1505–1510, oil on poplar panel; also The Extraction of the Stone of Madness, 1501–1505, oil on Baltic oak.

If the Garden of Earthly Delights is a riddle, the Haywain is a sermon. Bosch builds it on an old Flemish proverb. The world is a haywain, and each man takes what he can.

Look at the centre panel. A huge cart of hay rolls forward, and people of every class, clergy included, claw at it and fight over it. Behind the cart ride the powerful: a pope, an emperor, a king with French lilies, a duke. On top, a couple enjoy themselves to music, while a guardian angel looks up to Christ in despair. And who is pulling the cart? Devils, steering everyone towards hell on the right wing.

Hay is worth nothing, and the whole world chases it anyway.

Philip the Second bought a version of this painting in 1570, believing it was an original. Technical analysis later showed it was a copy of this one.

Now find the wide panel with the great eye. Philip owned it before 1560 and kept it in his private apartment. At the centre of the eye, in the pupil, Christ rises from his tomb. Beneath him are the Latin words: beware, beware, the Lord is watching. Around the iris run the seven deadly sins, acted out by ordinary people. A woman admires herself in a mirror held by a devil. A family overeats at the table. Two dogs fight over a bone while a couple envy a rich man's falcon. In the four corners wait death, judgement, hell and heaven. Imagine living with that eye on you every day.

Before you leave, find the small panel with gold lettering. A quack in a funnel hat, a sign of deceit, cuts into a man's head to remove the stone of madness. The patient's name is written below: Lubbert Das. And what comes out isn't a stone at all. It's a flower, probably standing for the money the quack is about to take.

Go back into Room 56, then into Room 55, and on to Room 55B, where Dürer's self-portrait hangs. Look for 55B at the doorway.

### 006 · Room 55B · Dürer — Self-portrait, and Adam and Eve
*Room 55B · 335 words*

**Where:** In Room 55B, find the half-length portrait of a young man with long curls, a striped cap and grey gloves, resting his arm on a ledge, with a window behind him. Adam and Eve are the two tall, narrow panels of life-size nudes against a dark background.

**What:** Albrecht Dürer, Self-portrait, 1498, oil on panel, 52 × 41 cm; also Adam and Eve, 1507, oil on panel, each about 209 × 80 cm.

The young man in this portrait is wearing gloves, fine grey kidskin. Those gloves are the whole argument of the painting.

Albrecht Dürer painted himself in 1498, and he tells you so. The inscription says he painted it after his own likeness, at twenty-six. In his day, a painter in Germany was a craftsman, someone who worked with his hands. So Dürer covers his painting hands in gloves, a sign of high rank. He dresses as a gentleman, in a black-and-white doublet and a striped cap, with a cloak tied by a blue-and-white cord. The message is clear. Painting is a liberal art, a work of the mind, and the painter belongs with the learned and the well-born.

The portrait had a long journey to Madrid. The city council of Nuremberg gave it to Charles the First of England. After Charles was executed, his collection was sold, and the painting was bought for Don Luis de Haro, who gave it to Philip the Fourth.

Now turn to the two tall panels, Adam and Eve. Dürer painted them in 1507, after his second trip to Italy. He wanted to join the Italian ideal of the perfect body to the German eye for precise detail. These are the first life-size nudes in all of northern painting. Notice how he tells them apart by colour. Adam is tanned, Eve is a pinkish white. Adam holds a branch with an apple. Eve takes the apple from the serpent.

Look for the little tablet beside Eve. Its inscription dates the work to 1507 years after the Virgin gave birth. The museum reads it as a reminder of Mary as the new Eve, who undoes the sin these two are about to commit.

These panels came to Philip the Fourth as a gift from Queen Christina of Sweden. A self-made gentleman, and the first man and woman, all in one room.

Next door is Room 56B: the early Italian Renaissance. Look for 56B at the doorway, and a large golden Annunciation.

### 007 · Room 56B · Fra Angelico — The Annunciation
*Room 56B · 332 words*

**Where:** In Room 56B, find the large, almost square altarpiece, nearly two metres each way: an angel kneeling to Mary under a pale arched porch, a garden on the left, and a row of small scenes along the bottom.

**What:** Fra Angelico, The Annunciation, 1425–1426, tempera on poplar panel, 190.3 × 191.5 cm.

Around 1420, a young painter in Florence called Guido di Pietro became a Dominican friar. Today we know him as Fra Angelico. And this altarpiece, painted a few years later for the Dominican convent at Fiesole, is one of his first masterpieces.

This is where the Italian rooms begin. In Florence in these years, painters and architects like Masaccio and Brunelleschi were rethinking how to build space and light. They looked back to ancient Rome and out at the real world. That rebirth is what we call the Renaissance.

The subject is the Annunciation. Under a light, arched porch, the angel Gabriel bows towards Mary, and she folds her arms in answer. Look at the slender columns and the shallow room behind her. Angelico is using the new architecture to hold the scene together.

Now look to the left, into the garden. An angel is driving Adam and Eve out of paradise. That's the key to the whole picture. The museum explains that it pairs the loss of paradise with the salvation of humanity. The first sin happens on the left, and the remedy begins on the right.

For a long time, critics dismissed this panel as a late copy, largely by assistants. Today it is seen as the opposite, an early triumph. Even the frame was new. Its plain square shape follows a recommendation Brunelleschi made in 1425, and it is one of the first altarpieces to do so.

Along the bottom, five little scenes tell more of Mary's life. In the wedding scene, look for a trumpeter playing a long straight trumpet for the celebration. And in the garden, flowers and birds are painted one by one, with real care.

The altarpiece stayed in its convent for almost two centuries. Then it was bought and given to a powerful Spanish duke, and it reached the Prado in 1861.

Stay in Room 56B for Mantegna's small Death of the Virgin, a panel with a view of a lake through the window.

### 008 · Room 56B · Mantegna — The Death of the Virgin
*Room 56B · 308 words*

**Where:** Stay in Room 56B and find the small upright panel, about half a metre high: apostles with candles around the Virgin's bier, and a bright window behind them onto a lake and a long bridge.

**What:** Andrea Mantegna, The Death of the Virgin, also called The Dormition of the Virgin, around 1462, mixed method on panel, 54.5 × 42 cm.

Most deathbed scenes close in around the dying. This one opens a window. And through it you can see a real place, the lake and the bridge of Mantua, where Andrea Mantegna worked for the ruling family.

He painted it around 1462, in the years he served the Gonzaga, the lords of Mantua. On the museum's map it's called the Dormition, the falling asleep of the Virgin. According to old legends outside the Bible, Mary called the apostles to her when she knew she was dying. All came except Thomas, who was away preaching in India.

Now look at what they're doing. In the centre, Saint Peter leads the rite, reading from a book. One apostle blesses her with holy water. Another, his back turned to us, swings the incense. The rest stand in two rows, holding candles and singing. On the left, Saint John is the only one without a candle. He holds a palm instead.

Then let your eye go through the window. The lake around Mantua, the long bridge across it, the houses on the far shore. The museum calls it one of the first views of a real, recognisable place in Italian painting. The floor tiles, the pilasters and the window frame all pull you back into that view. Mantegna was a master of perspective, and here it feels effortless.

Something is missing, too. The upper third of the panel has been lost. That missing piece, showing Christ receiving his mother, survives in a gallery in Ferrara.

The painting went from the Gonzaga to Charles the First of England. When his collection was sold, it was bought for Philip the Fourth of Spain. Kings had a way of collecting other kings' collections.

Stay in Room 56B for Botticelli's Nastagio panels, three wide paintings of a story set in a pine forest by the sea.

### 009 · Room 56B · Botticelli — The Story of Nastagio degli Onesti
*Room 56B · 338 words*

**Where:** Stay in Room 56B and find the three wide panels hung in a row, each well over a metre across: a pine forest by the sea, a naked woman chased by a horseman and hounds, and a banquet among the trees.

**What:** Sandro Botticelli and workshop, Scenes from the Story of Nastagio degli Onesti, panels one, two and three, 1483, mixed method on panel, each about 83 × 140 cm. The fourth panel is in a private collection.

These paintings were a wedding present. A naked woman, hunted through a forest by a knight and his dogs. Every Friday. For years.

The story comes from Boccaccio's Decameron. Nastagio, a young man from Ravenna, loves a daughter of the noble Traversari family. She will have nothing to do with him. Heartbroken, he leaves the city. In 1483, Sandro Botticelli and his workshop told the story in four panels. Three are here, and the fourth is in a private collection.

Start with the panel on the left. In a pine forest by the sea, Nastagio sees a woman running, savaged by mastiffs and chased by a horseman. The rider explains. He loved her, she scorned him, and he died for it. When she died too, she was condemned to this. Every Friday he hunts her down, for as many years as the months she ignored him.

In the second panel, the knight tears out her heart and throws it to the hounds. Look into the background. The chase is starting all over again.

Now the third. Nastagio has a plan. He invites the Traversari family to a banquet in that same pine wood. Just as dessert is served, the ghostly hunt appears, exactly as he predicted. The guests are horrified. And the young lady decides, on reflection, that she would rather marry Nastagio than end up like that. On the right, her maid comes to him with a less proper offer. He turns it down. He wants an honourable marriage.

These panels were commissioned by Antonio Pucci for the wedding of his son to Lucrezia Bini. Lorenzo de' Medici helped arrange the match, and on the third panel the Medici arms hang beside those of the two families. The fourth panel, the one that got away, shows the wedding feast.

A lovely gift. With a very clear message for the bride.

Go through the doorway into the long hall, Room 49, for Raphael. Look for 49 at the doorway, and a cardinal in red against a black background.

### 010 · Room 49 · Raphael — The Cardinal, and The Holy Family 'La Perla'
*Room 49 · 335 words*

**Where:** In the long hall of Room 49, find the half-length portrait of a cardinal in a shining red cape against a black background. The Pearl is the larger, upright painting nearby of the Virgin and Child with two children and an older woman, and ruins in a landscape on the right.

**What:** Raphael, The Cardinal, 1510–1511, oil on panel, 79 × 61 cm; and The Holy Family, or 'The Pearl', around 1518, oil on panel, 147.4 × 116 cm.

Nobody knows who this man is. For generations, scholars have hunted through the lives of Renaissance cardinals for a face to match. They're still not sure.

Raphael painted him around 1510, in Rome. The most likely candidate is Cardinal Giovanni Alidosi. The writer Pietro Bembo said that for Alidosi, faith was never certain, pure or sacred. Whether or not it's him, the painting fits that description very well.

Look at how little Raphael needs. A triangle of red against black, an idea he learned from Leonardo. No props, nothing to distract you. The red silk cape catches the light with a sheen that shows Raphael had been looking at Venetian painting. And then the face. Raphael moved the left eye as he worked, and you can still see the change with your own eyes. That small shift gives the gaze its edge. He's not quite looking at you. He's weighing you up.

Now turn to the larger painting, The Pearl. When Philip the Fourth saw it, the story goes, he said, here is the pearl of my paintings. The nickname stuck.

It's a gentle family scene. Mary sits with the Christ Child between her knees, and he rests one foot on a wicker cradle. Little John the Baptist offers him fruit from the fold of his fur. Behind them, Saint Anne, Mary's mother, kneels with her eyes closed. And on the left, Joseph is half hidden in the shadows of a window opening. The figures form a soft pyramid, another lesson from Leonardo.

It belonged to the Gonzaga of Mantua and to Charles the First of England before it came to Philip. French troops later took it to Paris, and it came home again.

One sitter nobody can name, and one painting a king named for good.

Walk to the far end of Room 49 and go on through Room 58B into Room 58, for Van der Weyden's Descent from the Cross. Look for 58 at the doorway, and a large gold-backed panel of mourners.

### 011 · Room 58 · Rogier van der Weyden — The Descent from the Cross
*Room 58 · 320 words*

**Where:** In Room 58, the large panel over two and a half metres wide, with near life-size mourners packed into a shallow gold box, the pale body of Christ in the middle and the Virgin fainting below him.

**What:** Rogier van der Weyden, The Descent from the Cross, before 1443, oil on panel, 204.5 × 259 cm.

At first glance, this looks like a carved wooden altarpiece, the kind with painted statues standing in a gilded box. It isn't. It's flat. Every figure is paint.

Rogier van der Weyden made it before 1443 for the Great Crossbowmen's guild of Leuven, for their chapel outside the city walls. Look up into the top corners, in the gold tracery. Two tiny crossbows hang there, the guild's signature.

Rogier squeezes about ten near life-size figures into that shallow box. There's no distance and no landscape, nowhere for your eye to escape. Joseph of Arimathea and Nicodemus take the weight of the body. A young man on the ladder helps from above. On the right, Mary Magdalene bends over, wringing her hands. Look at the faces. Almost everyone is crying, and the tears are painted one by one.

Now find the centre. Christ's pale body hangs in a long, slack curve. Directly below him, his mother has fainted, and her body falls in almost the same line. Her collapse mirrors his death. The museum points out that she is given the same prominence as her son. Their grief is one shape.

Down at the foot of the cross lie a skull and a bone. Tradition held that Adam was buried on this spot, so the death of Christ falls right where the first sin is laid to rest.

This blend of real detail and an unreal, golden setting struck painters of the time with enormous force. It left a deep mark on Flemish painting. Mary of Hungary acquired it for her palace chapel at Binche. It came to Spain, and Philip the Second later sent it to the Escorial, where it stayed for more than three and a half centuries.

Take a moment with it before you move on.

Stay in Room 58 for Antonello da Messina's Dead Christ, a much smaller panel of Christ held up by a single angel.

### 012 · Room 58 · Antonello da Messina — The Dead Christ supported by an Angel
*Room 58 · 278 words*

**Where:** Stay in Room 58 and find the small upright panel, about seventy centimetres high: the dead Christ seated and held up from behind by a single weeping angel, with a harbour town in the distance.

**What:** Antonello da Messina, The Dead Christ supported by an Angel, 1475–1476, mixed method on panel, 74 × 51 cm.

An angel, hardly more than a child, is holding up the body of Christ. He can barely manage the weight. And he's crying.

Antonello da Messina painted this small panel around 1475, just after a trip to Venice. There he saw the Pietàs of Giovanni Bellini, with the dead Christ held up by angels, and he borrowed the idea. Then he took it home to Sicily.

Look past the figures, into the background. That harbour town is Messina, Antonello's own city. He paints it with tiny, northern precision, the same patience he gives to every strand of Christ's hair. But the body itself is pure Italy. It has real weight and volume, a heavy body that sags against the angel's arm.

Now look at the angel's face, pressed close to Christ's. An inventory written in 1633 already described him as weeping. That's what people have noticed first, for almost four centuries.

And yet, for most of that time, nobody knew who painted it. A Spanish countess received it as a work by Dürer. It ended up in a convent in Galicia, in the north of Spain. When Napoleon's armies invaded, the nuns hid their treasures in private houses. When it finally reached the Prado in 1965, it was first given to a Venetian painter, Cima da Conegliano. Only two years later was it confirmed as Antonello.

So this little panel waited a very long time to be recognised. Northern detail, Italian weight, and a Sicilian town on the horizon. It was always exactly what it is.

Next door is Room 58A, for Memling. Look for 58A at the doorway, and a long, low altarpiece of the three kings.

### 013 · Room 58A · Memling — Triptych of the Adoration of the Magi
*Room 58A · 327 words*

**Where:** In Room 58A, find the long, low altarpiece in three parts, almost three metres wide: the three kings before the Virgin and Child in a ruined building at the centre, the Nativity on the left wing and the Presentation in the Temple on the right.

**What:** Hans Memling, Triptych of the Adoration of the Magi, 1470–1472, oil on Baltic oak, 95 × 271 cm.

After the grief of the last room, this is a deep breath. Nobody faints here. Nobody weeps. Hans Memling gives you order, calm and jewel-bright colour.

He painted this triptych around 1470. And he had a model. The centre panel and the right wing follow an altarpiece by Rogier van der Weyden, the painter of the Descent from the Cross you've just seen. Memling took Rogier's design and smoothed away the drama.

Start in the centre. The Virgin sits with the Child in front of a great column, part of a ruined building with broken arches. The first king kneels and kisses the Child's foot. The second kneels on the left, in an ermine cape and gold brocade, holding out his gift in a metal vessel. The third stands on the right, an African king. The museum explains the tradition behind him. Each king stood for one of the three continents known at the time, so the whole world comes to the stable. Behind them, attendants carry banners, one with a crescent and a star from the East.

Now the left wing, the Nativity. Mary kneels before the naked Child, who lies on her cloak. And here's a lovely touch. Joseph comes in holding a candle, and he cups his hand around the flame to keep it from blowing out.

On the right wing, Mary hands her son to the old priest Simeon in the Temple. Look for the sword hilt at Simeon's robe. It recalls his prophecy that a sword would pierce Mary's soul.

The triptych belonged to the Spanish royal collection and hung for a time in a castle oratory near Toledo, then at Aranjuez. It was a picture to pray with, quietly, up close.

Go back through Room 58 and Room 58B, then through Room 50 into the round Room 51. From there, go through Room 51B into Room 51A: medieval Spain. Look for a tall gold panel of a saint on a throne.

### 014 · Room 51A · Bermejo — Saint Dominic of Silos enthroned as a Bishop
*Room 51A · 336 words*

**Where:** In Room 51A, find the tall gold panel, almost two and a half metres high: a stern bishop-saint seated face-on on a gilded throne, in a gold brocade cope, with small colourful female figures set into the sides of the throne.

**What:** Bartolomé Bermejo, Saint Dominic of Silos enthroned as a Bishop, 1474–1477, oil on panel, 242 × 130 cm.

This saint doesn't look at you. He doesn't need to. He sits face-on, perfectly still, wrapped in more gold than you can take in at once.

These rooms around Room 51 hold some of the museum's oldest paintings, from medieval Spain. In the fifteenth century, Spanish painters looked hard at Flanders, at the oil paint and close detail you've just seen. They married it to the gold and glory of the medieval altar. The museum calls this panel one of the most representative images of Spanish Gothic painting.

Bartolomé Bermejo painted it between 1474 and 1477, as the centrepiece of a great altarpiece for the church of Saint Dominic of Silos in Daroca, in Aragon. Its carved frame survives too, and it's also in the Prado. Imagine it in place, with real wooden pinnacles and tracery around this painted throne. Bermejo was playing with you. Which gold is carved, and which is only paint?

Now go closer. The cope is gold brocade, woven with a pattern of pine cones or artichokes. Its border is a row of tiny saints, each standing under its own little canopy. The mitre is so exact that Bermejo may have copied a real one he had seen. The knot on the crozier looks like a monstrance made for Girona Cathedral.

Then look at the sides of the throne. Small women in bright colours sit in its niches. They are the Virtues, faith, hope and charity among them. They surround the saint like a guard of honour, a sign of his moral perfection.

Face and pose are rigid and ancient. The fabric, metal and jewels are as real as anything in Flanders. That tension is the whole charm of Spanish painting in these years.

Back in the round Room 51, take the lift up to Floor 2 and follow the signs to Rooms 76 to 83. If you take the stairs and they stop at Floor 1, follow the signs for Rooms 76 to 83 from there. Rembrandt is in Room 76.

## Floor 2 · Rembrandt & the Dauphin's Treasure

### 015 · Room 76 · Rembrandt — Judith at the Banquet of Holofernes
*Room 76 · 324 words*

**Where:** In Room 76, the Rembrandt room at the end of this wing. Stand before the large, almost square canvas of a young woman in gold, pearls and ermine, a kneeling girl offering her a shell-shaped cup, and an old woman in a white cap in the shadows behind.

**What:** Rembrandt Harmensz. van Rijn, Judith at the Banquet of Holofernes, 1634, oil on canvas, 143 × 154.7 cm (formerly called Artemisia); inscription "Rembrandt / f : 1634", signature considered doubtful by the museum, date accepted. Short intro to the Dutch and Flemish rooms 76–83.

Rembrandt painted this canvas in 1634, when he was about twenty-eight. And people have argued ever since about who she is. A widowed queen, about to drink her husband's ashes? Or a heroine on her way to kill a general?

These top-floor rooms hold Flemish and Dutch painting. For historical reasons, many of the great Dutch names never reached the Spanish royal collection. The museum has described this as the only fully accepted Rembrandt in a Spanish public collection.

For a long time she was called Artemisia, the queen who mixed her husband's ashes into her wine. The museum now reads her differently. It sees Judith, the widow who went into the enemy camp, sat at the banquet of the general Holofernes, and came home with his head.

So look for clues. A girl kneels with her back to us, offering a cup made from a nautilus shell on a golden stem. Behind, in the shadows, an old servant in a white cap holds a cloth. Look again. It's a half-open sack, with a tasselled cord hanging down. In the story, that's what Judith's maid carries the head home in.

X-rays show that Rembrandt changed his mind. Another figure once stood between the two women, perhaps holding a mirror. And the black background was added much later, by a restorer.

Now the twist. For the Dutch, Judith was a patriotic heroine, a symbol of their stand against Spain. Yet the painting turns up in the collection of a Spanish minister, the Marquis of Ensenada, listed simply as a Judith. Later it hangs in the palace of King Charles the Third.

So enjoy the light on her pearls, the gold chain, the soft ermine at her shoulders. A rebels' heroine, glowing in the king's house.

### 016 · Room 79B · The Treasure of the Dauphin — Cup with a gold mermaid
*Room 79B · 307 words*

**Where:** In the round, darkened Room 79B, where the whole treasure stands in one long curved case. Walk along it to find a small gold mermaid with two enamelled tails, holding a carved agate bowl above her head.

**What:** Anonymous, Cup with a gold mermaid, 1550–1575, gold, enamel, rubies, diamonds and agate, sculpted, repoussé and enamelled, 17.4 cm high; Treasure of the Dauphin (Tesoro del Delfín).

One hundred and seventy-nine rubies and five diamonds. That's what a palace clerk counted on this little cup in 1746. Count them today and you'll find a hundred and seventy-seven rubies, and just two diamonds. Somewhere along the way, a few stones went missing.

You're standing in the Dauphin's Treasure. These vessels belonged to Louis, the Grand Dauphin, heir to the throne of France. When he died, they passed to his son, Philip the Fifth, the first Bourbon king of Spain. In the nineteenth century they came to the Prado. Now the whole treasure is shown together in this round room, in a single curved case about forty metres long.

Now find the mermaid. She's small, about seventeen centimetres high. She has a golden body, a headdress of enamelled feathers, and not one tail but two, curling away in coloured enamel. With both arms she lifts a bowl of carved agate above her head. Below, four little enamelled fish, perhaps dolphins, hold everything up on an agate base.

What was it for? The museum suggests it may have been a salt cellar. Imagine the salt arriving at a royal table, carried by a mermaid. Who made it is a puzzle too. It has been called Florentine, but it was probably the work of a French goldsmith, made under the spell of the great Cellini.

It has had adventures. In 1813 the treasure was stolen, and it came back in 1816. And this cup has lost one piece for good: a little dolphin that once sat between the mermaid's tails.

So lean in close. More than four centuries old, no taller than your hand, and still a few rubies short.

### 017 · Room 82 · Clara Peeters — Still Life with a Sparrow Hawk, Fowl, Porcelain and Shells
*Room 82 · 334 words*

**Where:** In Room 82, in front of a mid-sized panel on a dark ground: a sparrowhawk perched on the rim of a wicker basket full of dead birds, a stack of white porcelain, and a few seashells.

**What:** Clara Peeters, Still Life with a Sparrow Hawk, Fowl, Porcelain and Shells, 1611, oil on panel, 52 × 71 cm, signed and dated "CLARA P. Ao. 1611".

We know almost nothing about the woman who painted this. Not her teacher, not her exact birth, not even whether she kept painting in later life. What survives is the work, and a signature. It's on the front, low in the centre: Clara P., and the year 1611.

Clara Peeters probably worked in Antwerp, and she was a pioneer. She was one of the first painters of her time to specialise in still life, and one of very few women painting for a living. The Prado owns four of her pictures. When it finally gave a whole exhibition to a woman painter, the first in its history, it chose her.

Now look at what she's laid out. On the rim of a wicker basket perches a sparrowhawk, painted about life-size. It's a female, the larger of the two. Around her lies the catch. A mallard in the basket, a woodcock, a hen, two plucked young pigeons on a red clay plate. Tiny finches hang by their necks from a twig. Find the bullfinch on the right.

Then the luxury. A stack of white plates and bowls sits on a blue and white dish. This is kraak porcelain, the ware shipped from China. Its blue has faded with time. And there are four seashells, each one painted so exactly that experts can name the species.

Peeters had a private joke. In at least eight of her paintings she hid tiny reflections of herself, on the side of a goblet or the lid of a jug. There's no shiny goblet on this ledge, so you won't find her here. Her signature will have to do.

By 1666 this panel was very likely in the Spanish royal collection in Madrid. A woman's still life in the king's palace, three and a half centuries before the museum gave her a show of her own.

### 018 · Room 83 · Jan Brueghel the Elder and Rubens — The Sense of Sight
*Room 83 · 302 words*

**Where:** In Room 83, among the five panels of the Senses. Find the wide one showing a palatial picture gallery crammed with paintings, busts and instruments, where a seated Venus looks at a picture Cupid holds up for her.

**What:** Jan Brueghel the Elder and Peter Paul Rubens, The Sense of Sight, 1617, oil on panel, 64.7 × 109.5 cm, from the series of the Five Senses; the other Senses hang in Room 83 too.

Hidden in this painting is one of the newest gadgets of its day. It's a telescope, modelled on the very first one ever patented, in 1608. Jan Brueghel painted it here only nine years later.

This is Sight, one of five panels on the five senses, and its companions hang here with it. Since Aristotle, sight had been counted the most important sense, so it gets the grandest stage. Two Flemish masters shared the work. Brueghel, nicknamed Velvet Brueghel, built the rooms and the treasures in tiny, glittering strokes. Rubens added the figures. The museum calls it one of their most successful collaborations.

At the centre sits Venus. Cupid holds up a painting for her, and it's a Christian subject: Christ healing a blind man. Sight of the eyes, and sight of the soul. Nearby, a copy of Raphael's Saint Cecilia hangs among ancient Roman busts.

Now go hunting. Find the telescope. Then the magnifying glasses, and even a pair of pince-nez. Look for compasses, and instruments for reading the stars. Look up at the paintings stacked frame to frame towards the ceiling.

Brueghel was a protégé of the Archdukes Albert and Isabella, his great patrons. He slipped their portraits into the picture, and a view of their palace at Mariemont. The set later passed from prince to prince as a gift, until a Spanish duke gave it to the king, most likely Philip the Fourth.

One last thing to find. The painting is signed and dated on a little sheet of paper, close to Venus's seat. It's a picture about the pleasure of looking, and it rewards anyone who keeps looking.

## Floor 1 · Titian, El Greco & Velázquez

### 019 · Room 1 · Floor 1 — the round room by the Goya entrance · Leoni's Charles V and the Fury
*Room 1 · 262 words*

**Where:** In Room 1, the round room on Floor 1 by the Goya entrance. Find the life-size bronze of the emperor Charles V, standing over a fallen figure among a heap of weapons on a tall pedestal.

**What:** Leone Leoni and Pompeo Leoni, Emperor Carlos V and Fury, 1551–1555, cast bronze, about 2.5 m high; the emperor, his detachable armour, Fury and the pedestal are separate pieces.

In 1549, the emperor Charles the Fifth ordered a bronze of himself. What he got has a secret: the armour comes off.

First, where you are. This is Floor 1, the main floor, and you're in Room 1, the round room by the Goya entrance. From here the long Central Gallery runs the length of the building, like a spine. The great Spanish rooms, El Greco, Ribera and Velázquez, open off one side of it. We'll start with Titian, just beside this room, then work our way round to Velázquez.

Now the emperor. The sculptor was Leone Leoni, and the figure and its armour were cast in Milan. His son Pompeo later finished the work in Spain. At the emperor's feet lies Fury, the image of disorder and rebellion, among a pile of weapons. The Latin words say it plainly: Fury, tamed by Caesar's virtue.

And here's the secret. The armour is a separate bronze shell, held on with large screws. Take it off, and underneath stands the emperor as a nude hero, in the classical manner. Look closely at how he's shown today, and see which emperor you've got.

The museum has been moving its Leoni bronzes. If this group isn't here in Room 1, you'll find the Leoni bronzes in the Central Gallery, Room 27, later on this route.

### 020 · Room 42 · Titian — The Andrians
*Room 42 · 334 words*

**Where:** In Room 42, in front of the big, almost square canvas of men and women drinking, dancing and sprawling on a grassy slope, with a sleeping nude in the lower right corner and a ship sailing off in the distance.

**What:** Titian, The Andrians, 1523–1526, oil on canvas, 175 × 193 cm, signed "Ticianus F."; also Titian, Self-portrait, around 1562, oil on canvas, in Room 41.

Down in the grass at the bottom of this picture lies a sheet of music. It's real, and you could still sing it. It's a round by Adriaen Willaert, a Flemish musician at the court of Ferrara. The words say, more or less, that whoever drinks and doesn't drink again doesn't know what drinking is.

Titian was the painter of the Habsburgs. The emperor Charles the Fifth made him a knight in 1533, and he worked for the family for over forty years. Yet he refused, again and again, to come and settle at the Spanish court. He stayed in Venice. He'd already said no to the Duke of Ferrara, who collected pagan scenes like this one for a private room.

The island is Andros, so loved by the wine god Bacchus that a stream there runs with wine. In the background an old man lies on a bed of branches. He's probably the river itself. But where's Bacchus? Look out to sea. A ship is sailing away, and we're meant to imagine the god and his followers on board. The party carries on without him.

Now find the sleeping woman in the lower right corner, a splendid nude, bathed in light. She isn't Ariadne, as some have claimed. Titian seems to have worked from a living model. And just beside her, a little boy is cheerfully relieving himself. For Titian's audience, he stood for laughter.

The picture was copied again and again by later painters. Its signature is easy to miss: Ticianus, low in the centre.

You passed the man himself in Room 41. In his self-portrait he's in his seventies, seen in profile, which was rare. Otherwise, Titian painted profiles only of sitters who had already died. He holds a brush and wears the golden chain of his knighthood. Painter and knight, as his medal by Leone Leoni put it.

### 021 · Room 44 · Titian — Danaë, and the Venus with an Organist
*Room 44 · 343 words*

**Where:** In Room 44. Stand before the wide canvas of a nude princess lying on a bed as a dark cloud bursts into golden rain, an old woman reaching to catch it; the two big pictures of Venus reclining with an organist hang in the same room.

**What:** Titian, Danaë and the Shower of Gold, 1560–1565, oil on canvas, 129.8 × 181.2 cm; also Titian, Venus with an Organist and a Dog, around 1550, and Venus with an Organist and Cupid, around 1555, both oil on canvas, Room 44.

There's someone in this painting who isn't interested in the god at all. Look at the old woman on the right. While a god pours down from the sky, she's holding out her skirt to catch the gold.

In the myth, Danaë was a princess locked away from every man, until Jupiter reached her as a shower of golden rain. In Titian's first version, made in Rome for a cardinal, a little Cupid stood at her feet. When he painted her again for Philip the Second, he swapped the Cupid for this old servant. Youth beside old age.

The canvas in front of you is a later version, from the 1560s. And the museum thinks it was Velázquez who found it, on his first trip to Italy. By 1634 it had been bought, with seventeen other pictures, for the new Buen Retiro palace in Madrid.

Now step close. The brushstrokes are short and broken, and the paint is so thin in places that you can see the weave of the canvas through it. Even the little dog may be a hint at desire.

Then turn to the two Venuses in this room. In each, the goddess reclines while a young organist plays and gazes at her. So play spot the difference. In one, there's a little dog, and she wears a wedding ring. That picture was probably made for a marriage. Its organist plays with one hand only. In the other, a Cupid takes the dog's place, and the organist plays with both hands.

The dog version has a royal history of its own. It belonged to King Charles the First of England, and after his death Philip the Fourth bought it.

In the nineteenth century this Danaë was kept in a reserved room, away from general view. Now she shares a room with everyone.

### 022 · Room 2 · Claude Lorrain — The Embarkation of Saint Paula
*Room 2 · 298 words*

**Where:** In Room 2, just off the round Room 1. Look for the tall harbour scene, taller than it is wide, with palaces lining the quay, ships at anchor, and a low sun blazing straight at you over the water.

**What:** Claude Lorrain, The Embarkation of Saint Paula, around 1639, oil on canvas, 211 × 145 cm, inscribed "IMBARCO / STA · PAVLA / ROMANA / PER TERRA STA".

This port never existed. Claude Lorrain built it in his imagination, as the ancient harbour of Ostia, near Rome. Then he quietly furnished it with real buildings. Among the palaces is the Villa Medici in Rome, and far out on the horizon stands the lighthouse of Genoa.

Claude was working in Rome. Around 1639 he painted this as one of four big canvases for a new royal palace in Madrid, the Buen Retiro. All four belong to the Prado. Each set a sacred story inside a landscape.

The story here is Paula's. She was a Roman widow who gave up her worldly life for prayer and penitence, and sailed east to join Saint Jerome. So where is she? Look among the crowd in the foreground. A whole party has come down to the quay to see her off. She is there among them, small and easy to miss. If you're not sure, the painter left a label. Somewhere on the canvas, an inscription spells it out: the embarkation of Saint Paula of Rome, for the Holy Land.

But be honest: your eye didn't go to Paula first, it went to the sun. Claude puts it low over the sea, right at the far end of the harbour, and lets its golden light pour towards you between the buildings. The museum says the light partially blinds the viewer. That's exactly the effect. You squint a little, the way you would on a real quayside.

Notice how the buildings and ships frame that light like the wings of a stage. Everything leads your eye out to sea, along the route Paula is about to take.

A saint's farewell, turned into a blaze of light.

Next door, Room 3: Poussin's Parnassus, with Apollo enthroned among the Muses on a green hillside.

### 023 · Room 3 · Poussin — Parnassus
*Room 3 · 310 words*

**Where:** In Room 3, in front of the wide canvas of a sunlit hillside crowded with figures: Apollo seated at the centre among the Muses and poets, a poet being crowned with laurel, and a nude woman reclining by a spring in the foreground.

**What:** Nicolas Poussin, Parnassus, 1630–1631, oil on canvas, 145 × 197 cm.

Every poet dreams of a welcome like this one. On the mountain of Parnassus, home of Apollo and the Muses, a poet is being received among the gods. Apollo offers him a drink of nectar, and a Muse sets a laurel wreath on his head.

Nicolas Poussin was a Norman who made his life in Rome, and he died there. He painted this around 1630, and he did it with a famous model in mind. The composition is based on Raphael's fresco of Parnassus, in the Vatican.

So who is the poet? Probably Homer, the first of them all. The woman crowning him is Calliope, the Muse of epic poetry. But there may be a second guest of honour, invisible this time. The painting may pay homage to the Italian poet Giovanni Battista Marino, who had been Poussin's patron. A thank-you, perhaps, in the language Marino loved best.

Now look at the bottom of the picture. The nude woman reclining in the foreground is Castalia, the sacred spring of the mountain. Little putti pass her water round to the poets. This was the water of inspiration, and whoever drank it could write.

See how calmly it's all arranged. Figures sit and stand along the slope in a gentle rhythm, nobody shouting, nobody falling. The colours are clear and cool. After Titian's wild party back in Room 42, this is a different idea of pleasure, a pleasure of the mind.

Its first known owners were the Mattei family in Rome. By 1727 it belonged to Philip the Fifth, at his palace of La Granja.

Nectar from Apollo, water from the spring. Poussin's point is simple: poetry is a gift from the gods, and then you still have to drink it.

### 024 · Room 5 · Artemisia Gentileschi — The Birth of Saint John the Baptist
*Room 5 · 287 words*

**Where:** In Room 5, in front of the wide canvas of an interior where several women gather round a naked newborn baby, while the old mother rests in bed behind them with an elderly man before her.

**What:** Artemisia Gentileschi, The Birth of Saint John the Baptist, around 1635, oil on canvas, 184 × 258 cm, inscribed "ARTEMITIA / GINTILES[...]" at the lower left.

Six large canvases were ordered in Naples for the king of Spain's new palace in Madrid. Four went to Massimo Stanzione, and one went to Paolo Finoglia, though his is now lost. The last went to a woman, and this is hers.

Artemisia Gentileschi was born in Rome and died in Naples. Around 1635 she was working in Naples for the Spanish viceroy. The set was to tell the life of John the Baptist, for the Buen Retiro palace. Her part was the very beginning.

In the Gospel, John's parents are old. Elizabeth has long been unable to have children, and the baby is a miracle. But look how Artemisia tells it. The miracle stays in the background, where Elizabeth rests in her bed and old Zechariah sits in front of her. The real action is in the foreground, among the women.

Several of them gather round the naked newborn. They tend him and lean in close, attentive and practical. There are only capable hands, and the ordinary business of a birth. It's a holy scene, told as a room full of women at work.

Now look right down at the lower left edge of the canvas. There's a signature, at the very bottom of the original painting. Artemitia Gintiles, it reads, and then the letters wear away. Below it, the canvas was later extended, and painted by a less skilful hand.

She signed at the edge of a picture for a king, in a set where every other painter was a man. And she made sure her name was on it.

### 025 · Room 7A · Caravaggio — David with the Head of Goliath
*Room 7A · 316 words*

**Where:** In Room 7A, in front of a mid-sized dark canvas: a young David, lit against black shadow, straddling the giant's body and bending to tie up Goliath's severed head by the hair.

**What:** Caravaggio, David with the Head of Goliath, around 1600, oil on canvas, 110.4 × 91.3 cm.

For years, the Prado's own catalogue didn't call this a Caravaggio. In 1872 it was listed as school of Caravaggio, the work of a follower. It took a restoration in the 1940s, and the Italian scholar Roberto Longhi, before the experts agreed. Even then, the museum admits, some agreed reluctantly.

Look at what the painter actually shows you. This is not the hero's victory pose. David isn't holding the head up for a crowd. He's straddling the giant's body, bending down, and tying the severed head by the hair with a rope, ready to carry it off. The famous moment is over. This is the dirty work that comes after.

The boy comes out of a shadowy background, caught in the light, with everything else falling away into dark. The colours are earthy, mostly ochres. And the brushwork is firm and sure.

Then science got involved. When the head of Goliath was X-rayed, it showed that Caravaggio first painted the giant just after death, wild-eyed, his mouth wide open in a scream. He painted over it. The face you see now is far more restrained. More recent tests found another change. At first, David's right ankle was wrapped in cloths. Caravaggio dropped that too.

The canvas has had its own adventures. At one point in the eighteenth century it was enlarged to nearly two metres tall. And X-rays found a number, 449, painted in white in the lower left margin. It was an old royal inventory number, covered up before the picture came to the Prado.

So the painting that was once only school of Caravaggio turned out to be the real thing. And the scream is still under there, just out of sight.

### 026 · Room 8B · El Greco — The Nobleman with his Hand on his Chest
*Room 8B · 293 words*

**Where:** In Room 8B, the first El Greco room. Look for the small half-length portrait of a man in black against a dark ground, a white ruff at his throat and his right hand spread flat on his chest.

**What:** El Greco, The Nobleman with his Hand on his Chest, around 1580, oil on canvas, 81.8 × 66.1 cm. Short intro to El Greco.

People have wanted this man to be somebody famous. Miguel de Cervantes, perhaps, the author of Don Quixote. Or the courtier Antonio Pérez. The truth is, nobody knows who he is.

The painter, at least, we know rather better. Domenikos Theotokopoulos was born on Crete in 1541, and Spain simply called him El Greco, the Greek. His colour owes much to Venice, and his heroic figures to Michelangelo in Rome. Then he came to Spain, settled in Toledo, and died there. This is one of the first portraits he painted in Spain.

As for the sitter, the suggestion the museum finds most convincing is Juan de Silva, Marquis of Montemayor, a military commander who also held the post of chief notary. And that would explain the hand. Spread flat on his chest, it looks like a man taking an oath. For a notary, swearing to the truth was the job.

Now spend a moment with him. Almost everything is black, the doublet and the background. Against it, three things stand out: the pale face, the white ruff, and that hand, its long fingers spread on his heart. Look just below it for the gilded hilt of his sword, glinting against the black. A gentleman's sword, and a gentleman's word.

His eyes meet yours, calm and steady. He isn't performing for anyone, he's simply there, quietly promising something.

The portrait came to the royal collection as a gift. The widow of the Duke of Arco gave it, and the duke had owned six El Greco portraits of gentlemen.

We may never learn his name. But four centuries later, he's still keeping his promise.

### 027 · Room 8B · El Greco — The Holy Trinity
*Room 8B · 311 words*

**Where:** Stay in Room 8B and find the very tall canvas, about three metres high: an old, bearded God the Father on a throne of clouds, holding the pale dead body of Christ, the white dove above and angels in bright robes crowding round.

**What:** El Greco, The Holy Trinity, 1577–1579, oil on canvas, 300 × 179 cm.

When El Greco arrived in Spain, this was his first commission. Everything depended on it. And he painted it for a place where nobody would ever see it up close.

The canvas once crowned the main altarpiece of the convent church of Santo Domingo el Antiguo, in Toledo. It hung high above the altar, with a painting of the Holy Face set even higher. The museum points out that it had to work at a great height. That's partly why the figures are so massive, and why the perspective works the way it does.

Now look at the subject. We usually picture the Trinity in glory, enthroned in heaven. El Greco gives us grief instead. God the Father, old and bearded, sits on a throne of clouds and holds the dead body of his son. Above them hovers the white dove of the Holy Spirit. It's the Trinity, and it's a lament.

He borrowed the idea from a composition by Albrecht Dürer. And the body of Christ owes its heavy, heroic weight to Michelangelo. But the colour is El Greco's own Venetian lesson. Christ is drained almost to grey, a cadaver among ashen clouds. Around him, the angels blaze in blue, carmine red, green, purple and yellow, under a golden sky. Life all around, and death at the centre.

Look at the Father's face, and at his hands. This is not a judge. It's a parent holding a child who has died.

In 1832 King Ferdinand the Seventh bought it for the royal collection.

And here's the thread to follow. El Greco's first Spanish commission was for Santo Domingo el Antiguo. Some thirty-five years later, his very last painting was made for the same convent, to mark his family's grave. That's where we go now.

Next door, Room 9B: El Greco's last great altarpieces, tall canvases glowing out of the dark.

### 028 · Room 9B · El Greco — The Adoration of the Shepherds and the late altarpieces
*Room 9B · 355 words*

**Where:** In Room 9B, among very tall canvases. Start with The Adoration of the Shepherds, a night scene over three metres high where all the light comes from the newborn Christ, a banner of angels above.

**What:** El Greco, The Adoration of the Shepherds, 1612–1614, oil on canvas, 319 × 180 cm; also El Greco, The Annunciation, The Resurrection and The Crucifixion, 1597–1600, and Pentecost, around 1600, for the altarpiece of the college of Doña María de Aragón, Madrid; and Fray Juan Bautista Maíno, The Adoration of the Magi, 1612–1614, oil on canvas, all in Room 9B.

El Greco painted this for his own family's grave. It's thought to be the last picture he made before he died. And then, a few years after his death, the nuns of the convent changed their minds.

The canvas was to hang above the family burial place in Santo Domingo el Antiguo, in Toledo, the same convent where his Spanish career began. But the nuns demanded that the remains be removed, and in 1618 the agreement was cancelled. The painting stayed in the church for centuries, until the Spanish state bought it for the Prado in 1954.

Look at the light. It pours from the baby himself, and washes over the Virgin, Joseph and three shepherds, and up to the angels, who carry a banner: Gloria in excelsis Deo. Even an ox kneels to look.

Around you hang four canvases from an earlier altarpiece, painted around 1600 for a college church in Madrid. In The Annunciation, a burning bush stands for Mary's purity, and angels make music. In The Crucifixion, probably made for the top of that altarpiece, three angels catch the blood of Christ. In The Resurrection, the soldiers reel in confusion, all except one, who sleeps through it.

Now play a game of faces. In Pentecost, flames settle on the Virgin and the apostles. The bald, bearded apostle on the right, looking straight out at you, has been identified as El Greco himself, or perhaps his friend Antonio de Covarrubias.

Then find the bright Adoration of the Magi by Juan Bautista Maíno. It was painted in exactly the same years, also for Toledo, by a younger artist who became a Dominican friar while working on it. His colours are clear and bright. The young Black king, Balthasar, wears a red cap with feathers. And the young man on the left, pointing at the Child, may be Maíno's own self-portrait.

Perhaps two painters, one old, one young, both looking out at you from the same room.

### 029 · Room 9 · Ribera — Isaac and Jacob, and The Martyrdom of Saint Philip
*Room 9 · 354 words*

**Where:** In Room 9, the Ribera room. Find the very long, low canvas of a blind old man in bed reaching out to a young man, then the huge square canvas of a near-naked saint being hauled up on a beam against a wide blue sky.

**What:** Jusepe de Ribera, Isaac and Jacob, 1637, oil on canvas, 110 × 291.5 cm; and The Martyrdom of Saint Philip, 1639, oil on canvas, 234 × 234 cm, signed "Jusepe de Ribera español / 1639".

Jusepe de Ribera was born near Valencia and died in Naples, where people called him Lo Spagnoletto, the little Spaniard. He never let anyone forget it. On the big square martyrdom in this room, look low on the right. He signed himself Jusepe de Ribera, español.

Start with the long, low painting. It's a story from Genesis, and it's a trick. Old Isaac is blind and close to death, and he means to bless his elder son, Esau. But his wife Rebecca prefers the younger twin, Jacob. She has dressed Jacob in his brother's clothes and covered his arm with a skin, to feel like Esau's hairy arm.

Now look at Rebecca. With one hand she pushes Jacob towards his father, and she looks straight out at you. The museum calls us her silent accomplices. Far away on the left, through an opening, you can see Esau coming home from the hunt, with no idea what's happening. On the table at the right sits the dish of game Isaac asked for. For a blind man, everything here is about touch.

Now turn to the martyrdom, two years later. For centuries it was thought to show Saint Bartholomew. In 1953 an American scholar showed that it is Saint Philip, about to be raised on a cross.

Two men haul on a rope to lift the beam, while a third grips the saint's leg. Their effort is enormous, and Philip is resigned. Around them, onlookers watch, some with pity, some with indifference. We look up from below, into a vast, beautiful blue sky. And nothing happens there. The heavens do not open, and there's no chorus of angels. For Ribera, this is something that happens on earth, among ordinary people.

It may well have been commissioned by the viceroy of Naples, probably as a gift for Philip the Fourth. Saint Philip was the patron saint of them both. By 1666 it hung in a main hall of the royal palace in Madrid, where the king received his audiences.

Next door, Room 9A: Velázquez's Surrender of Breda. Look for the forest of tall lances against the sky.

### 030 · Room 9A · Velázquez — The Surrender of Breda
*Room 9A · 331 words*

**Where:** In Room 9A, stand well back from the very large canvas, taller than it is wide, where two commanders meet in the centre and one hands the other a key, with a forest of upright lances against the sky on the right.

**What:** Diego Velázquez, The Surrender of Breda, also called Las Lanzas, around 1635, oil on canvas, 371.5 × 307.3 cm. Painted for the Hall of Realms in the Buen Retiro palace.

In the summer of 1625, after a long siege, the Dutch city of Breda surrendered to the Spanish army. About ten years later, the king wanted the moment on his wall. What Velázquez gave him looks less like a victory than the beginning of a friendship.

This is your first Velázquez, so a word about the man. He was born in Seville in 1599, and in his twenties he came to Madrid to paint the young king, Philip the Fourth. He stayed at court for the rest of his life. The rooms ahead follow him all the way to Las Meninas.

The picture was made for the Hall of Realms, in the king's new Buen Retiro palace on the edge of Madrid. Its walls showed the victories of Philip's reign, painted by the best artists at court, so Velázquez was among rivals. His scene is the handover of the keys. The Dutch governor, Justin of Nassau, offers them to Ambrosio Spinola, the Genoese general who led the Spanish troops.

Now look at the two men in the centre. Nassau bends forward with the key. Spinola, the winner, does not snatch it. He lays a hand on his enemy's shoulder, almost like a friend. The museum says the gesture seems more like the announcement of peace than the end of a war. Writers of the day praised Spinola's generosity to a respected enemy, and Velázquez paints exactly that.

Then look at the lances. On the right, the Spanish pikes stand straight and close against the sky. That's why Spaniards call this painting Las Lanzas. Behind the generals, smoke drifts over a wide, pale land, the only sign of the destruction.

Finally, find the sheet of paper lying on the ground in the lower right corner. It's blank. Velázquez didn't need to write his name. The painting is the signature.

Next door is Room 10A. Look for a long, low still life by Zurbarán: four vessels in a row on a dark ledge.

### 031 · Room 10A · Zurbarán — Still Life with Vessels
*Room 10A · 305 words*

**Where:** In Room 10A, find the long, low painting, less than a metre wide, of four vessels standing in a row on a wooden ledge against a black background.

**What:** Francisco de Zurbarán, Still Life with Vessels, around 1650, oil on canvas, 46 × 84 cm. Bequeathed by Francesc Cambó, 1940.

Four pots on a shelf. No fruit, no flowers, no skull, no clock. And yet this may be the most silent painting in the whole museum.

Francisco de Zurbarán painted it around 1650. He was born in Extremadura in 1598, a year before Velázquez, and like him he ended his days in Madrid. Here he gives a row of household things the kind of attention most painters saved for people.

Read them from left to right, the way he lined them up. First, on a pewter plate, an elaborate goblet, probably gilded silver. Next, a pale clay water jug from Triana, the potters' quarter of Seville. Its clay was so porous that water seeped through and cooled as it evaporated. Then a red vase that probably came from New Spain, today's Mexico. And last, a white Triana jug resting on a second pewter plate.

Most Spanish still lifes of the time carry a warning about time passing. A skull, a clock, flowers about to wilt. Here, the only clock is the light. It pulls each object out of the dark, one by one, and lays a soft shadow on the wooden ledge.

Look closely and you'll notice something odd. The vessels aren't quite upright, and the perspective doesn't quite add up. Zurbarán tipped them slightly so that the light would catch them better. He was painting what the eye enjoys, not what a ruler measures.

The picture came to the Prado in 1940, a gift from the Catalan collector Francesc Cambó. He gave an almost identical version to the art museum in Barcelona.

Stay with it for a moment longer. Four ordinary things, looked at as carefully as faces.

Next door is Room 10. Look for a pale, bare-chested young god crowning a kneeling man, surrounded by grinning, weather-beaten men. That's Velázquez's Feast of Bacchus.

### 032 · Room 10 · Velázquez — The Feast of Bacchus, 'Los Borrachos'
*Room 10 · 331 words*

**Where:** In Room 10, find the wide canvas of a pale, half-naked young god sitting outdoors and crowning a kneeling man, with a group of sunburnt, grinning men crowding in on the right.

**What:** Diego Velázquez, The Feast of Bacchus, known as Los Borrachos, 1628–1629, oil on canvas, 165 × 225 cm. Also in Room 10: Velázquez, Philip IV, 1626–1628, oil on canvas, 57 × 44 cm, a bust in armour.

Hardly anyone in Spain calls this painting by its proper title. For generations it has simply been Los Borrachos, the drunks. Look at the faces on the right and you'll see why.

Velázquez painted it for the king, and he was paid for it in the summer of 1629. He was about thirty, five years into his work for Philip the Fourth. He had just met Rubens, who was visiting Madrid, and he was about to leave for Italy.

In the centre sits Bacchus, the god of wine, pale and half naked, lit like a statue. He is placing a crown on the head of a young man who kneels before him. Behind the god, a nude figure raises a fine crystal glass. These belong to the world of myth. Then the temperature changes. On the right, a beggar and four men in dark brown capes crowd in, with weathered faces and lively eyes. One of them grins straight out at you, bowl in hand.

For a long time this was read as a joke at the expense of the ancient gods. More recent scholars see something kinder. Wine cheers people up, and it can stir the imagination. So look closely at the crown. It isn't made of vine leaves. It's ivy, the plant of poets. The god may be crowning a poet.

Also in this room is the man who paid for all this. It's a small portrait of the young Philip the Fourth in armour, a crimson sash across his chest. A technical study suggests it was once part of a larger canvas. X-rays show how carefully Velázquez built up the king's face.

So here is the new court painter, polishing the royal face on one wall, and on the other, letting a few weathered drinkers steal the show from a god.

Next door is Room 11. Look for a blacksmith's forge, where a young god crowned with laurel has just walked in with bad news. That's Vulcan's Forge.

### 033 · Room 11 · Velázquez — Vulcan's Forge
*Room 11 · 316 words*

**Where:** In Room 11, stand before the large canvas of a dark forge, where a young man in an orange robe with light around his head speaks to a row of half-naked blacksmiths who have stopped work.

**What:** Diego Velázquez, Vulcan's Forge, 1630, oil on canvas, 223 × 290 cm. Painted during his first stay in Italy; sold to Philip IV in 1634 for the Buen Retiro palace.

Some news should never be delivered at work. The young god in orange has just walked into a blacksmith's forge to tell the smith that his wife is cheating on him.

The smith is Vulcan, god of fire. His wife is Venus. And her lover is Mars, the god of war. The story comes from Ovid. Now here's the cruel part. The armour taking shape at this forge belongs to Mars. Vulcan has been hammering away for the very man who has betrayed him.

Velázquez painted this in 1630, on his first journey to Italy. His early biographer Palomino says he made it in Rome. He wanted to show the Italians, and himself, what he could do. So he chose nearly naked bodies, which can hide nothing. He had been studying ancient sculpture and copying Michelangelo in the Sistine Chapel, and here every body carries the feeling.

Watch the news travel. On the left, Apollo glows, a laurel wreath on his head and rays of light around it. Vulcan, closest to him, has frozen mid-task, and the way he stands gives away his lame leg. Then look along the row of smiths. Every one of them has stopped working. Mouths open, arms hanging, they stare at the visitor. Not one hammer is moving.

Now notice the light. This was his first large painting on a ground of lead white, and it's what gives his later pictures their silvery glow. Think back to the Feast of Bacchus next door, only a year or two earlier. The browns have lifted, and the air has cleared.

In 1634 Velázquez sold the painting to the king, for the new Buen Retiro palace in Madrid.

Apollo is the god of truth, after all. He just has terrible timing.

Ahead is the great oval Room 12, the heart of the Velázquez rooms. Look for Las Meninas and the royal family on horseback.

### 034 · Room 12 · Velázquez — Room 12 and the riders
*Room 12 · 261 words*

**Where:** In the great oval Room 12, step into the middle and turn slowly: the huge canvases of riders on horseback are the royal family, with the king's hunting portrait and a small late bust of him nearby.

**What:** Diego Velázquez and workshop, the Hall of Realms riders, around 1635, oil on canvas: Philip IV on Horseback; Elisabeth of France, Queen of Spain, on Horseback; Philip III on Horseback; Queen Margaret of Austria on Horseback; Prince Baltasar Carlos on Horseback, 1634–1635. Also: Gaspar de Guzmán, Count-Duke of Olivares, on Horseback, around 1636; Philip IV in Hunting Dress, 1632–1634; Philip IV, around 1653.

One palace hall once held five of these riders. Around 1635, the king's new Hall of Realms was hung with his family on horseback, and Velázquez was put in charge.

He painted the king and the little prince himself. Philip the Fourth rides in calm profile on a rearing horse, the hills outside Madrid behind him. In the corner lies a sheet of paper, blank, just like the one in Breda. His queen, Elisabeth of France, rides a white horse and looks straight at you. His parents are here too, Philip the Third and Margaret of Austria. On her chest hangs a famous pearl, La Peregrina. In these three, Velázquez's own hand shows most clearly in the horses.

Prince Baltasar Carlos, about six, charges toward you on a round-bellied pony. The picture hung above a door, so the pony is foreshortened for eyes looking up. Behind him, late snow still lies on the Guadarrama peaks.

The Count-Duke of Olivares, the king's all-powerful favourite, wasn't part of that hall. He had his own portrait painted, for himself. He twists in the saddle and stares you down. The king never needed to.

Then the king off duty, in hunting dress with his dog and gun. Look at his cap. It was added later, and the changes still show through the paint. And finally a small bust of Philip in middle age, in black and grey. He disliked watching himself grow old, yet Velázquez made this one of his greatest portraits.

Stay in Room 12. The biggest crowd here is gathered around Las Meninas.

### 035 · Room 12 · Velázquez — Las Meninas
*Room 12 · 345 words*

**Where:** In Room 12, stand well back, ideally facing it straight on, from the enormous canvas, over three metres tall, of a little blonde princess in a pale dress among her attendants, with the painter at a huge easel on the left.

**What:** Diego Velázquez, Las Meninas, 1656, oil on canvas, 320.3 × 279.1 cm.

Everyone in this painting has just noticed someone come in. The princess turns her eyes. The painter pauses, brush raised. Even the man on the steps at the back has stopped. And the people they're looking at are standing more or less where you are.

The date is 1656. The place, according to his early biographer Palomino, is a room in the old royal palace of Madrid, the very room you see. At the centre stands the Infanta Margarita, the king's daughter. Two maids of honour attend her. María Agustina Sarmiento kneels to offer her a little red jug, and Isabel de Velasco bows. These girls are the meninas of the title. Old palace inventories called the picture something else, the family of Philip the Fourth.

On the right are two court dwarfs, Mari Bárbola and Nicolasito Pertusato, who is prodding a large, sleepy mastiff with his foot. Behind them, the veiled Marcela de Ulloa talks with an attendant. And framed in the bright doorway at the back is José Nieto, the palace chamberlain.

Now find the mirror on the back wall. Two soft faces glow in it, Philip the Fourth and Queen Mariana, the Infanta's parents. They're watching the scene, and you're standing in their place. On the left, Velázquez works at a vast canvas that we only see from behind. Is he painting the king and queen? Or this very picture? Nobody knows for certain.

On his chest is the red cross of the Order of Santiago. The story goes that it was added later, once he had been made a knight.

Now step close. The princess's dress falls apart into quick dabs of grey and white. Step back, and it turns to silk, and the whole room fills with air.

Palomino called it the most illustrious of all his works. Not bad for a picture of a little girl who has been asked to stand still.

Leave Room 12 through the wide doorway into the Central Gallery. In Room 27, look for Titian's emperor on horseback, in gleaming armour, his lance lowered.

### 036 · Room 27 · Titian — The Emperor Charles V at Mühlberg
*Room 27 · 351 words*

**Where:** In Room 27 of the Central Gallery, find the huge canvas of a man in gold-trimmed armour on a dark horse with crimson trappings, riding alone with a lance against an evening sky. The two dark canvases of figures in eternal torment and the life-size Leoni bronze of a young king in armour are in the same room.

**What:** Titian, Emperor Charles V at Mühlberg, 1548, oil on canvas, 335 × 283 cm. Also in Room 27: Titian, Sisyphus, 1548–1549, and Tityus, around 1565, oil on canvas; Leone and Pompeo Leoni, Philip II, cast 1551, bronze.

The most famous portrait of the Emperor Charles the Fifth shows no battle at all. No enemy, no army, no dead. Just a man in armour, riding alone towards evening.

The battle was real. On 24 April 1547, at Mühlberg, Charles defeated the Protestant princes of the Schmalkaldic League. The next year Titian came north to Augsburg to paint him. The writer Aretino suggested showing the beaten enemy under the horse's hooves. It was turned down. Most of Charles's own soldiers were Protestants, and he was trying to reconcile the two faiths. So Titian painted a Christian knight, with a lance that recalls Saint George, and the emperors of ancient Rome.

Look at the armour, trimmed with gold, with an image of the Virgin and Child on the breastplate. It was made for him by the armourer Desiderius Helmschmid, and it's still kept in the Royal Armoury here in Madrid. There's even a wheel-lock pistol on the saddle, then a modern weapon.

The painting belonged to Charles's sister, Mary of Hungary. She is the thread that ties this room together. Look at the two dark canvases of figures in eternal torment. The giant Tityus lies chained while an eagle tears at his liver. Sisyphus, a king who defied the gods, staggers under his rock. Mary chose these punished rebels for the great hall of her palace at Binche, hung high between the windows. They were a warning to the German princes who had defied her brother. The other two of the four, Ixion and Tantalus, were lost in the Alcázar fire of 1734. This Tityus is a later version by Titian himself.

The Leoni bronze in this room, by Leone and Pompeo Leoni, is her nephew, the future Philip the Second, aged twenty-four. Mary ordered it too. Look for the eagle's head on the pommel of his sword.

One family's power, in three moods. The calm rider, the threat, and the heir.

Walk one room along the gallery to Room 26. Look for a sleeping young hunter stretched across the lap of Venus, and a little Cupid holding back a dog.

### 037 · Room 26 · Veronese — Venus and Adonis
*Room 26 · 292 words*

**Where:** In Room 26 of the Central Gallery, find the canvas of a young hunter in bright orange asleep across the lap of Venus in the shade of a tree, with a small Cupid on the right holding back a hound.

**What:** Paolo Veronese, Venus and Adonis, around 1580, oil on canvas, 162 × 191 cm. Also in Room 26: Guido Reni, Hippomenes and Atalanta, 1618–1619, oil on canvas.

Adonis is asleep. He has no idea that this is his last afternoon.

The story comes from Ovid. Venus, goddess of love, fell for the young hunter and begged him to keep away from wild boar. Their tusks, she warned him, strike like lightning. He didn't listen. His hounds would track down a boar, and the boar would kill him.

Veronese paints the calm before. Adonis lies across her lap in the shade, and Venus cools him with a little flag-shaped fan. The figures are life-size, close enough to touch.

But look at her face. Her eyes are wide and her lips slightly parted. She has just noticed Cupid, on the right, struggling to hold back one of the hounds. Cupid has dropped his bow and is gripping his quiver between his legs. The hunt is already calling. And she can't stop it.

Now the colour, Venice at its richest. Venus wears a purple-blue silk scattered with flowers in an Ottoman style. Adonis glows in pure orange, made partly with realgar, the only pure orange pigment of the day. It contains arsenic. Beautiful and poisonous at once, rather like the story.

The painting reached Spain thanks to Velázquez himself. He acquired it on his second journey to Italy, and it entered the royal collection. Its partner, a scene of Cephalus and Procris, is now in Strasbourg.

Also in this room, Guido Reni's Hippomenes and Atalanta shows another race against fate. Her suitors died if they lost, and he is about to drop the last golden apple to slow her down.

One more room along the gallery is Room 25. Look for a very long, low canvas of a tiled hall, with Christ kneeling at the right-hand end to wash a disciple's feet.

### 038 · Room 25 · Tintoretto — The Washing of the Feet
*Room 25 · 320 words*

**Where:** In Room 25 of the Central Gallery, find the very long, low canvas, over five metres wide, of a tiled hall full of seated men, with Christ kneeling at a basin at the right-hand end.

**What:** Jacopo Tintoretto, The Washing of the Feet, 1548–1549, oil on canvas, 210 × 533 cm. On permanent loan from Patrimonio Nacional.

This painting was designed to be seen from the side. Stand straight in front of it and it looks oddly lopsided, as if the main event had slid off to one end.

Tintoretto painted it in Venice around 1548, when he was about thirty, for the church of San Marcuola. It hung on the right-hand wall near the altar, so the congregation saw it at an angle. He put the most important figures at the end closest to them.

The scene is from the Gospel of John. Before the Last Supper, Jesus kneels to wash the feet of his disciples, a lesson in humility and service. Peter can hardly bear to let his master do it.

So try it. Walk to the right-hand end and look back along the canvas. The tiled floor rushes away from you, the disciples fall into a long diagonal, and everything leads to an arch beyond a canal, the true vanishing point. From there, the museum says, the painting becomes extraordinarily coherent. The setting is borrowed from a famous stage design, the Tragic Scene, by the architect Serlio. Tintoretto turns the Gospel into theatre.

Look at the disciple being helped off with his stockings, and at the dog in the middle of the floor. And on the right, in another room, the Last Supper is already under way.

Then the painting travelled. It ended up with King Charles the First of England. After his execution his collection was sold off, and in 1654 an agent bought this for the Spanish courtier Luis de Haro. Haro gave it to Philip the Fourth, who hung it in the sacristy of the Escorial. It stayed there until 1936, when it came to the Prado.

Walk back along the gallery, through Room 26 to Room 27, then turn into Room 12 and cross it into Room 14. Look for a pale Christ on the cross against a black background.

### 039 · Room 14 · Velázquez — Christ Crucified
*Room 14 · 299 words*

**Where:** In Room 14, stand before the tall canvas, about two and a half metres high, of Christ alone on the cross against a black background, his head bowed and his hair falling over half his face.

**What:** Diego Velázquez, Christ Crucified, around 1632, oil on canvas, 248 × 169 cm, inventory P001167. Also in Room 14: Velázquez, The Coronation of the Virgin, 1635–1636, oil on canvas.

After the noise of the court, silence. One figure, a cross, and darkness.

Velázquez painted this soon after he came home from Italy, around 1632. It may have been made for the convent of San Plácido in Madrid. Two years earlier, a carved crucifix had been desecrated in the city, and devotion to the crucified Christ was running high.

Look at how much he leaves out. No crowd, no weeping women, no hill of Calvary, no sky. Only darkness behind. Christ hangs by four nails, not three, as Velázquez's father-in-law, the painter Francisco Pacheco, recommended.

The wound in his side tells us he is already dead. And yet he doesn't sag. He seems to hold himself gently on the cross, as if sweetly dreaming rather than just released from agony. The body is smooth and ideal, a little like the work of Guido Reni, whom you met in Room 26.

Now come closer to the details. His hair falls forward and hides half his face, the most human touch in the picture. Above his head, the sign on the cross is written in Hebrew, Greek and Latin. Velázquez made mistakes in the Hebrew and the Greek. Even geniuses copy letters they can't read.

The painting later left the convent, passed through the collection of the royal favourite Manuel Godoy, and came to the Prado in 1829.

Also in this room is a very different Velázquez, The Coronation of the Virgin, with Mary crowned by the Father and the Son beneath the dove of the Holy Spirit. He painted it for Queen Elisabeth's new private chapel in the palace. It was his last religious painting.

Next door is Room 15, with Velázquez's portraits of the court buffoons. Look for a man in black standing alone in an empty grey space.

### 040 · Room 15 · Velázquez — The Buffoons
*Room 15 · 297 words*

**Where:** In Room 15, look for the tall portrait of a man in black alone in an empty grey space, and the smaller canvases of court buffoons seated on the ground, all looking out at you.

**What:** Diego Velázquez: The Buffoon El Primo, formerly called Sebastián de Morra, 1644; The Boy from Vallecas, Francisco Lezcano, 1635–1645; The Buffoon Calabacillas, 1635–1639; Pablo de Valladolid, around 1635. All oil on canvas.

In 1644 the king rode out to war in Aragon. Among those who went with him was a court buffoon known as El Primo. Velázquez went too, and painted his portrait there.

The court called people like him men of pleasure. Jesters, dwarfs, people whose bodies or minds set them apart, kept to amuse the royal family. Velázquez painted them again and again, and he gave them something the court rarely did. He looked at them as people.

El Primo sits on the ground in a red coat, legs straight out, fists resting on his thighs. For a long time this painting was called Sebastián de Morra. Only recently has the sitter been identified as El Primo. What hasn't changed is his gaze. Few portraits by Velázquez connect so directly with whoever stands in front of them.

Francisco Lezcano, the boy from Vallecas, came from the Basque Country and served the little Prince Baltasar Carlos. He sits beneath a crag in the Guadarrama hills, holding what looks like a pack of cards, as if inviting you into the game. The hat under his foot shows how small he was. Juan Calabazas, known as Calabacillas, crouches beside the gourd that gave him his name, his face almost out of focus. It may be Velázquez's most radical portrait.

Then Pablo de Valladolid, all in black, caught in the middle of a speech. There's no floor and no wall, nothing but a faint shadow, and yet he stands in real space. Over two centuries later, Manet called it possibly the most astonishing piece of painting ever made, and based his famous Fifer on it.

Next door is Room 15A. Look for a dim workshop of women spinning wool, with a bright room and a tapestry behind them. That's The Spinners.

### 041 · Room 15A · Velázquez — The Spinners, or the Fable of Arachne
*Room 15A · 346 words*

**Where:** In Room 15A, stand before the wide canvas of women spinning wool in a dim workshop, with a brightly lit room behind them where elegant ladies stand in front of a tapestry. Velázquez's Mars, a seated nude in a helmet, is in the same room.

**What:** Diego Velázquez, The Spinners, or the Fable of Arachne, 1655–1660, oil on canvas, 220 × 289 cm. Also in Room 15A: Velázquez, Mars, around 1638, oil on canvas.

For a long time, people thought this was a factory scene. A royal tapestry workshop, with spinners in front and elegant visitors at the back. Then a clue turned up in an old list of belongings.

In 1664, an inventory of the possessions of a man named Pedro de Arce included "a Fable of Arachne by Velázquez". Critics had already begun to suspect a myth. Now they had the title.

The fable goes like this. Arachne was a mortal weaver, so proud of her skill that she challenged the goddess Pallas Athena to a contest. She wove the love affairs of the gods, among them Jupiter, disguised as a bull, carrying off a princess. That gave the goddess her excuse. She turned the girl into a spider.

Now look into the bright room at the back. The figure in a helmet, raising her arm, is Pallas, arguing with Arachne. Behind them hangs the fatal tapestry. It copies Titian's painting of that very story, which Rubens had also copied here in Madrid. The whole myth is hidden in plain sight, at the back of a working room.

Some read it as Velázquez's case for his own art. Raw wool in front, a finished work of art behind, craft turning into form. It was painted in the same years as Las Meninas, and it's just as layered.

Then the spinning wheel, on the left. The spokes have melted into a blur, and your eye reads it at once as movement. In the eighteenth century the canvas was enlarged with strips on every side, which made the back room seem further away. Today's display hides them.

Also in this room, Velázquez's Mars, the god of war, sits on an unmade bed wearing nothing of his armour but his helmet, the rest dumped at his feet, looking thoroughly glum. After Vulcan's Forge, you may have a guess why.

Go back into Room 15 and straight on into Room 16, Murillo's room. Look for 16 at the doorway, and a tall Virgin in white and blue rising on a crescent moon.

## Floor 1 · Murillo, Rubens & Goya

### 042 · Room 16 · Murillo — The Immaculate Conception of Los Venerables
*Room 16 · 288 words*

**Where:** In Room 16, find the tall canvas, almost three metres high, of the young Virgin in white and blue standing on a crescent moon, hands crossed on her breast, rising through golden light among clouds of angels.

**What:** Bartolomé Esteban Murillo, The Immaculate Conception of Los Venerables, also called the Soult Immaculate, 1660–1665, oil on canvas, 274 × 190 cm.

For almost ninety years, this Virgin hung in the Louvre. Before that she belonged to Marshal Soult, one of Napoleon's generals in Spain. She only came home in 1941.

She was painted by Bartolomé Esteban Murillo, the painter of this room and the next. He was born in Seville in 1617, and he died there too. Velázquez left Seville for the court in Madrid. Murillo stayed at home and painted for his own city.

This canvas was commissioned by a churchman of Seville, Justino de Neve, for the Hospital de los Venerables, a house for priests. During the French occupation it left Seville and ended up in Soult's collection in Paris. Later the Louvre acquired it.

The subject is the Immaculate Conception, the belief that Mary was conceived free of original sin. Spain campaigned hard to have it declared official doctrine. That only happened, across the Catholic world, in the nineteenth century. Murillo painted around two dozen versions, possibly more than any Spanish painter of his time.

Now look at how everything lifts. She stands on the crescent moon in white and blue, hands crossed on her breast, eyes raised. The golden light around her is the sun she is clothed in. Angels tumble through the clouds beneath her feet. There are none of the usual symbols, no tower of David, no sealed fountain. Just a young girl, rising. The painting feels triumphant, as if the Immaculate Conception and the Assumption into heaven had become one picture.

Soult's name has stuck to her. In the museum's own records she's still the Soult Immaculate.

Next door is Room 17. Look for a homely family scene, with a small boy holding a bird out of reach of a little dog.

### 043 · Room 17 · Murillo — The Holy Family with a Little Bird
*Room 17 · 295 words*

**Where:** In Room 17, find the wide canvas of a homely interior: Joseph seated with the young Christ, who holds a little bird up out of reach of a small dog, while Mary pauses at her work to watch.

**What:** Bartolomé Esteban Murillo, The Holy Family with a Little Bird, around 1650, oil on canvas, 144 × 188 cm.

The Son of God has a bird in his hand, and the family dog wants it very badly.

Murillo painted this around 1650. It is the Holy Family, the most sacred household in Christian art. But there's no choir of angels and no golden sky. Only a plain room, a carpenter's bench, a spinning wheel, and a wicker basket.

Look at Joseph first. In many paintings he waits quietly in the background. Here he's at the heart of the scene, steadying the child against his knee. The painting reflects the devotion to Saint Joseph in Murillo's time. The museum describes him here as humble and silent, a symbol of discretion, devotion and generosity. He's the kind of father who joins in.

Then the child, holding the little bird up high, out of reach. And the small dog below, waiting for its chance. It's a moment any family would recognise. That's exactly the point. The everyday and the sacred live together here, quite naturally.

Mary pauses at her work to watch the game. Her glance ties the three of them together.

The idea has a famous ancestor. Murillo borrowed it from an Italian painting by Federico Barocci, known as the Madonna of the Cat. In Murillo's version, the cat has become a dog.

In 1744, after the death of its owner, Cardinal Gaspar de Molina, it was bought by Queen Isabella Farnese, who hung it in her palace of La Granja, outside Segovia. You'll meet her name again in the next room.

No miracle, no vision. Just a family, a pet, and a child who won't share.

### 044 · Room 16B · Van Dyck — Endymion Porter and Anthony van Dyck
*Room 16B · 289 words*

**Where:** In Room 16B, find the double portrait inside a painted oval: a man in shining white satin facing you, and beside him a slighter man in black, turned in profile, both resting a hand on a rock.

**What:** Anthony van Dyck, Endymion Porter and Anthony van Dyck, around 1633, oil on canvas, 119 × 144 cm.

Anthony van Dyck painted kings, queens and courtiers. But this is the only portrait we know in which he himself stands beside another person.

The other man is Endymion Porter, his friend and protector in London. Porter was secretary to the Duke of Buckingham, a diplomat at the English court, and the man who bought paintings for King Charles the First. Van Dyck, a Fleming from Antwerp, became Charles's court painter, and Porter's support may well have helped. Nobody knows why this picture was made. One idea is that it was a thank-you.

Here's a small coincidence. Van Dyck and Velázquez were born in the same year, 1599. Both grew up to paint kings. And both knew exactly where a painter stood in the order of a royal court.

So read this picture like a code of manners. Porter faces you squarely, in shining white satin. That frontal pose is the place of honour. Van Dyck, in black, turns in profile, and he's placed a little lower, so that he doesn't outrank a gentleman of the court. Every rule is respected.

And yet look at their hands. Both rest on the same rock, a symbol of how firm their friendship was. The oval around them draws them in, closer than protocol would normally allow. Look, too, at the light on that satin, and the ease of the two faces.

The painting later belonged to Queen Isabella Farnese, the same queen who bought Murillo's Holy Family next door.

Equal in friendship, careful in rank. Van Dyck manages both on a single canvas.

### 045 · Room 28 · Rubens — The Adoration of the Magi
*Room 28 · 293 words*

**Where:** In Room 28 of the Central Gallery, stand well back from the enormous canvas, almost five metres wide, of a night-time crowd of kings, servants and horses pressing towards the Virgin and the glowing Child on the left.

**What:** Peter Paul Rubens, The Adoration of the Magi, 1609, enlarged and reworked by Rubens about twenty years later, oil on canvas, 349 × 488 cm.

About twenty years after he finished this painting, Rubens found it again, hanging in the royal palace in Madrid. And he picked up his brushes.

This is the first of two Rubens rooms, so a word about him. Peter Paul Rubens was born in Germany in 1577 and made his name in Antwerp, the great city of the Spanish Netherlands. The Spanish kings collected him eagerly. Where Spanish painting tends towards shadow and restraint, Rubens brings energy, abundance and light.

Around 1608, the city of Antwerp ordered this canvas for its town hall, for the room where Spain and the Dutch were to sign a truce. The newborn Christ, receiving gifts from the kings of the earth, stood for peace. Within a few years it had passed to a powerful Spanish courtier, Rodrigo Calderón. When he fell from power, it went to King Philip the Fourth.

Then, in 1628, Rubens came back to Madrid. This was the visit when he met the young Velázquez. He saw his old painting in the palace and couldn't resist. He enlarged it with new canvas along the right and across the top, and reworked it with the freedom of his later years.

Now look at the light. It's night, but the brightness comes from the Child himself and falls on everyone around him. A long diagonal runs down from the upper right and lands on him. The muscular bearers in the foreground recall Michelangelo and Caravaggio. And look for the painter himself, the man in the purple coat behind the horse.

Kings, servants, horses, silk and gold, all pressing towards a baby. Rubens never did anything by halves.

### 046 · Room 29 · Rubens — The Three Graces, The Garden of Love and The Judgement of Paris
*Room 29 · 337 words*

**Where:** In Room 29 of the Central Gallery, start with the tall panel of three nude women embracing under a garland of roses. The wide canvas of elegant couples and flying cupids in a garden, and the very long canvas of three goddesses before a seated shepherd, are in the same room.

**What:** Peter Paul Rubens: The Three Graces, 1630–1635, oil on oak panel, 220.5 × 182 cm; The Garden of Love, 1630–1635, oil on canvas, 199 × 286 cm; The Judgement of Paris, around 1638, oil on canvas, 199 × 381 cm.

In 1626, Rubens lost his first wife. At the end of 1630 he married again, a young woman called Helena Fourment. From then on, as one catalogue entry puts it, he lived in a state of infatuation. This room is full of it.

Begin with The Three Graces, a painting Rubens made for himself. It was still in his house when he died. There's no known sketch for it, because Rubens made sketches for clients and assistants, never for himself. The Graces are Brightness, Joy and Bloom, goddesses of generosity, of giving and giving back. The one on the left may well have Helena's face.

Look at how they touch, hands on shoulders, arms around waists, the soft weight of real bodies. Above them hangs a garland of red and white roses. The museum's own text has a lovely line for it. Thanks is what Rubens is saying to life.

The Garden of Love is the same happiness, shared out among friends. Elegant couples gather in a garden, while chubby cupids, some with bird wings and some with insect wings, push them together. A statue of Venus presides from her grotto. The little boy flying overhead with a torch is Hymen, god of marriage. And the woman in blue bears a striking resemblance to Helena. After the king's death, it was recorded hanging in the very room where Philip the Fourth had died.

The very long canvas is The Judgement of Paris, ordered by the king himself around 1638. Paris must decide which goddess is the most beautiful. He chooses Venus, she promises him Helen of Troy, and the Trojan War follows. The king's brother, who handled the order in Flanders, thought it one of Rubens's best. He also worried, in his letters, that the goddesses were rather too naked. The king seems not to have minded.

### 047 · Room 32 · Goya — The Family of Charles IV
*Room 32 · 354 words*

**Where:** In Room 32, the eight-sided Goya room. Stand well back from the very wide canvas of a royal family in a row, glittering with silks, sashes and jewels, the queen in the middle and a painter half-hidden in shadow at the back left.

**What:** Francisco de Goya y Lucientes, The Family of Carlos IV, 1800, oil on canvas, 280 × 336 cm.

In the spring of 1800, at the palace of Aranjuez, the royal family of Spain sat for Goya one by one. He painted ten small oil studies of them from life, and five still survive. Then he put them all into one picture, fourteen figures in all, counting himself.

This is the first of three Goyas on our route. Here, the court painter at his peak. Upstairs on Floor 2, the young tapestry designer. Down on Floor 0, the deaf old man of the war and the Black Paintings. Born in a village near Zaragoza, he'd been made First Court Painter just months before this portrait.

Look at the centre. It's Queen María Luisa who stands in the middle, not the king. Her pose copies the little Infanta Margarita in Las Meninas, which you saw in Room 12. King Charles the Fourth stands a step forward, heavy with orders, the Golden Fleece among them. On the left, in blue, is Prince Fernando, the future king.

Some historians read the queen's pose as a cruel satire of an ageing woman. The museum sees something else, the triumph of a fertile queen surrounded by her heirs. Goya gives you both at once. Dazzling silk and diamonds, and faces that are utterly real.

Now find the painter. At the back on the left, in the half-light beside a huge canvas, stands Goya himself, his eyes level with the king's. It's the trick Velázquez played in Las Meninas, borrowed by a man who knew that picture well.

One last detail. Next to Fernando, a young woman turns her face away from us. She's probably his future bride, who hadn't yet been chosen. A royal portrait with a blank space for the future.

### 048 · Room 38 · Goya — The Naked Maja and The Clothed Maja
*Room 38 · 334 words*

**Where:** In Room 38, at the far end of the Goya wing. Find the two long, low paintings of the same young woman lying on a green couch with her arms behind her head: naked in one, and in the other dressed in white with a short yellow jacket.

**What:** Francisco de Goya y Lucientes, The Naked Maja, 1795–1800, oil on canvas, 97.3 × 190.6 cm, and The Clothed Maja, 1800–1807, oil on canvas, 94.7 × 188 cm.

In November 1800, an engraver visiting a Madrid palace was shown into a private cabinet hung with paintings of Venus. One was a naked woman by Goya. He wasn't impressed. He wrote that it was "without drawing and dull in the colouring".

The palace belonged to Manuel Godoy, the king's minister known as the Prince of Peace. In that cabinet, Goya's nude hung beside Velázquez's Venus in the Mirror and two paintings then given to Titian. A room of nudes for a very few eyes.

But look at how far Goya moved from those goddesses. Her pose recalls an ancient statue in the royal collection, the Sleeping Ariadne. Then he took away Cupid and every classical symbol, and laid her on a modern couch. She isn't a goddess. She's a woman, and she looks straight at you.

The dressed version came a few years later, and it's painted more quickly. Look at the nude, with its subtler transparencies and soft shifts of tone, and then at the brisk strokes of the yellow jacket.

Nobody could agree what to call them. One inventory says Gypsies, another says Venus. Then a later record called the clothed one "a woman dressed as a maja", and the name stuck. At the end of 1814 the Inquisition denounced both pictures as obscene.

So who was she? Gossip said the Duchess of Alba. Others said Pepita Tudó, Godoy's mistress. The museum says neither claim has any foundation. And the old story that Goya repainted the face to hide her identity? X-rays suggest the body was painted in one go.

Her name is lost. Her gaze isn't.

### 049 · Room 23 · Giambattista Tiepolo — The Immaculate Conception
*Room 23 · 348 words*

**Where:** In Room 23, beside Room 39. Find the tall, narrow altarpiece of the Virgin standing in the clouds, a crescent moon and a serpent at her feet, small angels around her.

**What:** Giovanni Battista Tiepolo, The Immaculate Conception, 1767–1769, oil on canvas, 281 × 155 cm.

This altarpiece was painted for a king, by the most celebrated decorator of the age. And within a few years it was taken down.

In 1767, King Charles the Third founded a new royal church at Aranjuez, dedicated to Saint Pascual. He ordered seven altarpieces from the Venetian Giambattista Tiepolo, who was then working in Madrid. They made up the entire painted decoration of the church. This one hung to the left of the high altar.

Now read it the way the friars would have. The Virgin treads on the serpent, putting right the weakness of Eve. Beneath her is the crescent moon, an ancient emblem of chastity. Around her head, the twelve stars of the woman in the Book of Revelation. Among the angels you'll find a palm, for victory, and a mirror, because she is without stain. And in the distance rises a tower, a sign of purity.

Then look at how it's painted. Quick, open brushwork, warm light, clouds that seem to move. That freedom was exactly the problem. The museum says his painterly freedom, so admired in Venice, did not please the king and his advisers. They preferred the polished, classical style of Anton Raphael Mengs.

Tiepolo died in Madrid in 1770. Soon afterwards his altarpieces were moved into the convent next door, and replaced with new ones by Mengs, Francisco Bayeu and Mariano Salvador Maella.

Now hold on to two of those names. Mengs and Bayeu set the taste at court. And in 1775, a young painter from Aragon, married to Bayeu's sister, started work for the Royal Tapestry Factory. His name was Goya. Tiepolo was the old master on his way out. Goya was the newcomer climbing in.

## Goya's Story · Floors 2 and 0

### 050 · Room 90 · Goya — The Parasol
*Room 90 · 308 words*

**Where:** In Room 90 on Floor 2, past the stairs from Room 85. Find the wide, bright picture of a young woman sitting on the ground with a little dog in her lap, while a young man behind her holds up a green parasol.

**What:** Francisco de Goya y Lucientes, The Parasol, 1777, oil on canvas, 104 × 152 cm, a tapestry cartoon; also The Pottery Vendor, 1778–1779, oil on canvas, in Room 93.

Before the war paintings and the Black Paintings, there was this Goya. Young, ambitious, and painting for the walls of royal palaces.

From 1775 he worked for the Royal Tapestry Factory in Madrid. His job was to paint cartoons. Not jokes, but full-size designs in oil, which the weavers copied thread by thread into wool. These rooms on Floor 2 bring his cartoons together.

This one went to the factory in the summer of 1777. It belonged to a set of ten country scenes for the dining room of the Prince and Princess of Asturias, at the palace of El Pardo. And you've already met that couple. They were the future Charles the Fourth and María Luisa, standing in their jewels in Room 32, more than twenty years later.

The documents show that Goya invented this composition himself. He may have started from a French painting of an old myth, the god Vertumnus courting Pomona. Goya kept the couple and dropped the gods. Instead you get a fashionable young woman and a majo, a young man in the popular dress of Madrid, gallantly keeping off the sun.

Now notice where you seem to be standing. Slightly below them. The tapestry was meant to hang above a window, so Goya painted it to be seen from underneath. The figures make a neat pyramid in the foreground, a lesson he'd learned from Italian painting. And see how the parasol's shade falls softly across her face.

If you have a moment, The Pottery Vendor hangs in Room 93, a few rooms along. Goya wrote proudly to a friend that the king and the royal couple had seen his new pictures, and this was almost certainly one of them.

Now go back past the stairs to Room 85 for the biggest cartoon of all: a harvest scene more than six metres wide.

### 051 · Room 85 · Goya — The Threshing Floor, or Summer
*Room 85 · 333 words*

**Where:** In Room 85 on Floor 2, the room with the staircase. The huge, sunlit harvest scene, more than six metres wide, is Summer. Nearby, the snowy scene of men wrapped in cloaks and a mule loaded with a pig is Winter.

**What:** Francisco de Goya y Lucientes, The Threshing Ground or Summer, 1786, oil on canvas, 277 × 642 cm, and The Snowstorm or Winter, 1786, oil on canvas, 277 × 303.4 cm, both tapestry cartoons.

By 1786 Goya was no longer the beginner. That summer he was named Painter to the King. And for the prince's dining room at El Pardo he produced the largest cartoon of the whole set. This one, more than six metres wide.

The subject is summer. Tradition offered an easy answer: Ceres, goddess of the harvest, crowned with ears of wheat. Goya didn't want a goddess. He gave the season to the people who actually worked it.

Look at them. Harvesters have dropped down beside a heap of freshly cut sheaves, recovering from the heat. It's siesta time, and you can almost feel it. On the right, one man is still working. And on the left, a little group is trying to get one man drunk. The museum takes him for the village fool, from his clothes and the way he stands.

Now turn to Winter, from the same set, hanging in this room. Snow drives across the whole scene. Three peasants trudge home in their cloaks, cold and hungry, with nothing in their hands. Beside them come two better-dressed men, like stewards of some great house. One carries a shotgun. The other leads a mule loaded with an enormous gutted pig.

And watch the peasants' dog. It's as thin and humble as its owners, and it's the first to sense the clash between the two groups.

That's the Goya to keep an eye on. Even in a design for a royal dining room, meant to look pretty over dinner, he slips in hunger and inequality. These cartoons sat in royal storerooms for most of a century, and came to the Prado in 1870.

Now take the stairs in the corner of this room, the ones you came up, and go down two floors, past Floor 1, to Floor 0. The lift by Room 86 goes there too. At the bottom, by the Murillo entrance, follow the sign to Room 71 and look for a marble of two young men side by side.

### 052 · Room 71 · Pupil of Pasiteles — Orestes and Pylades, or The San Ildefonso Group
*Room 71 · 314 words*

**Where:** In Room 71 on Floor 0, near the Murillo entrance. Find the white marble group of two naked young men standing side by side, one resting an arm on the other's shoulder, with a small altar at their feet.

**What:** Pupil of Pasiteles, Orestes and Pylades or The San Ildefonso Group, about 10 BC, white Carrara marble, height 161 cm.

Between Goya the tapestry designer and Goya the witness of war, take a breath with two young men who have stood together for about two thousand years.

They were carved around 10 BC, in the age of the emperor Augustus. The museum gives them to a pupil of the sculptor Pasiteles. Like many Roman sculptors, he borrowed from the Greeks. He took two figures from different centuries of Greek art and blended them so smoothly that you can't see the join.

Their later journey reads like a roll call of great collectors. The Ludovisi family in Rome. Queen Christina of Sweden. Then the Spanish crown, and the palace of La Granja de San Ildefonso, where they belonged to the collection of Queen Isabella Farnese. That palace gave the group its name.

But who are they? The royal inventories called them Castor and Pollux, the heavenly twins. The museum's title calls them Orestes and Pylades, the most loyal pair of friends in Greek legend. Many viewers simply see what's in front of you: friendship, and a brotherly arm around the shoulder.

Now look closely, because there's a confession to make. The original head was replaced in modern times with the head from an ancient bust of Antinous. The arms are modern too, though they keep roughly the old position.

Follow the torch in the right hand. It points down towards a small altar carved with the skull of an ox. Then step back and see the gentle S-curve running through the two bodies. The group was made to be seen from the front, just where you're standing.

The kings who hired Goya didn't only collect paintings. They collected the ancient world.

Now follow the Goya signs to Room 67, near the foot of the stairs by the Murillo entrance. It's the last of the Goya rooms on this floor, and it holds the Black Paintings.

### 053 · Room 67 · Goya — The Black Paintings
*Room 67 · 345 words*

**Where:** In Room 67 on Floor 0, by the Murillo entrance. Start at the narrow upright painting of a wild-eyed giant devouring a body: Saturn. In the same room are the long, dark crowd of witches before a black goat, two men fighting with clubs, and a small dog's head peering over a wide band of colour.

**What:** Francisco de Goya y Lucientes, Saturn, 1820–1823, mixed method on mural transferred to canvas; also Witches' Sabbath, or the Great He-Goat; Duel with Cudgels, or Fight to the Death with Clubs; and The Drowning Dog, all 1820–1823, from the walls of the Quinta del Sordo.

Nobody was meant to see these. Goya painted them on the walls of his own house.

We're meeting the end of his story before its cause. A few rooms on, in Room 64, is the war that changed him. This is where he ended up. A grave illness had left him permanently deaf in 1793. He'd lived through the French invasion and six years of war. Then in 1819 he bought a country house outside Madrid, known as the Quinta del Sordo, the House of the Deaf Man. On its walls, in his seventies, he painted what we now call the Black Paintings.

He worked straight onto the plaster, with oil in the mix. Saturn hung on the ground floor, across from a portrait of a woman, Leocadia Zorrilla. The old god devours his own child. The museum suggests he may stand for a very human feeling: the fear of losing one's power.

Now find the Witches' Sabbath, the long dark frieze. A great he-goat on the left. Before him, a crowd of witches and warlocks with animal faces. At the far right, a young woman sits apart, perhaps waiting to be initiated. When the wall was cut away, more than a metre and forty centimetres of the scene was cut off.

The two men fighting with clubs now seem sunk in the ground up to their knees. But X-rays and old photographs show that originally they stood on a grassy meadow. And the Drowning Dog is only a head, peering over a great empty field of colour at something we can't see.

In 1873 Baron Émile d'Erlanger bought the house and had the murals transferred to canvas. They lost a great deal of paint. He gave them to the state, and they've been here ever since.

Later artists saw the origins of modern art in these walls. An old man, alone, painting with absolute freedom.

### 054 · Room 64 · Goya — The 2nd of May 1808
*Room 64 · 303 words*

**Where:** In Room 64 on Floor 0. Of the two huge canvases of the same size, find the one full of rearing horses and turbaned riders, with Madrid citizens dragging them down: a street fight in daylight.

**What:** Francisco de Goya y Lucientes, The 2nd of May 1808 in Madrid or "The Fight against the Mamelukes", 1814, oil on canvas, 347.5 × 268.5 cm.

During the war, Goya stayed in Madrid and swore an oath of loyalty to Joseph Bonaparte, the king Napoleon had given Spain. Six years later, with the French gone, he was painting the day Madrid rose up against them.

On the 2nd of May 1808, the people of Madrid fought French troops in the streets. In 1814 the Regency Council, which governed Spain while the king was away, commissioned two great canvases from Goya, this one and its partner here in this room. For a long time people believed they were made to be shown in the streets. The museum says that's wrong. The bills for the frames were charged to the king's own household. These were pictures for the palace.

Now let Goya throw you into the middle of it. The riders in turbans are Mamelukes, horsemen of Napoleon's Imperial Guard. A rider is dragged from his saddle. Among the French you'll also find a dragoon and a dead grenadier.

And look at the Spaniards. They wear the clothes of different regions, as if the whole country had come to fight in one Madrid street. There's no general and no hero on a white horse. Just people with knives against cavalry.

The canvas very nearly didn't survive. In 1938, a truck carrying this painting and its partner crashed at Benicarló, on the Mediterranean coast. The left side lost patches of paint, and the first repair simply filled them with red ink. A careful recent restoration brought back Goya's original brightness, filling the losses invisibly with the help of old photographs.

So stand back and take in the whole thing. A furious tangle of bodies, painted for a king's palace, now hanging where anyone can see it.

### 055 · Room 64 · Goya — The 3rd of May 1808
*Room 64 · 316 words*

**Where:** In Room 64 on Floor 0, in front of the night scene: a line of soldiers in grey capes aiming their rifles at a man in a white shirt with his arms flung wide, a big lantern on the ground between them.

**What:** Francisco de Goya y Lucientes, The 3rd of May 1808 in Madrid or "The Executions", 1814, oil on canvas, 347 × 268 cm.

It's raining. It's night, on the edge of Madrid. And a man in a white shirt has thrown his arms wide.

After the uprising of the 2nd of May, French troops shot prisoners in many places around the city. Goya painted this six years later, as the partner to the street fight in this room. For a long time the scene was placed on the hill of Príncipe Pío. The museum points out that the landscape doesn't match. The buildings on the skyline look more like the edge of the city outside the Puerta de la Vega.

Look at the soldiers first. Grenadiers and guard sailors in campaign uniform and grey capes, a single line of rifles. They're turned away from us, so we never really see who they are.

Then look across at the men they're about to kill. They come out from the city in waves. Each one carries a different kind of fear. One hides his face. One clenches his fists. One prays. And in the middle, lit by the lantern on the ground, the man in white faces the rifles with his arms raised.

At their feet, in front of everything, lies an unceremonious pile of the dead.

Think about who this was for. A commission from the Regency, paid for in the end by the king's household, to commemorate a national uprising. And Goya gave them, not a victory, but the moment before a massacre, seen from the side of the victims. A painting about fear, not glory.

It was in the Prado within twenty years. The French writer Théophile Gautier saw it here in 1840. A restoration finished in 2008 brought back its original brightness, so the white shirt burns again against the dark.

## Floor 0 · The Nineteenth Century

### 056 · Room 75 · Gisbert — The Execution of Torrijos and his Companions
*Room 75 · 310 words*

**Where:** In Room 75, the long nineteenth-century gallery on Floor 0. Find the vast canvas, about six metres wide, of a line of condemned men on a grey beach, with soldiers behind them and bodies lying at the very front.

**What:** Antonio Gisbert Pérez, Execution of Torrijos and his Companions on the Beach at Málaga, 1888, oil on canvas, 392.5 × 602.5 cm.

This painting was ordered by a government, as a political statement. And the painter knew exactly whose example to follow.

We're now in Spain's nineteenth century. A century of revolutions and changing regimes, and of huge history paintings that told the nation its own story. Later in these rooms, the light breaks through. But we begin on a beach at Málaga.

José María Torrijos had been Captain General of Valencia. As a liberal, he was exiled in England. Then the governor of Málaga lured him into a trap. Sail from Gibraltar with your men, came the promise, and the rising will succeed. Torrijos set sail with about seventy trusted men. Their boat was intercepted, they were forced ashore, and after a few days of hopeless resistance it was over. On the 11th of December 1831, they were shot on the beach at Málaga, without trial.

More than fifty years later, in 1886, Sagasta's liberal government chose Gisbert, the liberal party's leading painter, to commemorate them. It didn't wait to buy a prize-winner at an exhibition, as usual. It commissioned the picture directly.

Gisbert shows the moment just before the shots. Look along the line. Worry, dejection and rage in some faces. Proud resignation in others. Two men embrace.

Now drop your eyes to the very front. The men already shot lie at your feet, cut off by the frame. A hand. A top hat on the sand. The museum calls it an inevitable reminiscence of Goya, and you've just come from his firing squad.

It became, in the museum's own words, one of the greatest political manifestos in all Spanish painting in defence of human freedom.

Go back towards the end of Room 75 where you came in, and through Room 62B into Room 61B. Look for 61B at the doorway, and a dying queen in a great canopied bed.

### 057 · Room 61B · Rosales — Queen Isabella the Catholic Dictating her Will
*Room 61B · 302 words*

**Where:** In Room 61B on Floor 0. Find the large, dim, hushed scene of a dying queen propped up in a great canopied bed, a scribe writing at her side and courtiers gathered in the shadows.

**What:** Eduardo Rosales Gallinas, Queen Isabella the Catholic dictating her Will, 1864, oil on canvas, 287 × 398 cm.

A queen is dying. And what she says in the next few minutes will help decide who rules Spain.

### 058 · Room 60A · Sorolla — Boys on the Beach
*Room 60A · 311 words*

**Where:** In Room 60A on Floor 0. Find the bright, wide canvas of three naked boys lying in the shallow water at the edge of the sea, seen from close above, with no sky at all.

**What:** Joaquín Sorolla y Bastida, Boys on the Beach, 1909, oil on canvas, 118 × 185 cm.

After firing squads, deathbeds and Goya's dark walls, the Prado lets you out into the sun.

In the summer of 1909, Joaquín Sorolla went home to Valencia for three months, from late June to late September. He spent the season painting by the sea. He signed this canvas with the next year's date, but the museum thinks he most likely painted it that summer.

Notice first what's missing. There's no sky and no horizon. Sorolla brings you right down to the water's edge, so close that the three boys fill the picture. They're not a detail in a seascape. They're the subject.

Now look at the water. What caught Sorolla was the movement of the sea itself, and he turns it into pure painting. Broad strokes of turquoise, blue, violet and mauve slide over each other as the wave runs up the sand.

Then follow the light from front to back. On the nearest boy, the highlights are matte, thick dabs of white paint. On the boy furthest away, half in the sea, they're bright and glassy. The deeper the boys lie in the water, the more they shine. Look at the shadows. They aren't brown or black. They're violet. And the skin runs from white with mauve reflections to a deep reddish bronze.

Sorolla wasn't the first to paint children by the sea. But unlike Mariano Fortuny before him, he cared above all about water in motion. Ten years later Sorolla gave this picture away himself, and in time it came to the Prado.

This collection was never meant for the public. For three hundred years these pictures hung in palaces, chosen by kings and queens for their own walls. The collection grew fast under Charles the Fifth, and the Habsburgs and Bourbons after him kept it growing. The original building next door, by Juan de Villanueva, was designed for natural history.
