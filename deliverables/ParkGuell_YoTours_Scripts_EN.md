# Yo Tours · Park Güell (Barcelona) · audio guide scripts (EN) · 7 Nov 2026 edition

## The gate

### 001 · Welcome to Park Güell
*Anywhere just inside your gate (heard before you reach the start) · 360 words*

**Where:** Play it anywhere just inside the gate you came in by, out of the way of the ticket check; it needs no view. The loop then starts on the entrance esplanade inside the Carrer d'Olot gate, between the two gatehouses.

**What:** Park Güell, Eusebi Güell's private garden-city estate on the Carmel hill, by Antoni Gaudí, works begun 1900, a public park since 1926 (aj1 Q3; op4 Q2; F1.9, F1.15, F3.1); the route in the official A–I order with the viaducts first (MAP; PLAN_V2 §1); practical lines (ACCREG 3.23, 2.11; FAQ; RESTRICT; MOB26; COMP); the three lengths (PLAN_V2 §3, Lead's decision 1); the three visitor gates (NODE268, ACCESS; RA M2 text).

Park Güell was never meant to be a park. The city's history museum calls it "a residential condominium for sixty well-heeled families in the purest British style". Eusebi Güell wanted to recreate the British residential parks.

Its makers were Güell, one of the great industrialists of his city, and Antoni Gaudí, his architect for over twenty years by then. Work began in 1900. This is how their private estate became everyone's park.

We begin at the gate and its two gatehouses, one of them the caretaker's. The longer tours then climb to the roads and bridges Gaudí built first. Then come the great stairway and its dragon, the hall of columns, the Austria Gardens, the Nature Square with its bench and its view, and the Portico of the Washerwoman. We end past Güell's own house, at the main gate on Carrer d'Olot, the way out.

Please listen on headphones: the park doesn't allow speakers. Before the great stairway there's a second ticket check. Beyond it the route runs one way, and you can't go back. Once you leave the park, you can't come back in. The paths are steep, with many steps, and the park publishes routes for people with reduced mobility. Everything is already on your phone, so you won't need a signal.

The full tour takes a little over two hours, the essential about an hour and a quarter, the highlights about three quarters of an hour, walking and a short wait included. Play the stops in order. The numbers on your phone may jump, but every stop tells you where to go next.

From the Marianao entrance, on the Carrer d'Olot side, walk along the inside of the wall to the two gatehouses by the main gate. From the Carretera del Carmel, or the Sant Josep de la Muntanya escalators, follow the signs down toward the main gate. Stay inside the park, and don't pass the second ticket check.

Start on the entrance esplanade, just inside the main gate on Carrer d'Olot, between the two little gatehouses with their tiled roofs. Turn to face the gate and the wall: The entrance esplanade · Park with a k.

### 002 · The entrance esplanade — Park with a k
*The entrance esplanade · inside the Carrer d'Olot gate, facing the wall and its medallions · 257 words*

**Where:** On the entrance esplanade just inside the main gate on Carrer d'Olot, between the two gatehouses, facing the iron gates and the stone wall with its tiled top and name medallions.

**What:** The boundary wall of rustic stone with ceramic tiling and "Park Güell" medallions (op6, F5.2); the estate as "a private estate, closed in by a wall, with a porter's service" (es8, E20); the English "Park" (op4, F1.12, Q2); the garden city begun in 1900 (aj3, F1.13); the gardens named after the Font del Carbó, and the tentative start of building around that spring, below Casa Larrard, in 1875 (s3 p. 99 "una tímida urbanització", op24, E111; FA M1: never "the first building on this hillside").

This wall was built to close an estate off from the city. The city's history museum, in its guide to Gaudí's Barcelona, describes the estate in Catalan as "a private estate, closed in by a wall, with a porter's service". The two gatehouses of the porter's lodge stand either side of the gate.

Look along the wall. The park describes it as "rustic stone topped with ceramic tiling and medallions bearing the name of Park Güell". Those medallions are the first clue to what Güell had in mind.

Find one of them and read it. Park, with a k. Güell spelt it in English on purpose: an English word for an English idea, set into a Catalan wall.

The same museum's dossier for schools puts the idea in a single line. The Park "began to be built in 1900 as a garden city". A garden city on a hill above Barcelona, with a wall round it and a porter at the door.

The hill wasn't empty of history, even then. Next to this gate, outside the park, are gardens named after an old spring, the Font del Carbó. Around that spring, below the old farmhouse you'll pass at the end, a modest start was made at building in 1875, a quarter of a century before Güell.

Now walk over to the gates themselves, the iron gates in the middle of the wall. Stand to one side, out of the way of people leaving, and look at their ironwork: The Carrer d'Olot gate · A glove shop window.

### 003 · The Carrer d'Olot gate — A glove shop window
*The Carrer d'Olot gate · just inside, to one side of the palm-leaf iron gates · 322 words*

**Where:** Just inside the main gate on Carrer d'Olot, standing to one side of the palm-leaf iron gates, out of the way of people leaving.

**What:** The 1878 glove-shop display case for Esteve Comella (op3, F1.8); Eusebi Güell's family, studies, textile and cement firms, politics and microbiology book (s1, F1.1–F1.6); the Palau Güell (1886) and the park (1900) (op3, F1.9); "a real story of friendship" (op3, Q1); the palm-leaf gates from Casa Vicens (op6, F5.3; no date, F15.38).

It began with a shop window. At the Paris Exhibition of 1878, Eusebi Güell saw a display case Gaudí had made for a glove shop, Esteve Comella's. The park's history says the partnership "began when Güell saw a window display that Gaudí had planned" for that glove shop.

Güell was born in Barcelona, the son of Joan Güell i Ferrer. He studied there, in France and in England. With Ferran Alsina he built up the cloth works that became the Colònia Güell, his industrial village. Then came cement. His Asland company opened some of the first Portland cement works in Catalonia. Güell's cement helped build the twentieth-century city.

He was a city councillor, then a senator, and president of the Centre Català. And this textile baron also wrote a book on microbiology, in French, published in Paris.

In 1886 Güell asked Gaudí to build his new house in the old city, the Palau Güell. In 1900 he gave him this hill to plan. The same history calls the bond between them "not simply that of an artist and his patron, but a real story of friendship".

Now look at the gates beside you. Their iron is shaped like palm leaves, and they weren't made for the park at all. The park's page on this entrance says they "do not form part of the original plan, but came from Casa Vicens". That was a house Gaudí had built in the city years earlier.

Find a single leaf in the ironwork, and follow it from its stem out to its tips.

A partnership that began in a shop window, and a gate that began somewhere else. Even the way in is a transplant.

Now turn back to face the stairway. Find a spot between the two gatehouses where you can see both roofs: the tall tower with its cross on your left, the other gatehouse on your right: The entrance esplanade · Factory seconds.

### 004 · The entrance esplanade — Factory seconds
*The entrance esplanade · between the two pavilions, both roofs in view · 298 words*

**Where:** On the entrance esplanade between the two gatehouses, where both roofs can be seen: the tall tower with its cross on the left as you face the stairway, the other gatehouse on the right.

**What:** The two entrance pavilions, the Porter's Lodge pair (op6, F5.4–5.5): the office "with a waiting room and telephone booth", and the caretaker's home, today MUHBA's Casa del Guarda (E1); among the first things built (F5.7 HEDGE); trencadís from factory seconds (s6 p. 26, F5.13) in more than ten whites (s6 p. 26, E100); trencadís history (s24, F15.20); the 1904 plans without the mushroom ventilators (Andruet, G41, G61 HEDGE; E103: no house named); the Hansel and Gretel legend (F5.17 LEGEND, G58, G59 HEDGE; s16 re-read, E60).

The most photographed roofs in the park are made of rejects. A study written for their restoration found, in Spanish, that the broken tile came "mostly from surplus and defective stock from ceramic factories". It was cheap, and it was easy to fit to curves.

Look at the white on the roofs. It isn't one white. The same study counted more than ten different shades of it, side by side.

Gaudí didn't invent trencadís, this broken-tile mosaic. Catalans and Valencians had dressed their walls in tile since the Middle Ages. Gaudí first used broken tile in the 1880s, and he made it his signature.

The park calls the pair the Porter's Lodge. One was the office, "with a waiting room and telephone booth". The other was the caretaker's home, today a small branch of the city's history museum, the Casa del Guarda. They were among the first things built on the estate.

Now look for the mushrooms on the roofs. The plans that Güell and Gaudí signed in 1904 don't show the mushroom-shaped ventilators at all. Even the mushrooms seem to have been an afterthought.

Tour guides love to say these are the gingerbread house from Hansel and Gretel. A music writer, Marta Porter, notes that the opera reached Barcelona's opera house, the Liceu, in 1901. It came in a Catalan version co-written by the poet Joan Maragall, a friend of both men. She even found an old news item linking it to the design of this lodge. Did Gaudí have the opera in mind? Nobody can show it.

Go over to the gatehouse with the tall tower, on your left as you face the stairway. Stand where you can see its tower and its door, out of the way: The tower pavilion · Telephones and a tower.

### 005 · The tower pavilion — Telephones and a tower
*The tower pavilion · outside, its tower and door in view · 220 words*

**Where:** Outside the gatehouse with the tall tower, on the left as you face the stairway, with the tower and its door in view, clear of the doorway and of people passing.

**What:** The office pavilion of the Porter's Lodge pair: a visitors' room with telephones in MUHBA's schools story (aj3, E88: fictional register, disclosed); two modest rooms, reception below and office above (s6 p. 20, E96); the tower, about 13 m, a hyperbolic paraboloid of two layers of thin brick tied in a double helix (s6 pp. 20, 22, E97); Gaudí's four-armed cross (s15, F5.9); the original porcelain-clad cross destroyed in 1936, a post-war cross until 1996, today's from photographs of the original (s6 pp. 27–28, E98, C20); the bullseye panes (s6 p. 26, E104).

A gated estate needed a front desk. In the story the city's history museum tells schoolchildren in Catalan, this house held "a room for visitors, with telephones and armchairs". Its restorers found two rooms of modest size: a reception room downstairs, and an office upstairs.

This was the estate's business end, built for work rather than for living, and its story is best told from out here.

Look up the tower. It stands about thirteen metres high. It's a hyperbolic paraboloid that narrows as it rises, built of two layers of thin brick, wound with twisted metal straps in a double spiral.

On top is Gaudí's cross, with arms pointing four ways. It's the third cross on this tower. The original was clad in pieces of porcelain, plates and cups and bowls, and it was destroyed in 1936, at the start of the Civil War. A cross put up after the war stood until 1996. The one you see was made then, from photographs of the original.

Look, too, at the windows, for the round bullseye panes of moulded, blown and tinted glass. They were made again when this pavilion was restored in the 1990s.

Now cross the esplanade to the other gatehouse, the one without the tower, and stand outside its door: Casa del Guarda · A modest dwelling.

### 006 · Casa del Guarda — A modest dwelling
*Casa del Guarda · outside its door, the gatehouse without the tower · 282 words*

**Where:** Outside the door of the Casa del Guarda, the other gatehouse, the one without the tower, on the right as you face the stairway; stand clear of the doorway and of any queue.

**What:** The Casa del Guarda, the caretaker's house, "a modest dwelling" (aj4, F5.6); an elliptical plan with two half-circles (s6 p. 23, F5.11, E13); La Pedrera's "mushroom-shaped dome", in the body only and attributed (s15; E103 HEDGE: s6 p. 23 puts its "sombrero en forma de seta" on the office pavilion; never used to find the house, which is found as the gatehouse without the tower, lead's decision 7 Nov); ground-floor walls 50 cm of stone from the park's old quarries (s6 p. 21, E99); private homes from the 1920s to the 1980s (s6, F5.14); the other pavilion a builders' hut (s6 p. 21, E102); restored at the end of the 1990s (F5.15); a MUHBA branch since 2010 (E1, G-S34); the exhibition and its three floors (aj4, F5.16, DATED).

Of everything Gaudí built on this hill, this is the one house made for a working man. The city's history museum calls it "a modest dwelling". It was to be the home of the estate's caretaker, right beside the gate he kept.

Look at its shape. The plan is an oval: an ellipse, closed at each end by a half-circle. On its roof, the La Pedrera foundation sees a "mushroom-shaped dome".

The house is built of the hill itself. Its ground-floor walls are half a metre thick, the restorers found, of stone from the park's old quarries.

The estate it guarded never filled up. From the 1920s to the 1980s both gatehouses were private homes, and they "suffered many changes and mutilations", in the Spanish words of the restorers' study. Later the other one even served as a builders' hut. This house was restored at the end of the 1990s, and since 2010 the city's history museum has run it.

Inside, the museum's exhibition is called "Güell, Gaudí and Barcelona, the expression of an urban ideal". There's one theme on each floor: the house, the park and the city.

If you're on the full tour and can go inside, there are three short Extras in the house, one for each floor. Start on the ground floor with: Casa del Guarda · Extra · The caretaker's rooms. Otherwise, the next main stop is up among the roads Gaudí built first. Walk along the inside of the wall, past this house, to the Marianao entrance, without going out. From just inside it, steps and ramps climb to the Low Viaduct, the Pont de Baix. Stop on it: The Low Viaduct · Roads before houses.

## Inside the Casa del Guarda

### 007 · Casa del Guarda · Extra · The caretaker's rooms

**Where:** Inside the Casa del Guarda, on the ground floor; the first paragraph can be heard as you go in, and the rest standing in the rooms, clear of the stairs.

**What:** The caretaker's ground floor: "a small space, designed to be used by a humble family" (aj4, E19); vestibule, kitchen, pantry and living room (s6 p. 20, E12); two parabolic arches in the load-bearing walls, with no catenary gloss (s6 p. 23, E13; FA S8); lime plaster with natural pigments, each room's original colour recovered in the 1996–99 restoration (s6 p. 25, E16, F5.15); the floor's theme, the house, Gaudí's "architectural uniqueness" (es4, E5; DATED).

Small as it is, this house was built with great care. The city's history museum describes it as "a small space, designed to be used by a humble family", and says it shows "great richness and compositional complexity".

As built, this floor held a vestibule, a kitchen, a pantry and a living room. Everyday rooms, for everyday life.

Look at the openings in the thick walls between the rooms. The restorers' study describes them as two parabolic arches.

Then look at the colour of the walls. They are lime plaster, painted with natural pigments. When the house was restored in the late 1990s, the restorers studied the layers of plaster and paint, found each room's original colour, and brought it back.

The museum's theme for this floor is the house itself, and what it shows of Gaudí's architectural uniqueness.

### 008 · Casa del Guarda · Extra · Four bedrooms and a promoter
*Casa del Guarda · inside, on the first floor (Extra) · 178 words*

**Where:** Inside the Casa del Guarda, on the first floor, standing clear of the stairs.

**What:** The first floor as built: four bedrooms and an attic space (s6 p. 20, E12); steel-and-ceramic beams instead of arches (s6 p. 23, E13); the floor's theme, the park (es1, E7; es4; DATED); the curator Mireia Freixa (with Mar Leniz, G80) and her line on the promoter (es1, E3); the museum's visits on Gaudí and Güell, the park and the city (es10, E22).

A house designed for a humble family needed bedrooms, and up here they had a whole floor of them. This level held four bedrooms, with an attic space above them.

Look at what holds the floor up. Downstairs Gaudí opened the walls with arches. Up here he used beams of steel and ceramic instead.

The exhibition's theme on this floor is the park: how the estate began, and how, in 1926, it became a park for the whole city. Why the estate failed is told at the foot of the great stairway.

The historian who curated this exhibition, Mireia Freixa, put Güell's part in one line when the house reopened. As an English press note reported her words, "behind every great artist is an ambitious promoter".

It's a fair point in this house. The museum's own visits here stress two relationships: the one between Gaudí and Güell, and the one between the park and the city below.

In the middle of this floor, a spiral stair winds up to the lookout. Its secret is in its core. Inside the round brick wall of the stair runs a second brick cylinder. It's the flue that carries the smoke out from the chimneys, and together with the outer wall it holds up the ceramic steps.

## The viaducts

### 010 · The Low Viaduct — Roads before houses
*The Low Viaduct (Pont de Baix) · on the viaduct, out of the way · 319 words*

**Where:** On the Low Viaduct, the Pont de Baix, at the top of the steps and ramps from the Marianao entrance, standing to one side where the plant pots along its railing can be seen.

**What:** The roads first, from 1900 (s6 p. 20, F3.3); a 3 km network (op11, F10.1); "all the viaducts, save details" by January 1903 (Andruet, G43); three five-metre viaducts from the Carrer d'Olot side, named on the first postcards (op29, F10.2); sloping columns and vaults of unhewn site stone (op29, F10.3; op11); the hill's stone sold (s3, F2.15); road, shelter and buttress (op12, F10.5); a cistern for each viaduct (op13, F8.6); the pots as railing (op13, F10.8); the 2019–20 recovery of the Low Viaduct's geometry and its railings' three levels of holes (op28, F10.9); the lean that carries the load (version-neutral, G16); the hope that plants would cover the structure (es29, E71).

Before any house, Gaudí built the roads. A study of the gatehouses says, in Spanish, that "the work began in 1900 with the building of the roads". The park says he designed three kilometres of road to link the plots. By January 1903, all the viaducts were finished, save details.

You're standing on the first of them. Gaudí planned three, each five metres wide, for carriages climbing from the Carrer d'Olot side to the top of the estate. Their names "already appeared on the first postcards of the park": the Pont de Baix, the Pont del Mig and the Pont de Dalt. Bottom bridge, middle bridge, top bridge.

They are made of the hill they climb. They stand on "sloping columns and vaults made from unhewn stones taken from the site itself". The estate even sold the hill's stone for masonry, by the cubic metre.

Each bridge did several jobs at once. A road on top. Shelter underneath, from bad weather. And, as the park puts it, "buttresses and retaining walls for the mountain". Each one also had its own cistern, filled with rainwater that was caught and filtered on the way.

Now look along the top. Small columns carry plant pots, and the pots make the railing. Over the years this bridge lost the shape Gaudí gave it. In a restoration finished in 2020, old documents showed the outer railings' original height, and they were rebuilt with three levels of openings. Find the three rows.

Its stone columns lean, too, and the lean carries the load.

The city's history museum says the roads were built "in the hope that in time the vegetation would cover most of its structure". These bridges were meant to disappear under the plants.

Now find a place beside the viaduct where you can see its leaning columns from the side, and keep to the path: The Low Viaduct · Why the columns lean.

### 011 · The Low Viaduct — Why the columns lean
*The Low Viaduct · beside it, its leaning columns seen from the side · 215 words*

**Where:** Beside the Low Viaduct on an open path, where its two rows of leaning stone columns can be seen from the side; keep to the path.

**What:** The Low Viaduct's curve on two rows of inclined columns forming a parabola (op13, F10.8); Kopsida's 2011 UPC master's thesis on this viaduct: the viaduct about 50 m long, columns up to 6 m, modelled from the Ajuntament's drawings (not measured by her), the lean's structural meaning, the thrust line inside the vault (G15, G16; FA M2, S1); the same solution later in the crypt of the Colònia Güell (s15, F10.10).

This bridge leans on purpose. Its official description reads like a diagram: it "draws a curved line and is supported by two rows of inclined columns forming a parabola that includes the central vault".

In 2011 an engineering student at Barcelona's technical university, Antigoni Kopsida, made this viaduct the subject of her master's thesis: a bridge about fifty metres long, on stone columns up to six metres high. She wrote: "The inclination of the columns apart from impressing aesthetically has also a structural meaning."

Here is what that means. The columns lean because the weight leans. The push of the road and its vaults doesn't run straight down. So each column is tilted along the path the load wants to take. In her analysis, the thrust stays inside the vault, and the vault is stable.

Follow one column with your eyes, from its foot up into the vault, and see how the slope carries on into the curve above it.

Gaudí kept the idea. The La Pedrera foundation notes that he later used the same inclined columns in the crypt of the Colònia Güell, the church of Güell's industrial village.

Step into the shelter under the viaduct, where the stone looks like the mouth of a cave: The Low Viaduct · Caves dug for stone.

### 012 · The Low Viaduct — Caves dug for stone
*The Low Viaduct · in the shelter under the viaduct · 280 words*

**Where:** In the covered shelter under the Low Viaduct, among the rough stone columns, standing to one side of anyone passing.

**What:** The viaducts "simulate natural caves" (op12); the architects' visit of 4 Jan 1903, Gaudí "en representació del Sr. Güell", Salvador Sellés's memoir "prèviament supervisada per Gaudí" (s3 pp. 102, 104, G50); Bassegoda's Catalan retelling of Sellés on the caves, torchlight, the caves artificial and dug for stone (s3 p. 102, F2.16, G51; FA M2, S2, S3); the fossil cave of 1900 and its Pleistocene animals, now in the natural sciences museum (s3 pp. 100–102; es16, E45); destitute families in the caves after the war (s3 p. 103, E43).

This shelter is a fake cave. In the park's words, the viaducts "simulate natural caves". But the hill had caves of its own, and they weren't natural either.

On the fourth of January 1903, Gaudí led the Catalan architects' association round the works, "on Mr Güell's behalf", as the Gaudí scholar Joan Bassegoda told it, in Catalan. At the top, one of them, Salvador Sellés, read out a description of the works, "checked beforehand by Gaudí".

In Bassegoda's retelling of Sellés's account, the caves were "shady openings in the ground, as if dug as burrows by some creature of troglodyte times". The party went in by torchlight. Bassegoda added that they were artificial, dug out for building stone.

In 1900, clearing a cave to level a street, diggers found fossils. Among them were the bones of Ice Age rhinoceros, elephants and deer, now in Barcelona's natural sciences museum.

After the Civil War, Bassegoda wrote, some destitute families made their homes in the caves.

Look at the rough stone around you, much as it came from the hill.

Gaudí dressed his bridges as caves, on a hill already hollowed out for stone.

Otherwise, go back down the steps and ramps to the esplanade, to the foot of the great stairway and the second ticket check. ### 013 · The Middle Viaduct · Extra · The carob bridge
*The Middle Viaduct (Pont del Mig) · where it comes into view (Extra; if the path is open) · 202 words*

**Where:** A little higher up the open path from the Low Viaduct, where the Middle Viaduct, the Pont del Mig, comes into view with the carob trunk; do not go on past the works closure.

**What:** The Middle Viaduct, "the carob bridge" (op12, F10.7; aj3 caption, V); the farm before the park: vines, olives and orchards, abandoned for poor access, low yields and phylloxera (es16, E48); the carob a survivor of the farm "perhaps" only (RA L4c: MUHBA's vegetation line lives in 031; op12 "natural growth… respected", FA N6: never "bent round"); DATED: the path higher up, to the High Viaduct, closed for works (op24, RA S2; its only home now, RA L4b).

Here is a road that made room for a tree. The park calls the Middle Viaduct, the Pont del Mig, "the carob bridge, where the natural growth of this interesting tree is respected". A photo caption in a dossier for schools from the city's history museum puts it, in Catalan, as "the middle viaduct with the carob trunk".

Find the trunk. Rather than lose the tree, the builders let it keep growing.

Before the park, this hill was farmland: vineyards, olive groves and fruit orchards. The park says the farms were given up: hard to reach, poor in yield, and, for the vines, struck by the plague of phylloxera.

So this carob is a survivor, perhaps, of the farm that came before.

## The Dragon Stairway

### 014 · The foot of the stairway — The healthy quarter
*The foot of the stairway · in the queue for the second control (standing; or just before the check if there is no queue) · 326 words*

Six weeks later, on an early June evening, Gaudí was hit by a tram at the corner of Bailèn and the Gran Via, in the city below. He was taken to the Hospital de la Santa Creu. He died there three days later.

He had moved out of his house here the year before, into his workshop at the Sagrada Família. So he knew this hill as a public park for only a few weeks, and by then he no longer lived on it.

2026 was named Gaudí Year, for the hundredth anniversary of his death.

A failed housing estate that became a triumph of a park. That's the shortest honest history of this hill. Sixty plots, and only two houses ever built. A market hall for an estate that never filled. A square on its roof, for crowds that came only later.

The man who paid for it stayed to the end, in the old farmhouse on the way down, Casa Larrard, until 1918. Then the city bought the park from his heirs and opened it to everyone in April 1926. That June, Gaudí died.

What was left behind is what you've walked through today. The city's history museum sums it up: "Gaudí's idea was a perfect symbiosis between urban planning, architecture and nature." Rain led down through columns, a square made into a roof, a hillside held up by a wave of stone.

And it was never one man's work. The colour on the bench and on the ceiling of the hall is largely Josep Maria Jujol's. Francesc Berenguer, Gaudí's right hand, drew the house where Gaudí lived. The builders were an uncle and his nephew. A caretaker kept the gate. And the lawyer Martí Trias bought the very first plot.

Remember the name on the wall, Park, with a k. Güell spelt it in English, for a British-style estate for the few. It became Barcelona's park instead, for everyone.
