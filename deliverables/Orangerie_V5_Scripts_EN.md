## Arrival

### 001 · An orangery for a garden

> Outside the museum, at its west end, the Concorde end of the Tuileries terrace, near the entrance.

Before it held a single painting, this building was a winter coat for trees. In 1852, Napoleon the Third had it built here in the Tuileries garden, to shelter the garden's orange trees and other citrus through the cold months. That's what an orangery is. And the name stuck.

It was designed for the job. The long side facing the Seine is the south side, and it's glass. The garden side, to the north, is almost blind. Plants need light, and they need shelter, and the building gave them both.

Hold on to that idea of light, because it runs through this whole visit. About seventy years after the orange trees, the building was given a very different tenant. Claude Monet's Water Lilies, eight huge compositions wrapped around two oval rooms and lit from above. The rooms were shaped to his wishes, for paintings of water and sky.

Here's the plan. First the Water Lilies, on this floor. Take your time there, because that's the heart of the visit. Then the lower level, where a collection built by a Paris art dealer, and reshaped by his widow, is waiting, with Renoir, Cézanne, Matisse, Picasso, Modigliani and more. And if you have time at the end, a second look at the lilies.

For now, go in through the entrance at this end. Stop in the entrance hall, just before the first oval room. The story begins there.

### 002 · A gift for the Armistice

> In the entrance hall, just before you step into the first oval room of the Water Lilies.

The guns had been silent for just one day. On the twelfth of November, 1918, the day after the Armistice, an old painter sat down and wrote to Georges Clemenceau.

"I am on the verge of finishing two decorative panels," Claude Monet wrote, "which I want to sign on Victory Day, and I am writing to ask you to offer them to the State on my behalf."

Two panels. That was the whole gift. It didn't stay that size for long. Clemenceau came out to Giverny, and he persuaded Monet to widen it, from two panels to the whole set of large panels he'd been working on for years.

Then the gift needed a home. A first plan, for a pavilion in the garden of what is now the Musée Rodin, was dropped. The choice fell instead on this old orangery, the winter house for trees. In April 1922 the deed of gift was signed. Eight compositions, on twenty-two panels.

The architect Camille Lefèvre rebuilt the inside to Monet's wishes, as two oval rooms lit from above. And Monet had known for years what he wanted from a room like that. Back in 1909, the critic Roger Marx had published his dream of it. What Monet wanted, Marx reported, was "the illusion of an endless whole, of a wave without horizon and without shore."

The rooms opened in May 1927. Between the deed and that day, something happened at Giverny that you should hear sitting down, in the second room. For now, just notice how far a promise can travel. The museum calls the finished gift a monument to peace.

Two panels to sign on Victory Day. Step through and see what they became. Stand near the middle of the first oval room, and start its track there.

## The Water Lilies

### 003 · Inside the infinity

> In the first oval room of the Water Lilies, standing near the middle, where you can turn and see all four compositions.

The light in this room comes from the sky. It falls through a glass roof and a pale stretched-fabric ceiling, and there's a little artificial light up there too. But mostly this is daylight, the way Monet wanted it. So what you see depends a little on the weather outside. A cloud passes over Paris, and the pond in front of you changes.

Around you are four of the eight compositions. Laid end to end, all eight would run to about ninety-one metres of painting. They're made of twenty-two panels, and every one is two metres high. The height never changes. Only the width does, cut to fit each stretch of curved wall.

The rooms themselves have a shape worth knowing. Two ovals, joined by a short passage. From above, the museum compares them to the sign for infinity. An endless loop, for a painter who dreamed of an endless whole.

This first room is about the sky and the plants, reflected in water, from morning to evening. So you're not looking across a pond. You're looking into its surface. Remember Monet's dream, a wave without horizon and without shore. Here it goes all the way around you.

Take a slow turn on the spot before you go up to any one painting. Let your eyes adjust to the light. Then begin with the smallest composition in the room, a single panel six metres wide. It's called Setting Sun, and its story continues in front of it.

### 004 · Monet — Le Soleil couchant (Setting Sun)

> In the first oval room, in front of the shortest of its four compositions, a single panel six metres wide.

By the standards of this room, this is the small one. Six metres wide, Le Soleil couchant, Setting Sun, is the shortest of the eight compositions. It's also the only one painted on a single panel. Every other composition is made of several canvases, joined edge to edge.

Why so short? Because of the stretch of wall where it had to go. Monet didn't leave that to the builders. He settled where each painting would hang, the rhythm from one to the next, even the spaces between them. Nothing in these rooms is where it is by accident.

Then there's the sun itself. These rooms run east to west, in step with the path of the sun. The scenes of sunrise sit toward the east, and the twilight toward the west. So a painted day follows the real one across the building. And here, in the smallest frame of all, the day ends.

Now the looking. Start from a few steps back, and let the whole panel settle in front of you. The title tells you the hour, and then it's up to your eyes. Where does the light sit in the water? Which colour does your eye go to first?

Then walk up close. In this room the brushwork is quick and full of energy, and up close the reflected sky turns back into strokes of paint. Step back again, slowly, and watch for the moment it becomes water.

That's the whole magic trick of these rooms, and you've just done it yourself.

Next, find Clouds. It's one of the two long compositions in this room. The other, on the Seine side, is Morning, and we'll save that for last. Listen to the Clouds track in front of it.

### 005 · Monet — Les Nuages (Clouds)

> In the first oval room, in front of one of its two longest compositions. Morning is the other, on the Seine side; Clouds is the long one that is not on the Seine side.

The clouds in this painting aren't in the sky. They're lying on the surface of a pond.

That's the whole trick of this room. Monet points you downward, at the water, and the sky arrives as a reflection. The room moves from clouds on the water's surface to reflections where the water lilies emerge. Les Nuages, Clouds, sits at the airy end of that journey.

It's one of the two longest paintings in this room, twelve and three-quarter metres from end to end. Its partner in length is Morning, on the Seine side.

Monet had been looking at this pond for a long time before he started. In 1909 the Durand-Ruel gallery showed forty-eight of his water-lily paintings. They were easel paintings, made to hang on an ordinary wall, each one a window onto the pond. So when you stand here, the subject isn't the new thing. What's new is the size, and the fact that it doesn't stop at a frame. It wraps around the room, and you stand inside it.

A painting like this was the work of years, not an afternoon at the water's edge.

Here's a way to look at it. Start at one end, and walk slowly along it to the other, without stopping, the way you'd stroll along the edge of a pond. Watch how the reflected sky changes as you go. Then turn round, and walk back. The second time, you'll see things the first walk missed.

Next, the composition of middle length in this room, eight and a half metres wide. It's called Green Reflections. Play its track there.

### 006 · Monet — Reflets verts (Green Reflections)

> In the first oval room, in front of the composition of middle length, eight and a half metres wide; it is neither the single short panel nor one of the two longest.

These paintings have been through a war of their own. In August 1944, during the Liberation of Paris, a shell struck this building, and some of the panels were damaged. A series of photographs recorded the harm. Afterwards the walls were repaired, and so was the paint.

That was not the end of the worry. From the late 1940s, the people looking after the Water Lilies began to fret about the air in these rooms, and about the light. They still do. Condition reports and careful treatments carry on to this day, and during building works in 2025 the paintings were cleaned. Ninety-one metres of canvas, and every centimetre of it a patient.

So what you're looking at has been watched over for nearly a century. Reflets verts, Green Reflections, eight and a half metres wide. If Clouds was the airy end of this room, the title tells you where this one sits. Down in the green, among the reflections of the plants, where the water lilies rise.

Here's a question to take with you along the canvas. Where does a lily stop, and its reflection begin? Pick one flower and stay with it. Follow its edges. The strokes here are quick and full of movement, and the line between the thing and its mirror image is never quite where you expect.

Then widen your gaze to the water around it, the reflections of the plants that give the painting its name.

That's the green of the title. Not a colour so much as a place to sink into.

To finish this room, go to Morning, the long composition on the Seine side.

### 007 · Monet — Matin (Morning)

> In the first oval room, in front of the long composition on the south wall, the Seine side of the room.

Here's a small riddle. Of the eight compositions in these two rooms, three are called Morning. Only one is an evening.

You've already met the evening, Setting Sun. And this is the first of the mornings. Matin, Morning, as long as Clouds across the room. It hangs on the south wall, the Seine side, the side where the old orangery was all glass. Where the orange trees once took the winter light, Monet's morning now hangs.

In the official order of this room, Morning comes fourth. So the order you've just walked runs from evening round to morning. Which suits a man who was still painting mornings in his eighties.

Not that Monet wanted you to follow anyone's order. He planned these rooms to be entered by more than one way, and walked without a guide. Any starting point is the right one. So treat this as a beginning, too.

Give this one time. Stand far enough back to take in the whole length, if the crowd lets you. Then let your eye travel slowly from one end to the other, and watch the light in the water change as you go. Now look across the room for Setting Sun, and back again. Evening, then morning, in the same breath. It's a whole day, held in one room.

Through the passage, the water changes company. The second room adds the branches of weeping willows at the water's edge, and the paintings there are built on the contrasts those branches make. Two more mornings are waiting, and the longest composition of all. So is the hardest part of Monet's story.

Walk on through the passage into the second oval room, and stop near the middle.

### 008 · The willow room

> Just inside the second oval room, having come through the passage from Room 1. Stand near the middle, where you can see all four compositions.

The first room was about the sky. This one is about the trees at the water's edge, and the years in which they were painted.

Its paintings are built on contrasts, the branches of weeping willows against the light of the pond. Those willows were painted by a man who had just lost a great deal.

His wife, Alice, died in May 1911. The next year, doctors found cataracts in his eyes. Then, early in 1914, his son Jean died at forty-six. That summer the war came. Michel and Jean-Pierre, the young men of the family, were called up. It is said that at times the guns of the front could be heard at Giverny.

Think of him then. In his seventies, widowed, a son buried, his sight beginning to cloud, and his country at war. Many men would have put the brushes down.

Monet's answer was to work bigger than ever. In 1915 he built a third studio, about twenty-three metres by twelve, under a roof of glass. The light fell from above, as it does in here. In that studio, after years of looking down at his pond, he painted canvases like the ones around you.

So take a moment in the middle and turn slowly. Four compositions, twelve panels, and all of them about the edge of the water. Whatever else was happening, this is where he chose to look.

Start with the shorter composition on the end wall, around the passage you just came through. That's Reflections of Trees. Start its track there.

### 009 · Monet — Reflets d'arbres (Reflections of Trees)

> Room 2, on the end wall around the passage from Room 1. It's the shorter of the two end compositions; the much longer one faces it at the far end.

Here's something easy to miss. These paintings don't hang on the wall. They're part of it.

In the first months of 1927, the canvases were glued straight onto the curved walls of these rooms. The French word for it is marouflage. It's a choice with no easy way back. A painting on a hook can be lifted down and carried off. These would have to come off the wall itself. So Monet's gift was never just eight paintings and a building to keep them in. In the end, the two became one thing.

This composition shows how it was put together. Two canvases, each a little over four metres wide, joined into one picture eight and a half metres long. See if you can find the place where one canvas meets the other. Once you've found it, notice how little it matters. The picture simply carries on.

Now the title. Reflets d'arbres, Reflections of Trees. Take it as a fair warning. Monet isn't showing you trees standing on a bank. He's showing you what the pond does with them. And a pond is an odd sort of mirror. It turns a tree upside down, and the slightest breeze breaks it up. So if you find trunks and branches here, think of them as hanging down into the water, not growing up out of the ground.

That's the shift in this second room. In the first room, the water held the sky. Here, the trees at the water's edge come into the picture, branches and all.

This is the shortest of the four compositions in here, and a good place to tune your eye before the long ones.

### 010 · Monet — Le Matin aux saules (Morning with Willows)

Monet's own trees were somewhere else entirely. His pond was at Giverny, about seventy-five kilometres from Paris. And he didn't paint this canvas on its bank. He painted it indoors, in that big glass-roofed studio. What you're seeing is a morning he knew by heart, made enormous.

Le Matin aux saules, Morning with Willows, is one of the long ones, three canvases joined edge to edge. It's the second of the three mornings in this cycle. You met the first in the other room. The third is right behind you.

### 011 · Monet — Le Matin clair aux saules (Clear Morning with Willows)

> Room 2, the south long wall: on your right as you face the far end of the room, directly opposite Morning with Willows.

Why would a painter hang two mornings in the same room, face to face?

Behind you, on the north wall, is Morning with Willows. In front of you, on the Seine side, is Le Matin clair aux saules, Clear Morning with Willows. They are exactly the same size. This one is made of three equal canvases, each about four and a quarter metres across. Same hour, same length, same willows. Only one word is different. Clair. Clear.

So here's a game this room is almost asking you to play. Stand in the middle and turn from one wall to the other. Give each a proper look before you decide anything. Which feels brighter to you? Which feels cooler, or nearer? Which would you rather wake up to?

There's no right answer printed on a card somewhere. Set face to face like this, the two walls make every small difference count. Whatever you notice, you noticed it yourself.

And remember who painted them. These canvases belong to the years after doctors found cataracts in Monet's eyes. One of these mornings is called clear, and it came from a man whose own sight was clouding. Hold that thought while you look.

Then try one more thing. Pick a single patch of this painting, about the size of a window, and stay with it for half a minute. Colours you didn't see at first may arrive late, the way your eyes adjust in a dim room.

If you're here with someone, play the game without comparing notes first. Then compare. You probably won't agree, and that's rather the point.

### 013 · The last years

> Sitting on a bench in the middle of Room 2, if one is free. Otherwise stand anywhere you can see the whole room.

In January 1923, a man of eighty-two went into surgery on his right eye. By then that eye could make out little more than light. The left one saw about a tenth of what it should. The patient was Claude Monet. The man who had urged him to go through with it was Georges Clemenceau.

His cataracts had been found more than ten years before. Now the surgeon, Doctor Charles Coutela, operated on the right eye. It did not bring the world straight back. Monet complained that colours looked wrong, too yellow, or too blue. He is said to have destroyed some of his own canvases in these years. Only with new corrective lenses, over the next two years or so, did his sight partly return.

Think about what that means for the walls around you. Some of this colour was laid down by a painter who could not fully trust his own eyes. And he kept on painting anyway.

He kept the panels, too. The gift was promised, the deed was signed, the rooms here were being built. And at Giverny, Monet went on retouching his canvases, and could not bring himself to let them go.

In the end, he didn't have to. He died at Giverny on the fifth of December 1926, at eighty-six. Within two months, the canvases were being fixed to these walls. The rooms opened that May. About five months too late for him.

So he never sat where you're sitting now. He never saw the light come down on the eight compositions, joined into the shapes and spaces he had worked out so carefully. He planned every part of this experience, and then left it for other people to have. People like you.

Take a minute with that. Let your eyes drift around the room without looking for anything in particular. Monet dreamed of an endless whole, and this is one way to find it.

When you're ready, walk back through the passage into the first room. There's one last story waiting there, and it's about the ceiling over your head.

### 014 · Hidden and found again

> Back in Room 1 on your way out of the Water Lilies, anywhere you can look up at the pale ceiling, before you take the stairs or lift to the lower level.

For about forty years, the Water Lilies lost their sky.

In the late fifties and early sixties, the State acquired a great private collection of modern paintings. You're about to see it downstairs. It needed a home, and the Orangerie had the space. So in the early 1960s, a new floor was built right on top of these oval rooms. The collection was hung up there, at the top of a monumental staircase. And the daylight Monet had planned for his paintings stopped at the new floor.

For decades, the lilies lived under a false ceiling. A fine collection above, and a dimmer room below. The man who had shaped every detail of this light was long gone, and nobody could ask him.

Putting it right took six years. The museum closed in January 2000, and the architect Olivier Brochet took the upper floor away. Light came down through the roof again. The collection went the other way, into about a thousand square metres of new galleries dug out below ground. The rooms reopened in May 2006.

Digging down held a surprise. The builders found a stretch of old city wall from the sixteenth century, from the reign of King Charles the Ninth. Part of it can still be seen on the lower level, so keep an eye out as you go.

Before you leave, look up once more at that pale ceiling. That light is Monet's idea, given back. Whatever the weather is doing outside, it's doing it to the paintings too, just as he wanted.

Now take the stairs or the lift down to the lower level, and start its track in the first hall.

## Paul Guillaume & Domenica Walter

### 015 · Paul Guillaume, the dealer

> On the lower level, in the first hall of the Walter-Guillaume collection, among the large paintings that open the collection. Any spot with room to stand will do.

Paul Guillaume did not start out in a gallery. Around nineteen ten, he was a young man working in a car garage in Paris. In the window, he put a few African sculptures. And one day a poet stopped to look.

The poet was Guillaume Apollinaire. He noticed the sculptures. Then he noticed the young man who had chosen them. With Apollinaire opening doors, Guillaume moved fast. In February nineteen fourteen he opened his own gallery, on the rue de Miromesnil. He was twenty-two.

What followed reads like a roll call of modern art. Through the poet Max Jacob, he met Modigliani, and rented him a studio in Montmartre. Through Max Jacob again, on Apollinaire's advice, he met Derain. In his gallery he staged the first exhibition to set Matisse and Picasso face to face. He published his own magazine, Les Arts à Paris. And he helped the American collector Albert Barnes build his famous collection, now in Philadelphia.

He had a bigger plan, too. He dreamed of creating the first French museum of modern art.

He never got the chance. Paul Guillaume died in nineteen thirty-four, at only forty-two.

So look around this hall with him in mind. The collection opens with big pictures by what the museum calls the modern primitives, among them Picasso, Rousseau, Derain, Modigliani and Matisse. It's hung that way on purpose, to follow Apollinaire's vision of his own time. Look out, too, for African and Oceanian sculptures from Guillaume's own collection, on deposit from the Quai Branly museum. The objects in that garage window, in a sense, have caught up with him.

Most of the rooms down here are given to one artist, and every stop in this guide stands on its own, so take them in whatever order the crowds allow.

As for the museum he dreamed of, it does exist, in a way. Just not quite as he planned it. That part of the story belongs to his widow. Stay in this hall, and listen to her story right here.

### 016 · Domenica and the State

> Stay in the first hall of the Walter-Guillaume collection. Nothing in particular to face: this is the story of how the collection came to the nation.

The collection around you carries two men's names. But the woman who shaped it was neither of them.

She was born Juliette Lacaze, and the world knew her as Domenica. She was seven years younger than Paul Guillaume, and thirty-six when he died. Later she married again, to the architect Jean Walter, and outlived him too.

What she inherited was a dealer's collection, full of bold and difficult work. She did not keep it that way. In the museum's own words, she transformed and reduced it. She parted with many of the most daring works of the period. And she bought what she loved. She liked Cézanne, for one, and added his landscapes.

Then came the deal with France. In nineteen fifty-nine and again in nineteen sixty-three, the state bought the collection in two parts, a hundred and forty-six paintings in all. A public appeal by the Friends of the Louvre helped pay for the most important works. And Domenica kept the right to live with her pictures for the rest of her life.

She made one request. The collection would carry the names of both her husbands, Jean Walter and Paul Guillaume. So the dealer's dream of a museum came true, in his widow's hands, and under a double name. It has been on permanent display here since nineteen eighty-four.

You'll meet Domenica herself shortly, painted by Derain under a very large hat. First, her first husband at twenty-three. Look for Modigliani's portrait of a young man in a dark suit and hat, against a red background, and start its track there.

### 018 · Faces of a dealer — Derain and Van Dongen

## Renoir

### 019 · Renoir — Jeunes filles au piano (Young Girls at the Piano)

> In the room of Renoir's paintings, in front of the upright canvas of two young girls at a piano, not the wider Lerolle sisters picture, about 116 by 81 centimetres. Renoir's works toured abroad until early 2026 and the current hang is unconfirmed, so a work may be missing.

By the early eighteen-nineties, Renoir was around fifty years old. And his friends were annoyed. The French state had never bought a single one of his pictures.

Two of those friends decided to fix it. One was Stéphane Mallarmé. The other was Roger Marx, a young official in the fine arts administration. Together they pushed for Impressionist painting to enter the national museums. After an informal commission from the state, Renoir took up this subject, two girls at a piano. And in eighteen ninety-two, one version was bought for the Musée du Luxembourg. Today that one hangs at the Musée d'Orsay.

But Renoir didn't paint it just once. He painted it several times. There are finished versions in the Metropolitan Museum in New York and in private hands. And there's this one.

This is the version Renoir kept. It spent a few years with his dealer during the First World War, but when he died, it was still in his studio. In nineteen twenty-eight, Paul Guillaume bought it.

So take a moment with the two girls and the piano, knowing that this is the version the painter held on to. The state got its official picture. Renoir kept one for himself.

Guillaume, it turns out, had an eye for exactly that kind of picture. Many of the collection's Renoirs stayed in the artist's studio until his death, and the dealer bought them from the family and the trade through the nineteen-twenties. The collection now holds twenty-five Renoirs, more than of any painter but Derain.

### 020 · Renoir — Yvonne et Christine Lerolle au piano

> In the room of Renoir's paintings, in front of the horizontal canvas of two young women at a piano, one playing, one turning the pages, with two pictures hanging on the wall behind them. The current hang is unconfirmed, so it may be missing.

There are three painters in this picture, if you count properly. Renoir, of course. And then, hanging on the wall behind the girls, two paintings by his friend Degas.

The girls are Yvonne and Christine Lerolle, the two eldest daughters of the painter and collector Henry Lerolle. When Renoir painted them, around eighteen ninety-seven, they were about eighteen and twenty. Music mattered in that house. The family held musical evenings, and one of their regular guests was Claude Debussy.

Yvonne sits at the keys on the left. Christine, on the right, turns the pages of the score. It's a quiet, ordinary moment in a family salon, and Renoir paints it like one.

Now look past them, to the wall. Those two pictures come from their father's own collection, and both are by Degas. One is a racing scene, Avant la course, Before the Race. Degas knew the sisters too. The Musée d'Orsay keeps a photograph he took of himself with Yvonne and Christine.

Here's the curious part. The Lerolles never owned this painting. Renoir never sold it to them, or gave it. It stayed in his hands, apart from a stay at his dealer's during the First World War. ### 021 · Renoir — Claude Renoir en clown (Claude Renoir as a Clown)

> In the room of Renoir's paintings, in front of the portrait of a small boy in a red clown costume. The current hang is unconfirmed, so it may be missing.

Every portrait has a price. This one cost a box of paints and a train set.

The boy is Claude Renoir, the painter's son. The family called him Coco. In nineteen oh nine, when this was painted, he was about eight years old. And he did not want to be here.

The trouble was the costume. Coco hated the red clown suit. Above all, he hated the stockings, which prickled and tickled his legs. The portrait needed several sittings, and every sitting meant the stockings again. In the end, they struck a deal. Coco would put the stockings on, for a short time only. In return, he would get a box of oil paints and a train set.

So when you look at this little clown, remember that he had been well paid to pose.

But Renoir wasn't painting a joke. He wanted a ceremonial portrait of his son, a picture in the grand manner. With it, he meant to measure himself against the old masters, and above all against Velázquez. That's quite an ambition for a picture of a boy in a costume he can't stand. Here, the ceremonial costume is a clown's red suit. And the young sitter has been promised a train.

So look at it both ways at once. See the red costume as Renoir wanted it seen, a painter's challenge to the old masters. Then spare a thought for the stockings, the cause of all the trouble. A great painter's ambition and a small boy's bargain, in a single picture.

### 022 · Renoir — Gabrielle et Jean (Gabrielle and Jean)

> In the room of Renoir's paintings, in front of the small canvas of a young woman holding a little boy on her knee as he plays with toy animals on a table. The current hang is unconfirmed, so it may be missing.

Anyone who has tried to photograph a toddler knows the problem. Now imagine painting one, in oils. Renoir had a solution, and her name was Gabrielle.

Gabrielle Renard came into the Renoir household to look after the children, and became the family's faithful servant. She had a gift the painter badly needed. She could always hold the attention of a very small model.

The model here is Jean, Renoir's second son. Between eighteen ninety-five and the following year, Renoir painted the two of them many times. Together the pictures make a real album of his son's early childhood. This is one page of it.

Look at how it works, with Gabrielle holding Jean on her knee. On the table in front of them are toy farm animals, and she's keeping him busy with them. That's the trick, because while the little boy plays, the painter paints.

Now look closely at the toys, because they're barely there. Renoir brushes them in so loosely that they melt into the tabletop. As the museum's own notes admit, we can barely recognise the cow in Gabrielle's hand. Renoir didn't care about the cow. He cared about the boy, and about the woman keeping him happy.

So this is a painting about a kind of work that's usually invisible. The nanny is doing a job, and the painter is doing a job, and the picture only exists because the two jobs fit together.

Gabrielle didn't stay in the background. She quickly became one of Renoir's favourite models, and about ten years later he painted her as a reclining nude, in the great tradition of the old masters. ### 023 · Renoir — Bathers and Gabrielle reclining

> In the room of Renoir's paintings, in front of his nudes: a bather with brown hair pinned up, drying her leg on a white cloth by the water, a golden-haired bather drawing a white drape across her chest, and a long horizontal reclining nude. The current hang is unconfirmed, so one may be missing.

In eighteen eighty-one, Renoir went to Italy. He was forty. After that journey, the way he drew began to change.

You can see the change in the seated bather by the water. Renoir painted her in eighteen eighty-three. She's a traditional figure in a timeless waterside setting, her brown hair pinned up. She sits on a white cloth and uses it to dry her leg. Look at her outline. The contours are firm, and the line is sure. The style is more confident, and it points straight ahead to his Large Bathers, Les Grandes Baigneuses, now in Philadelphia. Some historians think the model looks like Suzanne Valadon, whom Renoir often painted at the time. A maybe, nothing more.

About a dozen years later comes the bather with long hair. She steps out of the water and draws a white drape across her chest. Her loose golden hair echoes the movement of the plants behind her. This is Renoir's pearly period, named for the luminous skin tones. An almost identical bather hangs at the Barnes Foundation in Philadelphia.

And then the long picture. The woman reclining here is Gabrielle, the nanny with the toy animals, by now one of his favourite models. In nineteen oh six, Renoir painted her as a nude indoors, reclining on a canvas more than a metre and a half wide.

That puts her in very old company. The reclining nude indoors runs through Titian, Rubens, Goya, Ingres and Manet. In Renoir's last decade, Titian and Rubens mattered most to him. Indoor nudes are rare in his work. In just a few years around this one, he painted three large horizontal figures reclining on cushions.

Three nudes, then. A sharper line, a pearly glow, and the household's own nanny, laid out like a Venus.

### 024 · Renoir's quiet pictures

> In the room of Renoir's paintings, among the smaller works: a little still life of apples and pears, a vase of tulips in reds, greens and yellows, and a snowy landscape. The current hang is unconfirmed, so one may be missing.

Renoir is famous for faces and flesh. But some of his most revealing pictures here are small, and have no people in them at all.

Start with the apples and pears. It's a little canvas, painted around eighteen ninety-five. The museum singles it out as the Renoir still life most influenced by Cézanne, both in its subject and in its highly intricate composition. Here, you can watch Renoir trying things the Cézanne way, building the arrangement with care.

Then the tulips. The bouquet shimmers in reds, greens and yellows, in a green vase, against a background of red and beige strokes. He probably painted it at Cagnes, soon after his final move to the south. It stayed in his studio until his death, and Paul Guillaume bought it ten years later.

And finally, the snow. This is the earliest picture of the three, probably painted in early eighteen seventy-five, after a heavy winter snowfall. Snow is supposed to be white. Renoir looked harder. He was experimenting with coloured shadows, showing how snow reflects the colours of everything around it. Stand back a little and look into the shadows for that colour.

So three small pictures, across thirty years, and three different painters in one man.

## Cézanne

### 025 · Cézanne — Pommes et biscuits (Apples and Biscuits)

> In the room of Cézanne's paintings, find a still life of a plate and a few apples set on a chest. Cézannes from this collection are often lent out, and some may be away until early 2027.

In May 1952, a painting of apples caused a sensation in a Paris saleroom. The sale was at the Galerie Charpentier. The buyer was Domenica Walter, Paul Guillaume's widow, and she paid an unprecedented sum. The effect was instant. The purchase turned the public's attention to her, and to the magnificent collection she had been building.

That collection holds fifteen Cézannes. In the nineteen-twenties, Paul bought several major ones, among them portraits of the painter's wife and son. After his death, Domenica kept buying, landscapes above all. And then this still life, the most public purchase of them all.

She also bought one painting twice, in a way. Paul had acquired a canvas of flowers and fruit from the dealer Ambroise Vollard. Years after his death, Domenica bought a picture of flowers in a blue vase. Neither of them knew they were two halves of the same unfinished canvas, probably cut apart by a dealer long before. A curator, Michel Hoog, pieced the story together in 1992. Husband and wife had reunited a painting without ever meaning to.

Now the apples. The museum counts this among the symbols of Cézanne's great mastery of still life, and part of the reason is how little there is. A plate and a few apples, all set on a chest. The roundness of each apple is built out of colour. His experiments in expressing volume through colour are fully developed here. Give it a moment, and the whole arrangement settles, like a scale that has just stopped moving.

So this small group of fruit carries two stories at once. A painter building an apple out of colour, and a widow whose purchase made the public look at her whole collection.

Now find Madame Cézanne, the painter's wife, painted full length in a garden, and listen to her story there.

### 026 · Cézanne — Hortense: two portraits of Madame Cézanne

> In the room of Cézanne's paintings, find the full-length woman in a garden, then the seated woman in a black dress against a blue-green ground. Either may be away on loan until early 2027.

Cézanne painted Hortense Fiquet some twenty-five times. That's a great many hours of sitting still while someone stares at you. And yet, standing in front of her, you may find it hard to say what she was like.

She was a young model when they met, and she lived with Cézanne, and in 1872 she gave birth to their son, Paul. They married only in 1886, fourteen years later. Paul Guillaume bought both of these portraits of her in the nineteen-twenties.

Start with the garden, Madame Cézanne au jardin, Madame Cézanne in the Garden, from around 1880. A full-length figure out of doors is unusual for him. He preferred to frame people close, in bare rooms, and here her face gives nothing away. Then look down to the bottom of the canvas, which is barely painted. A wide patch of white priming simply shows through, as if he stopped mid-sentence.

The other portrait is later. She sits in a plain armchair, hands in her lap, in a simple black dress. The pose is stiff and upright, almost ceremonial, as if she were posing for a statue of herself. Behind her, a blue-green background is brushed on so loosely that you can't tell what kind of room she's in. Her face is completely expressionless, and rather unflattering. This one is unfinished too.

It would be easy to read all this as a marriage gone cold. Maybe it was, but the museum offers another reading. With the people closest to him, Cézanne put his research first. He wasn't chasing a mood or a flattering likeness. He wanted his sitter to have monumentality and permanence, to sit on the canvas as solidly as the apples on his table.

So give her a minute, because the longer you look, the less she seems bored, and the more she seems simply there, unmovable, the way a hillside is there.

Their son is close by in this room too: a boy's head and shoulders, cropped close against a blue-green ground.

### 027 · Cézanne — Portrait du fils de l'artiste (Portrait of the Artist's Son)

> In the room of Cézanne's paintings, find the close-cropped portrait of a boy, with just the back of an armchair at the right against a blue-green ground. Cézannes from this collection are often lent out; it may be away until early 2027.

Here's a small coincidence to start with: the boy in this portrait is called Paul. So was his father, Paul Cézanne. And so was the man who bought the painting in the nineteen-twenties, the dealer Paul Guillaume. Three Pauls, one canvas.

This is Cézanne's son, Hortense's boy. The picture dates from around 1880, so he is still a child here. Cézanne painted him more than once in those years. The museum calls this one the most ambitious and the most accomplished of them all.

At first it hardly looks ambitious, just a head and shoulders, seen close up. Then the decisions start to show. Young Paul seems to be perched on the arm of an armchair. But Cézanne paints only the back of the chair, off to the right, against a plain blue-green ground. The framing is so tight that it crops the boy, and it crops the chair, the way a photograph might.

Now follow the curves: the rounded back of the chair, the slope of the shoulders, then the neck, the oval of the face and the cap of hair. Each one answers the next, like lines in a rhyme. Cézanne simplifies every form and gives it a firm outline, in a way that recalls the work of Gauguin.

What's missing matters as much as what's there. There are no toys, no room, no little story about childhood, just a boy, part of a chair, and a colour behind them.

You might expect a father to soften a portrait of his son. Cézanne does something rarer, and looks at the boy the way he looked at everything, as a problem worth solving properly. And somehow that seriousness feels like a form of love.

Now look for a very different subject: a landscape where an orange rock rises behind a patch of trees, under blue sky. That's Le Rocher rouge. Play its track there.

### 028 · Cézanne — Le Rocher rouge (The Red Rock)

> In the room of Cézanne's paintings, find the upright landscape with an orange rock behind a patch of trees, topped by blue sky. It may be away on loan until early 2027.

Near the end of his life, Cézanne kept going back to the same unpromising place. It wasn't a cathedral or a famous view. It was a deserted quarry called Bibémus, near Aix. For about ten years from 1895, he painted its rocks again and again.

This canvas is one of those visits, from around 1895. It's about ninety centimetres tall and sixty-six wide. Domenica bought it after Paul Guillaume's death, and the museum calls it one of the masterpieces of Cézanne's maturity.

Look at how little he needs. A patch of trees, painted in short strokes laid across each other, stands out against the orange rock, and above it all there's a band of blue sky. Green, orange and blue are the whole subject.

And yet it doesn't feel empty. Let your eye rest on the trees for a while. Each stroke stays separate, crossing its neighbours, and still the patch holds together against the bare rock.

Young painters noticed. Cézanne died in 1906, and within two years, Picasso and Braque had begun the experiments that became Cubism. Four years after his death, Guillaume Apollinaire, the same man who had opened doors for Paul Guillaume, summed it up. "It is known that most new painters lay claim to this sincere and selfless artist."

Sincere and selfless are not the words you usually hear about a revolutionary. But stand here long enough, and they fit. There's no showing off in this picture, just a man in a quarry, trying, very patiently, to get it right.

Next, look for three of Cézanne's landscapes of trees and houses, painted years apart, and stand where you can see them together.

### 029 · Cézanne — Trees and houses: three landscapes

> In the room of Cézanne's paintings, find three landscapes: an upright view with a leaning tree trunk and a low wall, a hamlet among bare trees with pale houses, and the late landscape of the park at Château Noir. Any of them may be away on loan until early 2027.

If you want to watch a painter change his mind about the world, a wall of landscapes is a good place to do it. These three span roughly a quarter of a century of Cézanne's life. And they don't look like the work of the same eye.

Start with the earliest, the upright one, Le Pin à l'Estaque, The Pine at L'Estaque. He painted it during a stay at L'Estaque in 1876, and it shows the influence of Camille Pissarro. You can find that influence in the tall format, in the tree trunk right up front, and in the low wall. Follow that leaning trunk and its branches. They lead your eye, quite deliberately, into the trees at the centre. This is a painter still using the old tools of a landscape to guide you in.

Now the second, Trees and Houses, from around 1885. A little hamlet sits among two patches of leafless trees, near Le Jas de Bouffan, the Cézanne family house outside Aix. Dark trunks stand against very pale, almost schematic houses. And here something gives way. The planes blur into one another, and traditional perspective starts to dissolve. Where exactly is that house? In front of the tree, or behind it? Cézanne painted this view three times. The other two are far away, in New York and in Oslo.

Then the latest, In the Park of Château Noir, painted around the turn of the century, a few years before his death. Just notice how differently it is made from the pine at L'Estaque, and decide for yourself how far he has travelled.

Three walks in the country, then. In the first, the painter takes your hand and leads you in. By the last, he trusts you to find your own way.

### 030 · Cézanne — Bathers and tables

> In the room of Cézanne's paintings, find the long, narrow frieze of a boat and bathers, the tiny picnic scene with a church spire, and two still lifes: a straw-covered vase with a sugar bowl, and fruit with a napkin and a milk can. Any may be away on loan until early 2027.

One of the paintings in this room has been in three pieces. It's the long, narrow one, La Barque et les baigneurs, The Boat and the Bathers, about thirty centimetres high and well over a metre wide.

Cézanne painted it around 1890 as a decoration for the Paris apartment of the collector Victor Chocquet. It was meant to hang above a door, paired with another panel. According to the writer Georges Rivière, both were still unfinished when Chocquet died in 1891. At some point afterwards, the frieze was cut into three. Domenica gave two of the pieces to the state. The middle was still missing. In 1973, the national museums finally bought it, and the boat and its bathers were put back together.

Now the smallest picture here, Luncheon on the Grass, barely bigger than a book. Young people picnic near a village, and a church bell tower shows. In the centre a woman stands, and the church spire seems to carry her silhouette up into the sky. Cézanne may have been thinking of Poussin's bacchanals, which he copied at the Louvre, or of happy childhood memories around Aix. As for the title, it may have been added later. If it is his, it may be a sly nod to Manet's famous picnic.

Then the tables. In the still life with the straw-covered vase, look hard at the sugar bowl and the apples. You're seeing them from angles that can't all be true at once. Cézanne picks a coherent picture over a realistic one. There's even a slanting line nobody can quite name. A knife handle, perhaps.

In the still life with fruit, a napkin and a milk can, his usual props come back: the chest, the apples, the knife, the wallpaper. The greens are fainter and the lines more geometric, echoed by the pattern on the wall. That unusual viewing angle, the museum says, was a true innovation, even shocking in its day.

## Henri Rousseau

### 031 · Rousseau — La Noce (The Wedding Party)

> Among Henri Rousseau's paintings on the lower level; it may instead hang in the large first hall of the collection, with the big formats. Look for a tall group portrait of a wedding party in front of trees, with a dog in the foreground.

Henri Rousseau's day job gave him a nickname that has outlived almost everything else about him. He was a clerk at the octroi, the toll barrier at the gates of Paris. So people called him le Douanier, the customs officer, and the name has stuck ever since.

If you know one thing about him, it's probably his jungle pictures. Don't look for those here. The Rousseaus in this collection stay much closer to home: a wedding, a neighbour's cart, a child, anglers, a factory.

And this is the most ambitious of them. La Noce, The Wedding Party, is one of the most ambitious compositions Rousseau ever undertook. He painted it around 1905, when he was already in his early sixties.

Look at the guests first. Every face is a particular person, and nobody is just a generic relative filling a gap. Then look behind them. The trees are painted kind by kind, each species told apart with real care, and they shrink fast as they go back. A yellow-ochre background glows through, and together with the trees it forms a kind of frame around the whole party. It's a mandorla, the almond-shaped glory that surrounds holy figures in religious art.

That's no accident. The critic Adolphe Basler compared this painting to the idealised figures of medieval frescoes, and to the ex-votos of the old "Primitive" masters. So here is an everyday wedding party, painted with the stillness of an altarpiece.

And down in front, a dog, taking up its place as solemnly as anyone.

Rousseau did not have long left after this. He died in 1910, of gangrene from an infected leg. The wedding party passed later into Paul Guillaume's collection, and from Domenica's to the state.

### 032 · Rousseau — La Carriole du père Junier (Old Junier's Cart)

> Among Henri Rousseau's paintings on the lower level, find the wide picture of a family in a horse-drawn cart, pulled by a white mare, with three dogs.

Every neighbourhood has a grocer who knows everyone. Rousseau's was Claude Junier. He and his wife ran a grocery near the painter's home, and every morning Junier went out to buy vegetables from the market gardeners. He also trained horses, and he was very proud of his white mare, Rosa.

In 1908, the Juniers most likely commissioned this picture. Junier sits on the front seat. Beside him sits Rousseau himself. Behind them ride Madame Junier and two young relatives.

Now look at Rosa. She stands, curiously, on the tips of her hooves. It lets Rousseau show off the shadows underneath her, and it makes her look suspended, like a dancer on points, a carthorse doing ballet.

Look at the passengers too. Apart from Junier, every one of them faces you square on, stiff and upright. The museum compares them to Byzantine icons. Count the dogs while you're at it: there are three, which is rare in Rousseau's work.

Here's the part I love. Rousseau worked from a photograph of the family, but he didn't simply copy it. He changed the size and position of the dogs, and he left out a tree. The painter Max Weber pointed out that one dog was far too big. Rousseau's answer was that the painting demanded it.

That reply tells you a great deal. You might think Rousseau naive, as if he couldn't help the way his pictures came out. But a man who edits a photograph, moves the dogs and drops a tree is making choices. He knew what the picture needed better than the camera did.

It's a big canvas, almost a metre high and well over a metre wide, for a portrait of the local grocer. And the grocer's horse gets to be a dancer.

### 033 · Rousseau — L'Enfant à la poupée (Child with a Doll)

> Among Henri Rousseau's paintings on the lower level, find the portrait of a child holding a doll.

In 1908, Pablo Picasso was rummaging through a junk shop in Paris, run by a dealer known as Père Soulié. Among the odds and ends he found a portrait of a woman by Henri Rousseau. He paid five francs for it, and he kept it for the rest of his life. Today it belongs to the Musée Picasso.

That little purchase set off something much bigger. In November of that year, Picasso and his friends threw a banquet in Rousseau's honour at the Bateau-Lavoir. Picture it: Picasso's young circle, raising their glasses to a toll clerk turned painter, a man in his sixties.

So what did they see in him? Remember the modern primitives the collection opens with. Rousseau is right there among them, with Picasso and Matisse. In this building, primitive is a compliment.

Which brings us to this child.

The title doesn't tell us who this child was. So for once, let's do the looking together, with questions rather than answers.

Where are the child's eyes, on the doll or on you? Think back to the passengers in Junier's cart, turned to face you like icons. Does this child do the same? Look at the hands, and at how much space the doll takes up between them. And look at the ground, and what lies behind. Is it a real place, or a backdrop?

There are no right answers here, only yours. But if a plain picture of a child with a toy makes you look twice, you've felt a little of what drew those young painters to that banquet.

### 034 · Rousseau — Small worlds: anglers and a chair factory

> Among Henri Rousseau's paintings on the lower level, find the anglers with an aeroplane in the sky and a factory chimney behind, and the chair factory, which the collection owns in two versions, one small.

There's an aeroplane in this fishing scene. Rousseau painted Les Pêcheurs à la ligne, The Anglers, in 1908 or 1909, at the very dawn of flying. And there it is, sailing over a few people out with their rods.

Rousseau liked to paint ordinary people at leisure. Not heroes, not saints, just people with time on their hands. But he doesn't let them live in a timeless postcard. Behind them rises a factory chimney, and above them flies that machine. They're two small signs that this is the twentieth century, slipped into a scene that could otherwise belong to almost any decade.

Then there's the factory he chose to paint for its own sake. La Fabrique de chaises à Alfortville, The Chair Factory at Alfortville, from 1897. The museum's explanation for the subject is wonderfully simple: he chose it because it was so unusual. Who else would pick a chair factory as a subject?

Look at how big the building is. It's oversized compared with the landscape around it, and especially compared with the figures. They look like visitors in a world built for the factory.

And here's the bonus: the collection owns this factory twice. There's a small version too, only thirty-eight by forty-six centimetres, painted around the same time. If both are hanging, play spot the difference. In the small one, the landscape gets more room, and above all the sky. It's the same building, from around the same year, but in one, the factory is the hero. In the other, it shares the stage with the sky.

It's a quiet idea, and a modern one. The ordinary edge of the city, with its chimneys and workshops, is worth a painting.

## Matisse

### 036 · Matisse — Les Trois Sœurs (The Three Sisters)

> Among Matisse's paintings on the lower level. Look for a single upright canvas of three dark-haired young women seated against a plain brown background, two looking out, one reading.

Paul Guillaume helped send a great many paintings to America. This one, he went out and bought for himself.

### 037 · Matisse — A room in Nice: Femmes au canapé, and Le Boudoir

> Among Matisse's paintings on the lower level. Look for a hotel interior with a large red curtain on the right, a tiled floor, a window onto the sky, and two lightly sketched women by a divan. Le Boudoir, another interior, may hang nearby.

Here's a great painter who kept checking into the same hotel. From nineteen sixteen, Matisse often stayed at the Hôtel de la Méditerranée in Nice. He'd come south captivated by the light of the Midi. And the hotel itself became his subject.

This is one of those pictures. Femmes au canapé, Women on a Sofa, from nineteen twenty-one, shows his own hotel room.

But Matisse saw a room as more than furniture. In Nice he kept coming back to one idea. An interior that feels close and heavy, and a door or window that opens onto the free world outside. Inside and outside, shut in and let out.

Now look at how he builds that into a single picture. There are really two ways of looking here. One looks down at the floor, where the tiles give you pattern and surface, but almost no depth. The other looks right out through the window, far into the sky.

Follow the lines, and you'll find the composition makes a triangle that steers your eye toward one point out in the sky beyond the glass. The big red curtain on the right softens it, so the push isn't too hard. And there's no reflection on the window, nothing to stop your gaze on the way out.

The two women are almost an afterthought. They're over on the right, more or less sketched. The divan got more attention. Matisse repainted it to get the angle exactly right.

Then there's the colour, reds and greens, some warm, some cool, pulling against each other. The museum reads them as the painting's mixed feelings, the weight of the room and the wish to be out in that light.

If Le Boudoir is hanging near it, compare the two. Another room seen from the inside, another small world Matisse made out of an ordinary interior.

### 038 · Matisse — Femme au violon and Femme à la mandoline

### 039 · Matisse — The Odalisques

> Among Matisse's paintings on the lower level. Look for the reclining and seated women among screens, curtains and patterned fabric: a nude in blue tones before a Moorish screen, a nude on a white sheet before a red curtain, and the odalisques in red and in grey trousers.

In nineteen twenty-seven, some critics looked at paintings like these and saw a wallpaper dealer's display. It was meant as an insult. By accident, it comes close to describing what Matisse was after.

These are his odalisques, painted in Nice in the nineteen twenties. Matisse drew on two earlier French painters, Ingres and Chassériau. But for him, the museum explains, the theme was a pretext, a reason to paint the nude.

And he wanted more than that. He wanted the body, and even its shadow, to become part of the decoration around it. The pattern, the screen, the fabric and the woman, all lying flat together on the surface of the canvas. So the wallpaper was rather the point.

Start with the Blue Odalisque. The décor is cut down to a Moorish screen, and she's closed into a tight little space. One arm is raised, which lifts the curve of her chest. The whole thing is painted in blues, so thinly and fluidly that it brings watercolour to mind.

Now find the Reclining Draped Nude, where he strips the setting right back. A red curtain, a plain white sheet over the sofa, a cushion or two. Heavy black lines draw her outline, and she lies in a pose Matisse loved, legs drawn up, arms raised above her head. Look at her right side. The pink-tinted folds of her body echo the folds of the cloth at her hips.

Then find the two odalisques named for their trousers, one red, one grey. Same theme, two variations. Compare them for yourself.

Several of these came to Paul Guillaume in nineteen twenty-nine. And at the very moment Matisse was painting them, Picasso painted a languid reclining woman of his own. Keep these women in mind when you meet her.

## Picasso

### 040 · Picasso — L'Étreinte (The Embrace)

> Among Picasso's works on the lower level. Look for a tall pastel of a nude man and woman embracing in a bedroom, in blues and pinks. It is a light-sensitive work lent by the Louvre and may not be on the wall.

Picasso made it in nineteen oh three, in Barcelona, in the depths of what we now call his Blue Period. He was working in a studio he had once shared with his friend Carlos Casagemas. Two years earlier, in Paris, Casagemas had taken his own life. Picasso was deeply affected.

This sheet belongs to a whole run of drawings of a nude couple, standing together. They led up to one large painting, La Vie, Life, now in the Cleveland Museum of Art. So what you're looking at is Picasso working his way toward a much bigger picture, about life itself.

Look at the room around them. It's a bedroom. The colours are those cold Blue Period blues, warmed here and there with pink. The man and the woman hold each other, and their faces are barely visible. Nothing here is posed for us.

It's pastel, not paint, lent by the Louvre, and so fragile that some days it rests away from the light.

Paul Guillaume bought it in nineteen thirty, from the great dealer Ambroise Vollard. Guillaume was never Picasso's official dealer. What brought them together, the museum says, was a shared passion for so-called primitive art. ### 041 · Picasso — Les Adolescents and Nu sur fond rouge

> Among Picasso's works on the lower level; large works may hang in the collection's opening hall. Look for a big canvas of two nude young figures on a pink-ochre ground, one with a pitcher on her head, and a smaller nude against a flat red background.

There's another painting hiding in this one. Look down, below the legs of these two young figures. You'll see faint wavy lines that don't belong to anything. They're the trace of a different composition, laid out sideways, that Picasso painted over and abandoned.

Now step back and take in what he kept. Two nude adolescents, modelled in pink and red on a ground of nearly the same colour. On one side, a young man crosses his arms above his head. On the other, a girl, or perhaps an androgynous youth, balances a pitcher on her head. They feel less like people than like sculpture, calm and solid and almost ancient.

Where did that colour come from? In nineteen oh six Picasso spent time in Gósol, a village in the Catalan Pyrenees, with his companion Fernande Olivier. The earth there had an unusual ochre tint, and it seeps into everything here. This is his pink classicism. It looks forward, oddly, to the classical giants he would paint fifteen years later, which you'll meet in this collection too.

But Picasso didn't stay calm for long. Find the smaller nude against a flat red background, painted later that same year. She has long hair, and her head is tilted as she holds a lock of it. Yet everything is hardening. The body turns into simplified, geometric blocks. And look at the eyes, two black slits without expression. The face has become a mask.

Within a few months, Picasso would paint Les Demoiselles d'Avignon, now in New York. The museum calls this red nude the first of a series that led up to it, a picture that already holds the seeds of that revolution. So between these two canvases, in a single year, you can watch him change direction.

### 043 · Picasso — Grande baigneuse (Large Bather)

> Among Picasso's works on the lower level; as a very large canvas it may hang in the collection's opening hall. Look for a monumental seated nude with massive, statue-like limbs; nearby, a seated nude holding a drape, and two small studies of women at a fountain.

A few years earlier, this painter was breaking tables and bottles into Cubist fragments. And then, around nineteen twenty-one, he gives us this. A woman so massive she seems carved rather than painted.

She sits enormous on the canvas, with drapery about her, her limbs huge, her face heavy and solid. The proportions come from ancient sculpture, pushed until they're almost too much. It can feel monumental and slightly unsettling at the same time.

Why the turn? It's known as the return to order. Picasso looked back to the art of Greece and Rome, to Ingres, and to Renoir. And he wasn't alone. ### 044 · Picasso — Femme au tambourin (Woman with a Tambourine)

> Among Picasso's works on the lower level. Look for a wide canvas of a woman reclining on cushions, propped on one elbow and holding a tambourine, in mauve, yellow, garnet red and turquoise; nearby, a Cubist still life of a compote dish, bottle and glass on a tilted beige tabletop.

In nineteen eighteen, Paul Guillaume became the first dealer to give an exhibition to Matisse and Picasso together. He hung them in his gallery face to face, one against the other, and let the visitors compare.

### 045 · Modigliani — Le Jeune Apprenti (The Young Apprentice)

> Among Modigliani's portraits on the lower level. Look for a tall canvas of a seated young man in greys and browns, his head resting on one hand, a white collar and intensely blue eyes.

Most of the faces in this collection have names. Dealers, wives, nieces, the painter's own children. This young man has none. He's simply the apprentice, and Modigliani gives him as much care as he ever gave a patron.

## Soutine

### 046 · Soutine — Le Petit Pâtissier (The Little Pastry Cook)

> In the room of Soutine's paintings, in front of a small portrait of a thin young cook in a white pastry cook's hat and jacket, holding a flat, bright red cloth.

One December day in 1922, an American collector walked into Paul Guillaume's gallery in Paris, and a painting stopped him in his tracks. It showed a pastry cook. The collector was Albert Barnes. The painter was a young man from near Minsk named Chaïm Soutine, and few people outside Paris had heard of him.

Guillaume had. He'd found Soutine through an article by the critic Michel Georges-Michel, and he was one of the first to take his painting seriously. Now he took Barnes to Soutine's dealer, Léopold Zborowski. Barnes bought dozens of canvases. Through that one introduction, Soutine became internationally known.

And the pastry cook that started it all? It seems it went to America with Barnes. Guillaume never quite forgot it.

Soutine liked to work in series, and he painted this subject six times. In the early nineteen thirties, long after Barnes had made Soutine famous, Guillaume bought this version. The museum says he bought it "in memory of the first Pastry Cook that he had liked so much." So what you're looking at is a kind of consolation prize. A dealer's keepsake of the one that got away.

Now look at the boy himself. The face is stretched long, the neck is fragile, and the features and limbs are pulled out of shape. That distortion is where the power comes from. His hat and jacket are white, touched with grey and green. And in his hands is a cloth of flat, vivid red, the hottest thing in the picture.

One more thing. The pose may come from a portrait in the Louvre, Jean Fouquet's Charles the Seventh, King of France. A kitchen boy, sitting like a king.

### 047 · Soutine — Le Garçon d'étage and La Fiancée

> In the room of Soutine's paintings. Look for a hotel waiter in a red waistcoat, standing with his hands on his hips, then a tall, narrow portrait of a thin young woman in a white dress.

In a smart spa hotel, the staff are meant to be invisible. They carry the trays, open the doors, and nobody really looks at them. Soutine looked.

Between 1926 and 1928 he spent time at Châtel-Guyon, a spa town, where he met the collectors Madeleine and Marcellin Castaing. He seems to have watched the hotel staff closely there. ### 048 · Soutine — Bœuf et tête de veau and Le Lapin

> In the room of Soutine's paintings, in front of the still lifes of meat: a side of beef beside a calf's head against a dark ground, and a tall, narrow picture of a rabbit hanging by one leg beside a red jug.

For a painter whose pictures can look this violent, Soutine had surprisingly old-fashioned heroes. Not the rebels of his own day. Rembrandt. Chardin. Two old masters who were not afraid to paint a dead animal.

### 049 · Soutine — Houses that move: Le Village and the landscapes

> In the room of Soutine's paintings, in front of the landscapes: villages and roads whose houses lean and twist. Le Village is the most tangled of them.

Most landscape painters hope the view will hold still. In Soutine's landscapes, nothing holds still at all.

It started with a deal. His dealer, Léopold Zborowski, sent him to Céret, in the south, with his room and board paid. In return, Soutine handed over his paintings. He stayed about three years. Then came Cagnes, and the village of La Basse Gaude, about eight kilometres away.

Look for Paysage avec personnage, Landscape with Figure. A road climbs straight up the picture. The houses in the middle distance lean to the left, as if a wind had caught them. That lean is a habit he picked up in Céret, and he kept it.

Now Le Village. It was painted in 1923, one of nine pictures he made at La Basse Gaude in barely two years. The museum calls it the most radical and the most unusual of all his landscapes. You can see why. Every house, roof and tree is twisted and wound into its neighbour. There's no proper perspective and no balance. You can't quite tell where one building ends and the next begins. And yet it still reads as a village, and it feels more alive than most villages that keep their walls straight.

Les Maisons, The Houses, is another of his landscapes here. See whether its houses manage to stand any straighter.

Soutine's later years were hard. During the German Occupation he lived in hiding. He died in 1943, at about fifty. Thanks to Paul Guillaume's early eye, this museum now holds the largest collection of his paintings in Europe.

## Derain

### 050 · Derain — Arlequin et Pierrot (Harlequin and Pierrot)

> Among André Derain's paintings, or in the collection's opening hall, in front of the large picture of two costumed musicians with guitars: Harlequin in a chequered suit and two-cornered hat, Pierrot all in white with a ruff and a black skullcap.

### 051 · Derain — The painter's niece, and a Harlequin alone

### 052 · Derain — La Table de cuisine (The Kitchen Table) and the still lifes

> In the room of André Derain's paintings, in front of the large, dark still life of kitchen utensils on a table, painted mostly in browns. His smaller still lifes of fruit, bread, jugs and baskets hang nearby.

It's only a kitchen table, with its utensils, painted mostly in shades of brown. Yet in the nineteen thirties, critics stood in front of this picture and thought of Caravaggio.

La Table de cuisine, the Kitchen Table, was painted in the first half of the nineteen twenties. It's one of the most important of Derain's big, dark still lifes of kitchen things. From 1927 it was exhibited and reproduced, and success came quickly. What those critics saw was light pressing against dark, the drama Caravaggio was famous for.

Now look at how still it is. The whole picture is built like a piece of architecture, solid and static. After Soutine's swaying houses, it can feel like stepping into a cool, quiet room.

That calm suited Paul Guillaume.

Around it hang smaller still lifes. Pick any one and look for the black lines Derain draws around the basket, the grapes or the glass. It's the same calm, in miniature.

Then find Pears and Jug, Poires et cruche. The museum calls it a spirited sketch rather than a finished work. Here you catch Derain thinking out loud. The pears are lit with quick strokes of vibrant yellow.

A finished table and an unfinished sketch, in the same room. Put them together and the secret is out. That stillness was never an accident. It was built, stroke by stroke, by a painter who knew exactly how fast he could go, and chose to go slowly.

Nearby, his nudes and his landscapes of Provence are waiting.

### 053 · Derain — Nudes and the light of Provence

> In the room of André Derain's paintings, among his nudes and his landscapes of Provence. Look for a seated blond nude resting her head in her hand, and a village above a road under a deep blue sky.

Derain was a modern painter who kept looking over his shoulder at the old masters. This corner of his room shows you how.

Find the Blond Model, Le Modèle blond. She sits with her legs under a cloth, her head resting in her right hand, and her body curves into an oval of dark contours. Derain drew on Boucher, Fragonard and Renoir's bathers for her. If you've been to the Renoir room, she may look like a relative.

Then the light changes. In 1932 Derain was painting in Provence. In The Road, La Route, the village of Eygalières sits above the road to Saint-Rémy, warm colour under an intensely blue sky. In Trees and Village, it's the same village, framed by plane trees, under a stormy sky. One place, two weathers.

Old masters indoors, the light of Provence outdoors. This is the side of Derain that Paul Guillaume chose to collect, and it's where his collection takes its leave of you. A dealer who loved the new, and still wanted it to keep faith with the past.

When you've seen all you want down here, one more look waits upstairs. Follow the signs back up to the Water Lilies, step into either oval room, and listen to its track there. Short of time? Follow the exit signs instead. A last stop waits on the garden side as you leave.

## Back to the Water Lilies

### 054 · A second look at the Water Lilies

> Back on the ground floor, in either of the two oval Water Lilies rooms. Find a bench or a quiet spot facing whichever composition you like best.

Nothing on these walls has changed since you first walked in. But you have.

You've met a pastry cook and a hotel waiter, a dealer dressed as Pierrot, a girl who grew up in her uncle's paintings. And here's something worth knowing as you look at the water again. Monet painted these panels between 1914 and 1926. So while Soutine was painting his pastry cook, and Derain his Harlequin and Pierrot, an old man at Giverny was still at work on these. The same years. A completely different idea of what a painting is for.

Remember that upper floor? The collection you've just seen once hung on it, right above these rooms. Now the two live side by side, the lilies in the light and the collection below.

So the light you're sitting in is a kind of homecoming. It comes in through the ceiling, and these rooms run east to west, with the course of the sun. Depending on the sky outside, the water can look quite different from an hour ago.

Take a few minutes. There's nothing more to find here, only more to see.

When you're ready, follow the exit signs out to the garden side of the building. The last stop is waiting on the footbridge.

## Closing

### 055 · Into the Tuileries

> Outside, on or just beyond the footbridge from the museum's garden-side exit, with the Tuileries garden in front of you and the building behind.

Remember the winter coat for orange trees? You're standing at its back. For most of its life, this garden side was very nearly a blank wall. Only recently was it given a door, the one you've just used, and the footbridge under your feet.

Turn and look along the length of the building. You heard at the start about two panels, promised for Victory Day. This is how far that promise grew. The museum calls the gift a monument to peace.

Now turn the other way. Around you is the Tuileries garden. It goes back to Catherine de' Medici in the sixteenth century, and the great garden designer Le Nôtre reshaped it in the seventeenth.

Inside, you spent time with Monet's water and Monet's sky. Out here, you have the real ones. Walk slowly if you can, and give the garden the same patience you gave Monet's walls. Watch a cloud cross the sun, or the wind turn the leaves, and see how the colours shift. After the Water Lilies, you've had some practice. The light out here may never look quite ordinary again.

Thank you for spending this time with me, with Paul Guillaume's painters, and with Monet. You've met a dealer who ended up inside a painting he ordered, the widow who reshaped his collection, and an old painter who gave his water lilies away. Whatever stays with you from the faces downstairs, I hope you keep a little of that light.

Enjoy the garden, and take your time out here.
