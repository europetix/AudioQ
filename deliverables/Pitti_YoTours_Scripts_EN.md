## 001 · Piazza Pitti — Before you go in

*On Piazza Pitti, facing the palace. The ticket office is on the right-hand side of the façade; the way in is the central portal into the Ammannati Courtyard, where the main staircase and the lifts are on the right-hand side.*

This enormous house was built for a man who wasn't a prince. Luca Pitti was a Florentine banker, and in the middle of the fourteen-hundreds he began a palace here, on the slope below the hills. Florentines have long told the story that he meant to outshine the Medici. Some say Brunelleschi drew the first design. Nobody can prove either.

Here's what is certain. Within a century the Pitti family had run short of money, and they sold the house to the very family it was meant to outshine. Around 1550 the Medici moved their court in. And from then until 1919, it belonged to whoever ruled Tuscany. The Medici first, then the Habsburg-Lorraine grand dukes, and finally the kings of a united Italy. With a short Napoleonic interruption in the middle.

Take a moment with the front before you go in. More than two hundred metres of rough, oversized stone, and the same round arch repeated across the whole width. It was built to look as if nothing could ever move it.

Inside, the museums are stacked by floor. One floor up is the Palatine Gallery, the Medici paintings in the old state rooms. The museum calls it the greatest concentration of Raphael's work anywhere in the world. The Imperial and Royal Apartments are on the same floor. They're seen on a short visit led by museum staff, starting from the Palatine entrance hall. This guide takes you there on your way back down from the second floor, with a track to play while you wait. On the second floor are the Gallery of Modern Art and the Museum of Fashion and Costume. The Russian icons and the Palatine Chapel open off the courtyard on the ground floor. And the gardens behind the palace have a section of their own, so you can walk them first or last.

Once you're through the central doorway, you'll be in the Ammannati Courtyard. The main staircase and the lifts are on its right-hand side. Go up to the first floor, to the entrance of the Palatine Gallery. We'll start again there, in the first room.

## 002 · Galleria delle Statue — The first rooms of the Palatine Gallery

*In the first rooms of the Palatine Gallery on the first floor: the antechamber, then the long Gallery of the Statues that leads to the room marked Sala del Castagnoli (the Castagnoli Room).*

Almost every painting in the rooms ahead once belonged to the Medici. But it wasn't the Medici who hung them here. It was the family that came after them.

The Habsburg-Lorraine grand dukes created this gallery between the end of the seventeen-hundreds and the early eighteen-hundreds. They chose around five hundred works from the great Medici collections and put them in the palace's state rooms. And they hung them in a deliberately old-fashioned way, the way picture galleries were hung in the sixteen-hundreds. Frame against frame, wall to wall, with sculpture, vases and stone-inlaid tables in between.

So don't expect one painting on a white wall. Here the rooms are part of the show. You're meant to feel the richness first, and find the masterpieces second.

One tip before you start. Every room has its name written above the door, in Italian. Sala di Saturno, Sala di Giove, and so on. Every track in this part of the guide starts with that same name. So if you ever lose your place, look up at the door, and play the tracks that carry its name. The route keeps the five great Planet Rooms, with ceilings by Pietro da Cortona, for the very end.

Walk the length of the statue gallery to the room marked Sala del Castagnoli, with a great round stone table in the middle. Play its track there.

## 003 · Sala del Castagnoli — The Castagnoli Room

*In the room marked Sala del Castagnoli (the Castagnoli Room), at the end of the Gallery of the Statues: the room with the large round inlaid-stone table at its centre.*

This room is a crossroads, and it sits between two very different worlds of the palace.

One way lies the Volterrano Wing, a run of smaller rooms that was once the private apartment of a grand duchess, Vittoria della Rovere, in the sixteen-hundreds. Far ahead, at the very end of the route, are the state rooms of her husband, Grand Duke Ferdinando the Second. Those are the Planet Rooms, which Pietro da Cortona frescoed for him in the 1640s. Private life on one side, power and ceremony on the other. And this room in between.

It takes its name from the painter Giuseppe Castagnoli, who decorated these walls in the early eighteen-hundreds. That was when the Habsburg-Lorraine grand dukes were turning the old palace rooms into a picture gallery. He isn't the reason anyone comes in here. The reason is standing in the middle of the floor.

The Table of the Muses has been in this room since 1855. It's one of the great showpieces of Florentine stone inlay, and it took around twenty years, and a small army of craftsmen, to make. Before you walk up to it, look at it from a few steps back. At this distance it reads like a painting. Up close, you'll see it's something much stranger.

Walk up to the table and play its track there.

## 004 · Sala del Castagnoli · The Table of the Muses

*Beside the round table in the centre of the room marked Sala del Castagnoli (the Castagnoli Room). Walk around it slowly, without touching or leaning on it.*

There isn't a drop of paint on this tabletop. Every colour you see is the natural colour of a stone.

The technique is called commesso fiorentino, Florentine mosaic. Craftsmen sawed hard stones into thin slices and cut each slice to the shape of a petal, a leaf or a fold of cloth. Then they fitted the pieces together so tightly that the joins almost disappear. The Medici took this art so seriously that, back in the fifteen-hundreds, they set up a court workshop just for it, the Opificio delle Pietre Dure. This table comes from the far end of that long story.

Work on the top began in the 1830s, under Aristodemo Costoli and Giovan Battista Giorgi. It was not a quick job. The bronze base came later still, in the 1850s. It was modelled by a rising sculptor from Siena, Giovanni Dupré, and cast by Clemente Papi. In 1855 the finished table was set up in this room. Four names on the record, and many more hands that nobody wrote down.

Now look closely. The centre is usually described as Apollo with the Muses, the goddesses of the arts, who give the table its name. Follow the changes of colour inside a single shape, and you can watch the craftsman choosing stone the way a painter mixes paint. A vein in the stone becomes a fold. A darker patch becomes a shadow.

Then look down. The bronze base carries the Seasons, with small children among them. They're worth crouching for, if your knees agree.

By the time this table was finished, the last of the Medici had been dead for more than a century. Florentine stone inlay was still making wonders for the family that came next.

From here the museum's route may lead you through a string of smaller rooms, the Volterrano rooms and the Music Room, before it reaches the room marked Sala di Prometeo above the door. There, look for Filippo Lippi's large round panel, with tiny figures moving through rooms behind the Virgin, and play its track in front of it.

## 005 · Sala di Prometeo · Filippo Lippi — The Bartolini Tondo

*In the Sala di Prometeo, in front of Filippo Lippi's large round panel. Stand close enough to see the small scenes in the background.*

The man who painted this was a friar. Filippo Lippi belonged to the Carmelite order, and he was one of the great storytellers of early Renaissance Florence. Around 1452 he painted this round panel for Leonardo Bartolini, probably to hang in his family's house.

A few years later, this same friar would run off with a young nun, Lucrezia Buti. Their son, Filippino, grew up to be a great painter in his own right. But all that was still ahead of him. What he made here is a calm, devout picture of a family.

Start in front. The Virgin sits on a throne with the Child on her knees. She's picking seeds out of a pomegranate and holding them out to him. A pomegranate, packed with seeds, stood for fertility. It also hinted at the suffering that lay ahead of him.

Now let your eye travel back, into the rooms behind her. On the left is the birth of the Virgin herself. Her mother, Saint Anne, lies in bed, and women bustle around to care for her and the newborn, and to bring gifts. This is a fifteenth-century Florentine bedroom. It's how a wealthy family really celebrated a birth.

On the right, up on a staircase, Anne meets her husband, Joachim. Tradition linked that meeting to the conception of the Virgin.

So look at what Lippi has done. There are three moments in one circle. The Virgin's parents meeting. The Virgin being born. And the Virgin, grown up, holding her own son. A generation after Brunelleschi taught Florence how to build space with perspective, Lippi uses those receding rooms as little stages, one story to each.

The deeper you look into the painting, the further back in time you go.

Stay in the Sala di Prometeo and look for a Raphael: a young woman against a black background, one hand resting on her belly. That's La Gravida. If she isn't on these walls, she has gone back to her old place in the Sala dell'Iliade, so keep her track for that room, and walk on through a few smaller rooms to the Sala di Ulisse.

## 006 · Sala di Prometeo · Raphael — La Gravida

*In the Sala di Prometeo, in front of Raphael's half-length portrait of a young woman against a black background, one hand on her belly and the other holding a glove. The museum's page lists her here; she used to hang in the Sala dell'Iliade, so if she isn't here, play this track there.*

She's been called La Gravida, the pregnant woman, since the nineteenth century. We don't actually know that she was.

Look at her hands. One rests on a ledge, holding a glove, and the other rests on her rounded belly. That single gesture is where her name comes from. Some nineteenth-century viewers looked at it and decided she was expecting a child, and the title stuck. Maybe she was, or maybe they simply saw what they expected to see. The painting doesn't say.

Nor does it tell us who she is. Raphael painted her around fifteen-oh-seven, in the last years of his time in Florence, when he was in his mid-twenties. Scholars have suggested names over the years: a woman of the Bufalini family, perhaps, or Emilia Pia, a daughter of the Pio family. None of the ideas has held up, and for now, as the museum puts it, she's still a mystery.

What she wore, we know exactly. A white gown, a yellow bodice with a black sash, and wide sleeves of red damask. Her hair is caught up in a tightly woven net. There are rings on her fingers, and a gold chain whose pendant slips out of sight into her neckline. This is a well-off woman, dressed with care for an important picture.

And then there's her face. She's set against a plain black ground, with no landscape, no symbols, no story to hide behind. Her look is calm and direct. She knows she's being looked at, and she looks straight back, and Raphael lets that be enough.

So here she is, five centuries on. Raphael gave her everything except a name of her own. Later viewers gave her one, and it may not even be true.

If you're in the Sala di Prometeo, walk on through a few smaller rooms to the room marked Sala di Ulisse. Look for Raphael's Madonna dell'Impannata, a Virgin and Child with two women saints and a softly lit window behind them, and play its track there. If you're listening in the Sala dell'Iliade, carry on with that room's tracks.

## 007 · Sala di Ulisse · Raphael — Madonna dell'Impannata

*In the Sala di Ulisse, in front of Raphael's Madonna dell'Impannata. Stand back far enough to see the whole group and the window behind it.*

This painting is named after a window. Look past the figures, through the archway behind them. That softly glowing pane is an impannata, a window covered not with glass but with a translucent screen. It lets in a gentle, milky light, and it gave the picture its name.

Raphael painted it in Rome around 1511, in his first years there, while he was busy with his great Vatican frescoes. It was ordered by Bindo Altoviti, a Florentine banker with close ties to the papal court. It was probably made for his second marriage, to Fiammetta Soderini. The painting was sent home to Florence and hung in the Altoviti palace for more than thirty years.

Then politics caught up with it. Bindo sided with the enemies of the Medici, and Duke Cosimo seized his property in Tuscany. In 1554 the painting became Medici property. In Florence, backing the wrong side could cost you your art collection.

Now the detective story. X-rays taken last century showed that Raphael first planned a bearded Saint Joseph in this group, and then painted him out. And after a recent restoration, some scholars believe Raphael finished only the two women saints himself. The Virgin and the two children were most likely completed by his pupils.

So look again with that in mind. The older woman is Saint Elizabeth. The younger one, perhaps Catherine or Mary Magdalene, reaches in to touch the Child's side. Down below, the infant John sits with a leopard skin, and on the left hangs a green curtain.

Compare the two saints' faces with the Virgin's. If the restorers are right, you're seeing the master's hand right beside his students'. Can you tell the difference? It's harder than it sounds.

Next, walk through a tiny marble room, known as Napoleon's Bathroom, into the room marked Sala dell'Educazione di Giove. Look for a young woman in a shimmering dress, calm as anything, holding a man's severed head by the hair. That's Cristofano Allori's Judith. Play its track in front of it.

## 008 · Sala dell'Educazione di Giove · Cristofano Allori — Judith with the Head of Holofernes

*In the Sala dell'Educazione di Giove, in front of Allori's Judith. Start close, then take a step back.*

The severed head in this painting has the painter's own face.

Cristofano Allori painted himself as Holofernes, the enemy general whom Judith killed to save her city. And he gave Judith the face of his lover, a woman Florentines knew as la Mazzafirra. He finished it around 1610 to 1612, and he poured his own life into it.

The story told in Florence was that she had left him, and that this painting was his confession. This is what loving her cost me. It's a wonderful piece of gossip, and we can't prove it. But the faces are not in doubt. He chose to put himself in the dead man's place.

Now step back and look at how it works. At first the picture seems almost serene. A beautiful young woman stands upright and calm, looking out past you. An old servant waits behind her. Then your eye drops to her lowered hand, and you see what she's holding by the hair.

Look at her clothes. That shimmering, extravagant silk isn't only Allori showing off. It's a tribute to the thing Florence was richest in. The city's fortune was built on cloth, and fabric painted this convincingly was a kind of civic boast.

There's one more thread to follow. Allori learned from Caravaggio's dramatic light, and that lesson reached him partly through Artemisia Gentileschi. She was painting her own Judiths for the Medici in these same years. One of them hangs in the Iliad Room, a little further along this route. Her Judith and his are close cousins.

The painting reached the Medici only after Allori's death. Four centuries on, she still holds his head, and she is still perfectly calm.

Caravaggio's Sleeping Cupid usually hangs in this room as well. Look for a small, dark canvas of a sleeping winged child. Its track starts in front of it.

## 009 · Sala dell'Educazione di Giove · Caravaggio — Sleeping Cupid

*Usually hung in the Sala dell'Educazione di Giove, near Allori's Judith; if it is not on the wall, ask the room staff where it is currently shown. Stand close.*

This little painting reached Florence while the man who painted it was on the run.

Caravaggio painted it in 1608, on the island of Malta. Two years earlier he had killed a man in Rome and fled with a price on his head. In Malta he was welcomed and admitted to the Knights of Malta. One of his fellow knights was a Florentine, Fra Francesco dell'Antella, and this Cupid was painted for him.

Then everything went wrong again. Caravaggio was thrown into prison there, escaped to Sicily, and went back to life as a fugitive. Meanwhile, by the summer of 1609, dell'Antella had sent the painting home to his brother's house in Florence. It caused a stir. Poets wrote sonnets about it. So while Florence admired his Cupid, Caravaggio was still on the run in the south. He died the following year, at Porto Ercole on the Tuscan coast. The Medici acquired the painting later in the century.

Now look closely, because this is not quite the sweet picture its title promises. The subject is an old one, Love asleep, his weapons laid aside. But this child is too thin. You can see his ribs. His skin is pale, almost grey, with a greenish tinge in the shadows. His mouth hangs open and his head has fallen back. Some have wondered whether Caravaggio painted a sick child, or even a dead one. Nobody knows.

Look at the light. One hard beam picks out his face and chest, and the rest falls away into darkness. This is Caravaggio's great trick, the one that changed European painting, here in a single quiet image.

Love, exhausted and defenceless. Standing where you are, you're close enough to wake him.

The Sala della Stufa is next on the route. Its track starts there.

## 010 · Sala della Stufa — The Grand Duke's Hot Room

*In the Sala della Stufa, the small room after the Sala dell'Educazione di Giove, just before the door to the atrium of the Moor's Staircase and the Sala dell'Iliade. Stand in the middle so you can turn to each wall.*

This was the Grand Duke's private hot room.

It began as an open gallery. In the seventeenth century it was closed in and turned into a stufa, a bath heated the way the ancient Romans heated theirs. It was reserved for Grand Duke Ferdinando the Second, beside his bedroom.

To decorate it, he brought in Pietro da Cortona, the painter of the Planet Room ceilings. This is where their partnership began. Cortona painted the Four Ages of Man, from Ovid, one on each wall. The Golden and Silver Ages came first, in 1637. He returned for the Bronze and Iron Ages in 1641.

Start with the Golden Age, the happiest of the four. People live in peace, the earth gives freely, and nobody carries a weapon. It's flattery, of course. The scene celebrates Ferdinando's peaceful rule, and his marriage that same year to Vittoria della Rovere. Her family name means "of the oak", and her family's heraldry is worked into this happy world.

Then turn through the other ages and watch it all go wrong. By the Bronze and Iron Ages, war has arrived. These walls brought a lighter, more theatrical style of painting to Florence. Ferdinando liked what he saw. Soon Cortona was at work on the ceilings of the state rooms.

The route continues through the door into the atrium of the Moor's Staircase, and on into the room marked Sala dell'Iliade. Its track starts there. If it's closed, staff will send you back the way you came, to the Sala di Giove and then the Sala di Saturno. Skip the Iliad tracks, and in each room play the tracks named on its door.

## 011 · Sala dell'Iliade — The Room of the Iliad

*In the Sala dell'Iliade, entered from the Sala della Stufa through the atrium of the Moor's Staircase. Stand near the marble group in the centre and look up at the ceiling.*

Here's a puzzle. This room is named after the most famous war in literature. Yet under the Medici, this was no room for heroes or ambassadors.

In the grand dukes' day, important guests walked through the Planet Rooms the other way round. They started at Venus, and the sequence ended next door, in Saturn, the first of the Grand Duke's own private rooms. So this room lay beyond it, on the private side of the court. One account even says it held a billiard table.

The war came later. A few years after Napoleon's fall, Luigi Sabatelli painted the ceiling for the Lorraine grand dukes, who had taken over from the Medici. Look up, and the story of Troy runs right across the vault. Below it, the walls are hung two and three deep, Florentine fashion, the big pictures centred and the smaller ones fitted around them.

In the middle stands the calmest thing in the room. It's Lorenzo Bartolini's Charity, a marble mother with her children. She has stood here since eighteen sixty-one.

Three works here have their own track. Andrea del Sarto's great Assumption of the Virgin. Bartolini's Charity. And Artemisia Gentileschi's Judith and her Maidservant. Artemisia's Mary Magdalene hangs in this room as well.

Start with the great altarpiece of the Virgin rising into heaven above a crowd of apostles. That's Andrea del Sarto's Assumption.

## 012 · Sala dell'Iliade · Andrea del Sarto — The Passerini Assumption

*In the Sala dell'Iliade, in front of the tall altarpiece of the Virgin rising above the apostles around her tomb. Step back far enough to take in both halves.*

This altarpiece is almost four metres tall. And two of the most important figures in it don't belong in the story at all.

The story is the Assumption. At the end of her earthly life, the Virgin Mary is taken up into heaven, body and soul. Christians celebrated that belief for many centuries, though it only became official Catholic dogma in nineteen fifty. Andrea del Sarto paints it the classic way, on two levels. Above, Mary rises on a cloud among angels. Below, the apostles crowd around her empty tomb, some looking up, some looking at one another.

Now bring your eyes down to the front. Two figures kneel there, the closest of all to you. One is a bishop in rich vestments, with three golden balls. That's Saint Nicholas. The other is a woman in a plain habit, dressed as a Franciscan lay sister. That's Saint Margaret. Neither of them was at the tomb.

They're here because of who paid. The altarpiece was ordered by a woman named Margherita Passerini, for the high altar of Santa Maria dei Servi, a church in Cortona. Her father was called Nicholas, and so was her son. And Margaret is simply Margherita in English. So in the middle of a miracle, in the best seats, kneels her family.

Andrea del Sarto painted this in the late fifteen-twenties, only a few years before his death. Vasari, who wrote the first great book of artists' lives, called him the painter without errors. Some hear a hint of criticism in that: too smooth, perhaps, too correct. Look at the faces and the falling cloth, and judge for yourself.

More than a century later, in sixteen thirty-nine, one of Margherita's heirs sold it to Grand Duke Ferdinando the Second, who sent it here to the Pitti.

So, heaven at the top, and the donor's family at the bottom. That's how Renaissance altarpieces worked, and Andrea makes it look effortless.

Next, the marble mother and children in the middle of the room. That's Lorenzo Bartolini's Charity.

## 013 · Sala dell'Iliade · Lorenzo Bartolini — Charity

*In the Sala dell'Iliade, beside the white marble group at the centre of the room. Walk round it until you find the scroll in a child's hands.*

At first sight, she's the gentlest thing in this room. A young mother, her children, smooth white marble. But this sculpture is making an argument. And the argument is written down.

Lorenzo Bartolini was born in a village near Prato and trained partly in Paris. In his day, the grand style for marble was Canova's: ideal bodies, cool and perfect, modelled on ancient statues. Bartolini went looking for something more natural. He studied real bodies, and he studied the Florentine Renaissance, and he tried to bring the two together.

He designed this group for the chapel of the grand-ducal villa at Poggio Imperiale, and worked on the marble for years afterwards. It was still in his studio in eighteen thirty-five. Look at how human she is. She isn't a goddess on a pedestal. She's a mother busy with her children.

Charity was an old subject. Traditionally she's a woman surrounded by children, feeding them, sheltering them, giving. Bartolini gives her something else to do. Walk round until you find the scroll in a child's hands. It's in Latin, and it's a line from the Gospels. Love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul and with all your mind, and your neighbour as yourself.

This mother is making her child study. Charity, for Bartolini, means feeding the mind and the conscience as well as the body. Scholars call her Charity the Educator, partly because she appeared on the title page of a magazine called The Educator.

She has stood in this room since eighteen sixty-one. And she fits it rather well. Up on the ceilings of the Planet Rooms ahead, painters spent years showing a prince being educated by the gods. Down here, at eye level, a mother is quietly doing the same job.

One more work in this room, and it's a hard turn from this quiet mother. Look for Artemisia Gentileschi's Judith and her Maidservant: two women in the dark, a severed head, a sword.

## 014 · Sala dell'Iliade · Artemisia Gentileschi — Judith and her Maidservant

*In front of Artemisia Gentileschi's Judith and her Maidservant, two women in a dark tent with a sword and a basket. It hangs here in the Sala dell'Iliade.*

Most painters who took on this story painted the killing itself, the sword coming down. Artemisia Gentileschi paints the minute after.

In the old story, the widow Judith slips into an enemy camp and beheads its general, Holofernes, to save her town. Here she and her maid, Abra, are still inside his tent, in the dark. His head is half-bundled into a basket between them. And they've both stopped, turned toward something off to one side, a sound we can't hear. You're watching two women a heartbeat from being caught.

Look at the sword. Holofernes' own heavy blade rests back against Judith's shoulder, its edge close to her bare neck. It's a reminder of exactly what she's just done, and of what will happen to her if the camp wakes. But look at her grip, and at her face. This isn't panic, it's control. The light is Caravaggio's kind of light, one hard source and deep shadow, with the two faces lifted out of the black. The steadiness, though, is Artemisia's own.

So is the partnership. Judith's hand rests on Abra's shoulder. Painters often made the maid old, or frightened, or barely there. Not here, though. Abra isn't simply a servant doing a job. She's an accomplice, and the two women hold the danger between them.

It's worth knowing who painted it. Artemisia came to Florence from Rome as a very young woman. She became the first woman admitted to the city's Academy of Drawing, in a profession that barely let women through the door. She worked for Grand Duke Cosimo the Second. She came back to Judith again and again across her life. The Uffizi has her version of the beheading itself.

This is the quieter, tenser sequel, where nobody screams and nobody moves. They just listen.

If this painting isn't on the walls when you visit, it may be on loan. Either way, walk on to the room marked Sala di Saturno, the first of the Planet Rooms, and play its track when you're inside.

## 015 · Sala di Saturno — The Room of Raphael

*In the Sala di Saturno, the first of the five Planet Rooms on the normal route. Stand in the middle of the room, where you can see the ceiling and the Raphaels on the walls.*

If you had time for only one room in this palace, many people would choose this one. In here, you can watch Raphael grow up.

He came to Florence as a young man in his early twenties, and some of the portraits and Madonnas he made there hang on these walls. So do pictures he painted later in Rome, as the pope's own painter. Raphael died at thirty-seven. A great deal of that short career fits into this one room.

The pictures arrived by many routes. A Medici pope's commission. A grand duke's purchase. An altarpiece bought for a chapel in a small Tuscan cathedral, and later bought again by a Medici prince.

Now look up. The ceiling is the work of Ciro Ferri, a pupil of Pietro da Cortona. It finishes a story that runs through all five Planet Rooms. Each room stands for one age in the life of an ideal prince. Turning away from pleasure in Venus. Learning in Apollo. War in Mars. Good government in Jupiter. And here, at the end, old age. The prince, his earthly life nearly over, is led up into the heavens. Saturn, the god of Time, waits for him, and Fame stands ready with a crown.

Under the Medici, guests began at Venus, and the sequence ended here, in Saturn, the first of the Grand Duke's private rooms, just beyond the throne room. Today the route runs the other way, so you meet old age first and finish with youth and Venus.

Five tracks in this room are given to Raphael. Start with the round painting of a mother hugging her child, the Madonna della Seggiola.

## 016 · Sala di Saturno · Raphael — Madonna della Seggiola

*In the Sala di Saturno, in front of the round painting of the Virgin hugging her child, with the young Saint John at the right edge.*

There's a lovely story about this painting. Raphael is walking through Rome when he sees a peasant woman in a doorway, sitting on a low stool with her child in her arms. And he paints exactly what he saw. It's a charming legend, and almost certainly invented. The chair gives it away.

You only glimpse the chair, an upright decorated with round knobs. And it's no peasant's stool. It's a chamber chair, the kind reserved for high dignitaries of the papal court, and those knobs may be a nod to the balls on the Medici coat of arms. Many think the painting was made for the Medici pope, Leo the Tenth. Raphael painted it around fifteen twelve, in his Roman years.

So she's probably not a woman from a doorway. But look at how Raphael makes her feel like one. Mary lifts one knee to hold her son and clasps him against her chest. Her cheek rests against his temple, yet her eyes turn out to you. On the right, the young Saint John joins his hands in prayer and gazes at them. Their arms lock together, and the three bodies follow the round edge with perfect ease.

Now the colour. A white turban shot with gold thread, and a scarf of greens and reds. A red sleeve against the Child's yellow tunic, and the deep ultramarine of her dress. And in the glances, a little melancholy, as if she already knows what will happen to this child.

By fifteen eighty-nine it hung in the Tribune of the Uffizi. Later, the Grand Prince Ferdinando brought it to the Pitti and kept it in his bedroom. Napoleon's armies took it to Paris, and it came home after his fall. It became one of Raphael's most copied paintings.

The legend gets one thing right. Whoever she was, she doesn't look like a queen of heaven. She looks like a mother, holding on.

Next, look for the smallest Raphael in the room, a little panel of God the Father in a blaze of light. That's Ezekiel's Vision.

## 017 · Sala di Saturno · Raphael — Ezekiel's Vision

*In the Sala di Saturno, in front of the smallest Raphael in the room: a little panel, about the size of a sheet of paper, with God the Father in a blaze of light above a band of clouds and a wide landscape below.*

Of all the Raphaels in this room, this is the one you have to lean in for. It's about the size of a sheet of paper.

Raphael painted it in Rome around 1517, at the busiest point of his career. The subject comes from the Old Testament prophet Ezekiel, who saw the heavens open. Vasari described it as a Christ painted like Jupiter in the sky, surrounded by the four Evangelists, just as Ezekiel describes them.

Now look at the centre. God the Father floats with his arms raised in blessing. He's held up by two small angels, and by the creatures that stand for the four Gospel writers. The eagle is John. The ox is Luke. The lion is Mark. And the figure in the pale blue-violet cloak is Matthew, who is always shown as an angel.

Behind them the sky has burst open into a hot, glowing light. Look closely at that glow. It's full of tiny angel heads, some painted in a single colour and some only scratched into the surface, so the whole circle seems to shimmer.

Then drop your eyes below the clouds. There's a whole world down there, land and sea running away into the distance, with flashes of light and shadow. On the left, a ray of light falls on two tiny figures beside a horse. One of them is usually taken to be Ezekiel himself, the man who is seeing all this.

For a long time, some scholars doubted that Raphael painted it. His workshop was full of gifted assistants by then, and Giulio Romano or Penni were suggested instead. But the museum argues that the quality is simply too high. Huge figures, a vast space, all squeezed into this small panel, and painted with such soft changes of colour.

The same vision was woven into the canopy over the bed of state of the Medici pope, Leo the Tenth. Here, though, you can hold the whole sky in a single glance.

Next, look for a small, very dark painting of the Virgin standing with her child in her arms. That's the Madonna del Granduca.

## 018 · Sala di Saturno · Raphael — Madonna del Granduca

*In the Sala di Saturno, in front of the small, dark painting of the Virgin standing with the Christ Child in her arms against a black background.*

The darkness around this Madonna is the most famous thing about her. And Raphael didn't paint it.

He painted her around fifteen-oh-six, during his years in Florence, around the same time as La Gravida, back in the Sala di Prometeo. He was in his early twenties and learning fast. From Leonardo he took the soft play of light and shadow. From the Florentine sculptors before him, Donatello and Luca della Robbia among them, he took something else: their countless Madonnas in terracotta and marble. You can feel that in her, almost a statue coming alive.

Then there's the black. X-rays show that Raphael set her in a room, with a pillar holding up arches, and a glimpse of countryside beyond. At some later date, someone painted all of that out. What's left is a mother and child alone in the dark.

Look at what that does. Her eyes are lowered, and she seems sad. The child looks calmly out at you. The museum reads it as love, together with a painful awareness of what lies ahead for him. Without the room around them, there's nowhere else for your eyes to go. It's an accident of history, but the accident is beautiful.

The name came much later. Around eighteen hundred, Ferdinand the Third, Grand Duke of Tuscany, authorised her purchase, and she came to the Pitti. He loved her. When Napoleon's armies drove him into exile, he took her with him. When he came back, he kept her in his private rooms, and she went on public view only when he was away. So she became the Madonna del Granduca, the Grand Duke's Madonna.

She has hung here in the Sala di Saturno since eighteen eighty-two.

Next, the biggest Raphael in the room: the Madonna del Baldacchino, the Virgin enthroned beneath a great canopy.

## 019 · Sala di Saturno · Raphael — Madonna del Baldacchino

*In the Sala di Saturno, in front of the largest Raphael in the room: the Virgin enthroned beneath a great canopy, with four saints and two small angels at her feet. Step back first to take in the whole painting.*

This is the painting Raphael walked away from.

He began it around fifteen-oh-seven, for the Dei family's chapel in the church of Santo Spirito, here in this same part of Florence. It was his only big altarpiece for the city. The Virgin sits enthroned beneath a great canopy, the baldacchino that gives the picture its name, with the Christ Child on her lap. Four saints stand around her: Peter and Bernard, James and Augustine. At the foot of the throne, two small angels read from a scroll.

Look at how calmly the figures fill the space. You can feel Raphael learning from the painters around him in Florence. Leonardo's soft modelling and his pyramid of figures. Fra Bartolomeo's grand, still architecture. He was in his mid-twenties, taking in everything the city could teach him.

And then he left. In fifteen-oh-eight, Pope Julius the Second called him to Rome, to paint the papal apartments. Raphael went, and the altarpiece stayed behind, unfinished. It never reached Santo Spirito.

Instead it began a long journey. Raphael's friend Baldassarre Turini bought it in Florence for his family's chapel in the cathedral of Pescia, a small town in Tuscany. Much later, in sixteen ninety-seven, the Medici Grand Prince Ferdinando bought it, and had the canvas enlarged with a strip along the top. Look up there and you're looking at the prince's addition, not Raphael's.

So this picture is a hinge. On one side is the young Raphael of the small Florentine pictures, like the Granduca you've just met. On the other is the Raphael who, within a few years, would be painting the School of Athens in the Vatican. The Baldacchino was among the last things he worked on in Florence, and his most ambitious Florentine picture. Stand in front of it, and you're standing at the turn.

Last in this room, look for a pair of matching portraits, a husband and his young wife. They're Agnolo and Maddalena Doni.

## 020 · Sala di Saturno · Raphael — Portraits of Agnolo and Maddalena Doni

*In the Sala di Saturno, in front of the pair of matching half-length portraits of a husband and wife. Look for the young woman's jewelled pendant.*

At the end of January, fifteen-oh-four, a Florentine wedding joined money to a great name. He was Agnolo Doni, a rich cloth merchant, about thirty years old. She was Maddalena Strozzi, from one of the noblest families in the city. She was about fourteen.

And Agnolo wanted portraits. He went to a young painter from Urbino who had recently arrived in town, and ordered a matching pair. Raphael painted them as a set, and they were once hinged together, so they opened and closed like a book.

Look at Maddalena first. Her pose may seem familiar. The museum points out links to Leonardo's Mona Lisa so close that Raphael seems to have studied it, here in Florence. But look at the difference. Leonardo melts every edge into soft haze. Raphael makes every shape and every colour sharp and clear.

Then look at her jewel. It's a gold pendant with a mount shaped like a unicorn, set with a ruby, an emerald and a sapphire, and finished with a pearl. On a bride of fourteen, that's quite a statement. Across from her is Agnolo, the merchant who ordered and paid for both panels.

Now the part that's easy to miss. The hinges once let people turn the panels and see the backs, because the backs are painted too. If they're on view, find them. On Agnolo's is the Flood. On Maddalena's is what came after, the survivors Deucalion and Pyrrha bringing humanity back to life. A disaster on his side, and a new beginning on hers. It's hard not to read that as a wish for a marriage.

More than three centuries later, in eighteen twenty-six, Grand Duke Leopold the Second bought the pair from the couple's heirs. They went into the picture gallery he was building here at the Pitti.

Next is the room marked Sala di Giove, the Room of Jupiter, through the doorway. Play its track there. If you've already been through it, walk back through it to the Sala di Marte.

## 021 · Sala di Giove — The Throne Room

*In the Sala di Giove, the Room of Jupiter, between the Saturn and Mars rooms. Stand near the middle and look up at the vault.*

Think of this room as the end of a very long walk. Under the Medici, an important visitor was led through one grand room after another, each a little richer than the last, until he arrived here. This was the throne room. The Grand Duke received his guests under a canopy of state, and if you look up at the vault, you can still see the hooks it hung from.

The ceiling was painted by Pietro da Cortona in the early 1640s, and it is pure flattery. Jupiter, king of the gods, is crowning a young prince, the perfect Medici heir, just stepping off the Argonauts' ship. Hercules has handed him his club. Victory is writing a letter on her shield. It's an M. For Medici, of course.

But the cleverest compliment is the one most people miss. Around Jupiter float four female figures. They're almost certainly the four moons of Jupiter. Galileo found them through his telescope in 1610, and he named them the Medicean Stars, to win the family's favour. It worked. Within months he was their court philosopher. So as far as the Medici were concerned, they didn't just own this palace. They owned a small piece of the sky.

Of everything on these walls, start with the woman in white silk. That's Raphael's Veiled Lady.

## 022 · Sala di Giove · Raphael — La Velata (The Veiled Lady)

*In the Sala di Giove, in front of Raphael's portrait of a woman in white silk with a veil over her hair.*

There's a mystery in this portrait, and it starts with a man who saw it almost five hundred years ago. Giorgio Vasari, the painter who wrote the first great book of artists' lives, saw her in a Florentine merchant's house. And he wrote that she was the woman Raphael loved all his life. Later tradition gave her a name. Margherita Luti, the baker's daughter. La Fornarina.

It's a wonderful story. And it may not be true.

Look at what she's wearing. That isn't a baker's daughter's dress. The white silk sleeves are slashed and gathered so the fine linen puffs through. There's gold thread on the bodice and a string of beads at her throat. This is a very expensive outfit, and many scholars read it as a commissioned portrait of a young noblewoman.

Then there's the veil, the detail that gives the painting its name. In Raphael's world, a veil over the hair meant one thing. She was married. Which makes the love story a little more complicated.

Here's what isn't in doubt. Raphael painted her in Rome, around 1512 to 1515, when he was about thirty and the most celebrated painter in the city. And a face very like hers turns up again in Rome, in his painting known as La Fornarina, where she's half undressed and wears an armband with Raphael's own name on it. Many see the same eyes and the same mouth. So whoever she was, she was probably more than just another client.

Now watch the silk. Raphael paints white on white, the dress, the veil, the linen, and never lets it go flat. Every fold catches a slightly different light. And her hand, resting on her chest, is half a gesture of modesty, and half the gesture of someone about to speak.

Five centuries on, we still can't be sure of her name. Raphael, I suspect, would have liked it that way.

Andrea del Sarto's young Saint John hangs in this room too. Play his track in front of him.

## 023 · Sala di Giove · Andrea del Sarto — Young Saint John the Baptist

*In the Sala di Giove, in front of Andrea del Sarto's young Saint John the Baptist: a youth half-wrapped in bright red drapery against a dark background, holding a scroll.*

This young saint was painted from a living model. And yet, when people look at him, many of them think of a statue.

Andrea del Sarto painted him around fifteen twenty-three, for a wealthy Florentine banker, Giovanni Benintendi. Benintendi wanted to honour Saint John and the sacrament of baptism. So he hired several painters to make pictures for a decorated wooden setting in the antechamber of his palace. Pontormo painted an Adoration of the Magi for it. Andrea's John was its centrepiece.

Tradition gave painters two ways to show John: a baby playing with the infant Christ, or the grown prophet of the desert, gaunt and wild-haired. Andrea chose neither. His John is a youth, lit hard against the dark, wrapped in bright red drapery. He holds a scroll with the Latin words Ecce Agnus Dei, Behold the Lamb of God. It's John announcing Christ, and pointing ahead to the cross.

Now look below him. Down there are the signs of his hard life in the wilderness. There's the simple terracotta bowl he used to baptise Jesus, a rock, and the camel hair he wore. And there's a humble cross made of cane.

Andrea liked to work from live models, and you can see it in the face. The museum also notes that he looks a little like Michelangelo's David. Look at the skin. Andrea was a master of warm flesh, the kind that looks as if blood is moving under it. Watch how the light moves over his face.

Thirty years later, in fifteen fifty-three, the painting was given to Duke Cosimo the First, and so it entered the Medici collections.

Next is the room marked Sala di Marte, the Room of Mars. Play its track there. If you haven't yet seen the Sala di Saturno, visit it first, then come back through here.

## 024 · Sala di Marte — The Room of Mars

*In the Sala di Marte, the third of the five Planet Rooms on today's route, entered from the Sala di Giove. Stand near the middle and look up at the ceiling first.*

If you've been reading the ceilings, here's a small surprise. You've been reading the story backwards.

Under the Medici, a guest crossed these five Planet Rooms the other way. He began in the Room of Venus, and the sequence ended at Saturn, the first of the Grand Duke's private rooms. The ceilings told a young prince's education in that order. First pleasure, then learning, then war, then the crown. So you've just left the crowned prince in the throne room, and stepped back onto the battlefield.

Look up. Pietro da Cortona painted this ceiling in the mid-sixteen-forties, and here the prince learns to fight. Around the edges, a sea battle rages. Mars watches over it, and lights the young prince with his star to give him strength. Then comes the reward. Hercules builds a trophy out of the enemy's armour, and a line of prisoners is led toward three calm figures. They are Victory, Plenty and Peace. And in the middle, the Medici coat of arms, crowned with the name of the man who paid for it, Ferdinando the Second.

One of Peter Paul Rubens's greatest paintings usually hangs here, and it is no victory parade. Rubens was a Flemish painter, and a working diplomat too. This picture is war itself, with no glory left in it.

Look for the large canvas of an armoured Mars striding forward, a woman clinging to him, and figures fallen under his feet. It's called The Consequences of War. Play its track in front of it.

## 025 · Sala di Marte · Rubens — The Consequences of War

*In the Sala di Marte, in front of the large Rubens allegory: an armoured Mars striding forward, a woman clinging to him, figures fallen underfoot. The painting normally hangs in the Sala di Marte; if it isn't here, ask an attendant.*

Most paintings leave us guessing what the artist meant. For this one, we have his explanation, in his own words.

It began with a favour between friends. Justus Sustermans was a Flemish painter working in Florence as court painter to the Medici. In sixteen thirty-seven he asked Rubens for a painting. Rubens sent it with a letter, dated the twelfth of March sixteen thirty-eight. In it he went through the canvas figure by figure, telling his friend exactly what each one meant. Very few paintings come with the painter's own key.

So let's read it with him. At the centre, Mars strides out in armour, sword raised. Venus, the goddess of love, clings to him and tries to hold him back. He's tearing himself away. Ahead of him, the Fury Alecto urges him on with her torch.

Now look down, under their feet. There's a mother with a child at her breast, an architect with his instruments, and a woman with a broken lute. Family, building, music. Everything that peace makes possible, trampled.

On the far left, a woman in black raises her arms to the sky. That's Europe, in mourning. Near her stands the temple of Janus, with its doors flung open. In ancient Rome those doors were shut in peacetime, and opened for war.

Rubens knew exactly which war he meant. The Thirty Years' War had been tearing central Europe apart for almost twenty years. He had spent years as a diplomat, working for peace. Now, near the end of his life, he made the argument in paint instead.

And here's the part people often get wrong. This was not a gift to a grand duke. It was a painter's picture, made for a fellow painter. It only entered the Medici collections in sixteen ninety-one, when Grand Prince Ferdinando bought it.

War isn't glorious here. It's simply what the painting shows.

Next, through the doorway, is the room marked Sala di Apollo, the Room of Apollo. Play its track there.

## 026 · Sala di Apollo — The Room of Apollo

*In the Sala di Apollo, the fourth Planet Room on today's route, entered from the Sala di Marte. The Sala di Venere, the last room, is beyond it.*

Walking backwards through the story has its advantages. You've just come off the battlefield. Now you step into the schoolroom.

In the Medici version of the tale, this was the second stage of the young prince's education. He had met Venus and learned to resist the pleasures of the senses. Here, Apollo took him in hand. Apollo, god of poetry, music and light, was his teacher. This is where the prince learned reason, letters and the arts, before he was sent next door to learn war.

Look up at the ceiling, because it hides a small drama of its own. Pietro da Cortona began it. But in sixteen forty-seven he left Florence, with the work unfinished. It was his loyal pupil, Ciro Ferri, who came back and finished it, around sixteen-sixty. Some people say they can see the seam. Ferri's figures are a touch harder, they say, and his colours a little cooler. See if you can spot it. Either way, the ceiling reads as one.

So one room, two painters, and years between the first brushstroke and the last. Teacher and pupil, finishing the lesson together. That seems about right for the room of learning.

The most famous resident on these walls is a woman wrapped in a cloak of her own copper-gold hair, her eyes raised and full of tears. She's Titian's Mary Magdalene. Find her first, and play her track in front of her.

## 027 · Sala di Apollo · Titian — The Penitent Magdalene

*In the Sala di Apollo, in front of Titian's half-length Magdalene: a woman covered by her long copper-gold hair, looking upward.*

She's crying. Come close and you'll see the tears, caught in her eyes, painted as clear as glass.

In the Gospels, Mary Magdalene is the first witness to the Resurrection. Over the centuries, legend added a great deal more. It merged her with the sinful woman who anointed Christ's feet. It said she spent her last years as a hermit in a cave in France, doing penance, with only her long hair to cover her. Almost none of that is in the Bible. But by Titian's day, the picture was fixed. The penitent Magdalene, her hair her only garment, her eyes turned to heaven.

Titian probably painted this one in Venice in the early fifteen-thirties, for Francesco Maria della Rovere, the Duke of Urbino. Look at what he does with the hair. It falls over her shoulders in a cloak of copper and gold, catching the light as it goes. And down at the bottom left stands a jar, the ointment jar from the anointing story. Look at it closely. Titian signed his name on its rim.

Now, the obvious thing. The hair is there to cover her, and that's what makes this a devotional picture. It's also exactly what makes us look. Titian knew that perfectly well. Penitence and beauty, in the same brushstroke.

It was a hit. When the writer Giorgio Vasari saw it at the court of Urbino in fifteen forty-eight, he called it a rare thing. Titian and his workshop copied it again and again. This version is generally considered the earliest of the type.

And here's how it reached Florence. In sixteen thirty-one, it came with the inheritance of Vittoria della Rovere, part of the great Urbino collection. Keep that name in mind. You'll meet it again in the next room.

The room marked Sala di Venere is through the doorway ahead, the last room on today's route. Play its track there.

## 028 · Sala di Venere — The Room of Venus

*In the Sala di Venere, the last room of today's Palatine route and the first room of the old Medici sequence. Canova's marble Venus stands in the centre.*

You've reached the last room of today's route. Under the Medici, this was where it all began.

You've walked the prince's life backwards. And now, at last, you've reached the beginning.

In the Medici story, this was the very first lesson. Before a young prince could be taught by Apollo, hardened by Mars and crowned by Jupiter, he had to learn to turn away from Venus. From comfort, and from the pleasures of the senses. Look up. Pietro da Cortona built this ceiling around that idea.

Which makes this a slightly mischievous room to finish in. Because the walls are full of exactly the kind of beauty the prince was meant to resist.

Four works here get their own track. At the centre stands a marble Venus by Antonio Canova. And around the walls hang three paintings by Titian, made across some thirty-five years of his life. There's a young man's picture, the Concert. There's a portrait of his ferocious friend, the writer Pietro Aretino. And there's a woman in a blue dress, known simply as La Bella.

Begin with the marble woman in the middle of the room. Walk over to Canova's Venus, and play her track beside her.

## 029 · Sala di Venere · Canova — Venus Italica

*In the centre of the Sala di Venere, beside Canova's marble Venus. Walk all the way round her as you listen.*

This statue was made to fill a hole. A very famous hole.

For more than a century, the pride of Florence had been an ancient marble goddess known as the Medici Venus. She stood in the Tribune of the Uffizi, the most famous antique statue in the collection. Then the French carried her off to the Louvre.

In eighteen-oh-two, the King of Etruria, the short-lived kingdom that then ruled Tuscany, asked Antonio Canova to make a copy. Canova was the most celebrated sculptor in Europe. The idea of replacing a masterpiece tempted him. But he refused to simply copy it. He made a Venus of his own.

It took years, and it outlived the kingdom that ordered it. Then Napoleon's sister Elisa became Grand Duchess of Tuscany, and she persuaded her brother to pay the bill, twenty-five thousand francs.

Now walk round her. The ancient Venus stands still and faces you. Canova's Venus moves. She has just stepped out of her bath, her vase of perfumed oils at her feet. Her weight is on one leg, her body turning away, and she pulls the cloth around her in a single twisting gesture. Her head is bent, and she isn't posing. She's reacting to being seen. And she's the size of a real woman.

Look at the drapery where it clings, damp, against her body. Then at the skin of her shoulder and her back. Canova was famous for making marble read as cloth and as skin, and you can see why.

In April eighteen-twelve she arrived in the Tribune, set on a revolving base so visitors could turn her and see every side. And then history turned too. Napoleon fell, and the ancient Venus came home. Canova's Venus, by now ousted from her place, was moved here, to the Pitti.

She was made as a stand-in. She stayed as a star.

On the walls around her hang the three Titians. Start with the Concert, three men gathered around a keyboard.

## 030 · Sala di Venere · Titian — The Concert

*In the Sala di Venere, in front of Titian's Concert: three half-length men around a keyboard instrument.*

Something has just happened in this painting. We're never told what.

Three men are making music. In the middle, a man in a dark blue cloak trimmed with fur sits at a spinet, a small keyboard instrument. His hands are still on the keys. Behind him stands a monk in the black and white of the Dominicans, a viola da gamba at his side. The monk has reached out and touched the player's shoulder.

And the player has turned his head. He isn't looking at us. He's looking at the monk. The two of them share a glance, and whatever passes between them, it isn't for us.

On the left stands the third man, a young singer. He wears a feathered cap, which in Titian's Venice was a coveted accessory, a sign of boldness and virility. He's dressed for attention. And yet he's the one left outside the moment, like us.

For centuries, this painting carried another name. When Cardinal Leopoldo de' Medici bought it in sixteen fifty-four, it was sold as a work by Giorgione. He was the mysterious Venetian painter who died young, around fifteen ten, and whose few pictures changed Venetian painting. You can see why people thought so. The warm, smoky light. The story that never explains itself. Today, though, scholars unanimously give it to Titian, painted when he was a young man, in Giorgione's world and very nearly in his shadow.

We don't know who these three men were, or what they were playing. People have proposed many readings, and none of them has won.

What we have is the moment. The hand on the shoulder. The music stopped mid-phrase. Two men who understand each other, and a third who is just a little too late. Five centuries on, we're still standing where the singer stands.

Next, find Titian thirty-five years later. Look for his portrait of the writer Pietro Aretino, a big bearded man in a red cloak with a heavy gold chain.

## 031 · Sala di Venere · Titian — Portrait of Pietro Aretino

*In the Sala di Venere, in front of Titian's half-length portrait of a heavy bearded man in a wide red cloak and a gold chain.*

This is the face of the most feared writer in sixteenth-century Italy. And he had a complaint about the portrait.

Pietro Aretino was a poet, a satirist and a pamphleteer. He made his living partly by praising the powerful, and partly by threatening to ruin their reputations if they didn't pay. He was also one of Titian's closest friends. In Venice, the two of them and the sculptor Jacopo Sansovino formed the inner circle of the city's artistic life.

Aretino ordered this portrait himself, early in fifteen forty-five. That October he sent it as a gift to Duke Cosimo de' Medici, here in Florence. That's how it came to the Medici.

Look at how it's painted. The face is finished, alert and watchful. The long beard, the proud set of the shoulders, the heavy gold chain. But the great red cloak that fills most of the canvas is painted with astonishing freedom. Broad strokes, folds and reflections suggested rather than spelled out.

Aretino noticed. In a letter, he grumbled that if he'd paid Titian more coins, the strokes would have been brighter, softer and more solid. In other words, you get the cloak you pay for.

But here's the turn. What Aretino called unfinished is exactly what we now treasure. It's Titian's free, sketching style, where the paint stops describing cloth and simply becomes it. Stand close and you see only strokes. Step back and a living man fills the frame.

Now turn and look back across the room at the Concert. Same painter, about thirty-five years apart. There, everything is soft and hushed. Here, the brush moves fast and doesn't apologise.

The sharpest pen in Italy, painted by the friend who knew him best.

One more Titian waits in this room, the last painting on today's route. Look for a young woman in a blue dress. She's known as La Bella.

## 032 · Sala di Venere · Titian — Portrait of a Lady (La Bella)

*In the Sala di Venere, in front of Titian's half-length portrait of a young woman in a blue dress with burgundy sleeves. After this, the way out is through the Sala delle Nicchie.*

We know exactly what she wore. We just don't know who she was.

The man who bought it never said. He was Francesco Maria della Rovere, the Duke of Urbino, and he acquired it from Titian in Venice in the later fifteen-thirties. When he wrote to his agent in Venice to hurry the painting along, he didn't give her a name. He called it simply that portrait of the woman wearing the blue dress.

So who is she? Most scholars now think she isn't anyone in particular. She's an ideal, a picture of perfect feminine beauty, which was a favourite subject at the Italian courts. Even her name came later. She was only called La Bella, the Beautiful One, in the late seventeen-hundreds.

But the dress is very real. Look at the blue damask, worked with fine gold embroidery. Sleeves of burgundy velvet, slashed so that puffs of white fabric show through. A fur draped over her right hand, with a kind of casual elegance. Then look at her face. Honey-coloured hair in knotted braids. Pale cheeks touched with red. And dark eyes, with a gaze that is very much awake.

Now remember the Magdalene, back in the Room of Apollo. Same painter, and very probably the same patron, the Duke of Urbino. And the same journey. In sixteen thirty-one, both paintings came to Florence together, in Vittoria della Rovere's collection. One woman in tears and penitence, the other in blue damask and perfect composure. Two ideals, from one duke's collection.

She may never have existed. But on this whole long walk, nobody is better dressed.

That's the end of the Palatine Gallery. The way out leads through the Sala delle Nicchie to the entrance atrium. From there, take the stairs or the lift up to the second floor, for the Gallery of Modern Art. The Royal Apartments start from this same atrium, and the guide brings you back here for them on your way down.

## 033 · Gallery of Modern Art — A different century

*At the entrance to the Gallery of Modern Art on the second floor. From the stairs or lifts, follow the corridor and turn left; the rooms are numbered from 1 to 30 and run in a loop.*

When this gallery opened in 1924, the word modern meant something rather different. There's no Picasso up here. No Matisse. What there is, across thirty numbered rooms, is Italian painting and sculpture from the late seventeen hundreds to the early twentieth century.

And at its heart, one of the richest collections anywhere of the Macchiaioli. They were Tuscan painters who took their easels outdoors and built pictures out of patches of light and colour, in the years when Italy was becoming a single country.

The rooms themselves belong to the story. These were family rooms of the Lorraine grand dukes.

The loop runs roughly in order of time. Neoclassicism and Napoleon's years come first. Then the grand history paintings. In the middle of the loop, the Macchiaioli, with portraits and landscapes around them. And in the final rooms, later Tuscan painting, Symbolism and the early twentieth century.

Watch the century change as you walk. It starts with marble gods and emperors. By the middle of the loop, it's painting soldiers, woodcutters and women sitting in the shade by the sea.

Room one is where the loop begins. The first rooms hold the Neoclassical work, and a very famous face waits for you there.

## 034 · The Neoclassical rooms — marble, and Napoleon's years

*In Rooms 1 and 2, the first rooms of the loop. Room 1 holds Tenerani's marble Psyche (official); Room 2 is given to Napoleon's years (the museum lists a Sèvres bust of Napoleon there). Benvenuti's Saxons' oath and the colossal head of Napoleon hang in these first rooms; their exact room is not published.*

The first figure in this gallery is a girl who's been left behind. In Room one, Pietro Tenerani's marble Psyche has just been abandoned by Cupid, as the old myth tells it. He carved her around 1819, and she is about as calm as heartbreak gets.

That calm is the whole point. Neoclassical artists were reacting against the swirl and drama of the Baroque. They looked back to ancient Greece and Rome for something cleaner. Clear lines. Balanced compositions. Cool colour. Feeling, yes, but held in check.

Then Room two, and the mood changes. Suddenly it's all about one man.

One great canvas in these first rooms is by Pietro Benvenuti, painted in 1812. It shows the Saxons swearing an oath to Napoleon after the Battle of Jena. In these first rooms hangs Giuseppe Bezzuoli's portrait of Napoleon's sister, Elisa Bonaparte Baciocchi. Remember Bezzuoli's name, by the way. He turns up again a few rooms on, in a story about one of his students.

These rooms are about the Napoleonic years in Tuscany, and about the people who paid for art here, before and after the emperor fell. Grand history and family portraits hang side by side.

And in these first rooms there's also a colossal marble head of Napoleon himself, far bigger than life. It has quite a story, including how it ended up in Florence at all. Stand in front of it to hear it.

## 035 · Canova and workshop — Colossal Head of Napoleon

*In the first rooms of the Gallery of Modern Art, in front of the colossal marble head of Napoleon, far bigger than life (not the small porcelain bust of Napoleon in Room 2). Its exact room is not published; if it isn't on view, walk on to Room 3.*

Napoleon met Antonio Canova five times in Paris, and he wasn't an emperor yet. It was 1802. He was First Consul of France, thirty-three years old, and he wanted his face fixed in marble by the most famous sculptor in Europe.

Canova, it is said, took the job reluctantly. He was Italian, and French armies had been carrying Italian art off to Paris. If you've walked through the Palatine Gallery, you've already seen the proof. Canova's Venus Italica stands there because the ancient Medici Venus had been taken to France. But he went, and he modelled the head in clay, from life.

Now look at the scale. This isn't life-size. It's colossal. Canova idealised him, the way the ancients idealised their rulers. But not too far. The brows are heavy. The mouth is set. Whatever he felt about the sitter, he carved the face of a real man.

Here's the twist. This marble probably isn't all Canova's own work. The museum gives it to Canova and his workshop, around 1810, and says it is only traditionally believed to be his. The head became a kind of bestseller. Copies were turned out in the marble workshops of Carrara, sponsored by Napoleon's sister Elisa, the woman you may have just seen in Bezzuoli's portrait.

And then there's how this one reached Florence. In 1845, Napoleon's brother Louis Bonaparte gave it to Grand Duke Leopold the Second, to thank him for his hospitality during Louis's exile in Florence. Leopold's family, the Lorraine, had only been able to come home to Florence once Napoleon fell. So the conqueror's face ended up in their palace, as a thank-you present.

As for Canova, he turned down Napoleon's invitation to move to Paris as his sculptor. After Waterloo, he worked to bring Italy's looted art back home.

The enormous painting in Room three is about another ruler who outstayed his welcome.

## 036 · Stefano Ussi — The Expulsion of the Duke of Athens

*In Room 3 of the Gallery of Modern Art, in front of the huge history painting that fills one wall. Step back to take in the whole crowd.*

Somewhere in this crowd, the painter has hidden himself. Keep an eye out for an armed man with a bandaged head. We'll come back to him.

The story is from July 1343. Walter of Brienne, the Duke of Athens, ruled Florence, and Florence had had enough of him. Stefano Ussi took the scene straight from Machiavelli's Florentine Histories. We're inside the Bargello palace, and the duke is signing away his power.

Look at the faces around him. Behind him stands his councillor, Cerretieri Visdomini, grim as a closed door. On the left, calm and composed, are the plotters. Archbishop Acciaioli stands with representatives of the nobles and of the people. From the right, a soldier gestures urgently. And through the window, the city is already celebrating.

Now look at the size of it. More than three metres high and almost four and a half metres wide. Some of the figures were probably modelled by his friends.

But this isn't really a painting about 1343. Ussi painted it in 1860, and to Florentines that year, the subject was a mirror. Tuscany had just seen off its own ruler, the Lorraine grand duke Leopold the Second, the same man who was given Napoleon's colossal marble head. Ussi meant the old story as a tribute to that new one, the end of rule by foreign princes.

The painting was a triumph. It won first prize at the National Exhibition in Florence, and a gold medal at the Paris Exhibition a few years later.

Now find him. Among the armed men, there's one with his head bandaged, as if he'd been in the fight himself. That's Ussi. He didn't just paint the crowd that threw out a tyrant. He put himself in it.

## 037 · Romanticism and history painting

*In Room 5 of the Gallery of Modern Art, among the large history paintings. Rooms 6 to 9 follow.*

In nineteenth-century Italy, a painting of the distant past was often a comment on the present. You've just seen one. This room is full of them.

The paintings here sit between two worlds, Romanticism and Realism. Romanticism reached Italy in the eighteen twenties, and it brought big subjects, strong colour and high emotion. The great name was Francesco Hayez, trained in Venice and settled in Milan, the painter of The Kiss. The gallery owns work of his as well.

Here, look for Giuseppe Bezzuoli's Entry of Charles the Eighth into Florence, painted in 1829. A French king enters the city, and the painting turns the moment into a grand spectacle. Bezzuoli taught at the Florence Academy, and this is the manner his students were trained in.

Somewhere in these rooms, look too for Giovanni Dupré's sculpture of Abel, dying. No crowd, no costume. Just a young body, and a stillness that feels very close to life.

And next door, a rebellion starts. In the rooms that follow, a group of painters in Florence begin to turn their backs on all this theatre. Room six shows their first experiments, like Giuseppe Abbati's view of the cloister of Santa Croce. Then come rooms of portraits and a room of landscapes.

One of the rebels was a student of Bezzuoli's own, Giovanni Fattori. Keep his name in mind. Walk on through the portrait and landscape rooms to Room ten, where the Macchiaioli rooms begin in earnest, and play its track as you go in.

## 038 · The Macchiaioli rooms

*In Rooms 10 to 13, the heart of the Macchiaioli display; Room 6 holds their first experiments. Play this as you enter Room 10.*

Imagine being called a stain, and deciding to keep the name. Macchia is Italian for a patch, or a smudge. In the early eighteen sixties, critics used it to mock a group of painters in Florence who built their pictures from patches of colour instead of smooth, careful blending. The painters took the insult and wore it with pride.

Their idea was simple and stubborn. Paint what you actually see, out of doors, in real light. Soldiers, farm workers, the coast, a quiet afternoon at home. This gallery holds one of the richest collections of their work anywhere.

Take these rooms one at a time. Look for the pictures of Cristiano Banti, painter and collector, and for Giovanni Boldini's portrait of Alaide Banti.

Then look for Diego Martelli, the critic and patron who championed these painters. Federico Zandomeneghi painted him twice. And look for two landscapes by Camille Pissarro, a rare French Impressionist voice in this Tuscan company.

In Room twelve, Silvestro Lega's Singing a Stornello, a folk song in a quiet interior. And in Room thirteen, Fattori's Italian Camp after the Battle of Magenta. Notice the title. Not the battle, with its charge and its glory. The camp, afterwards.

One more of Fattori's best-loved pictures is in this gallery, a tiny panel of women under an awning by the sea. Its story comes a little later. When you're ready, follow the room numbers on to Room seventeen, where the last stretch of the loop begins.

## 039 · Toward the twentieth century

*In Room 17, at the start of the last stretch of the loop, which runs on to Room 30.*

After the Macchiaioli, Italian painting stopped agreeing with itself. These last rooms show it heading off in several directions at once, and you can walk them at a brisker pace.

Room seventeen opens with portraits, the faces of a prosperous new middle class. In Room nineteen, look for one more Fattori, a quiet portrait known as La cugina Argia, his cousin Argia, painted around 1861. It's a nice farewell to the painter you've been following.

Toward the end of the century, painters split. Some turned to Symbolism: moody, half-mystical pictures full of dreams and personifications. Others took up Divisionism, the Italian answer to French Pointillism, laying small strokes of pure colour side by side so that they shimmer when you stand back. Watch for both.

Then, in the final rooms, the twentieth century arrives, some of it given to the gallery by private collectors. Take these rooms at your own pace.

Before you go on to Room nineteen, look in Room eighteen for a tiny panel, only twelve centimetres high, of women sitting under an awning by the sea. That's Fattori's Rotonda di Palmieri. Play its track in front of it.

## 040 · Giovanni Fattori — La Rotonda di Palmieri

*In Room 18 of the Gallery of Modern Art, in front of Fattori's Rotonda di Palmieri, if it is on view: the Ministry of Culture catalogue lists it in Room 18; the museum's own site does not publish its room. It is tiny, only 12 by 35 centimetres, so look closely along the walls.*

One of the most loved paintings in this gallery is twelve centimetres high. If you've found it, well done. Plenty of people walk straight past.

Fattori painted it in 1866, at the Bagni Palmieri, a bathing establishment on the seafront at Livorno, his home town. The rotonda of the title was part of the bathing establishment, out over the sea. Over it stretches a long ochre awning for shade. And under the awning, in a row, sit seven women.

They aren't here to swim. They've come to sit out of the sun and take the sea air, fully dressed. Nothing is happening. That's the subject.

Now lean in. None of the figures is bigger than your fingernail, and yet each one is a person. One leans forward. One sits back. One turns toward the sea. You can't make out a single face. You can read every posture.

And look at how it's made. The ochre band of the awning, the blue of the water, the soft greys of the dresses, the strip of coast. Each one is a patch of colour, set down beside the next without blending. This is the macchia at its purest. Fattori was about forty. Monet's Impression, Sunrise was still six years away.

He probably worked from quick studies, then built this up later. Either way, it doesn't feel composed. It feels glimpsed.

The costume museum on this floor even keeps a woman's garden dress whose shape its curators compare to the dresses worn right here.

Then take the last rooms at your own pace. When you've finished, follow the signs back to the landing and go along the corridor to the right. A staircase of two short flights takes you into the Museum of Fashion and Costume, on this same floor. Play its track at the entrance.

## 041 · The Palazzina della Meridiana

*At the entrance to the Museum of Fashion and Costume, in the Palazzina della Meridiana on the second floor, reached from the landing along the corridor to the right and a staircase of two short flights, with a stair-lift. This is a dead end: you leave the way you came.*

This building is named after a spot of sunlight. More exactly, after the line it travels along.

In 1699, Vincenzo Viviani, a pupil of Galileo, made a meridian in the vestibule of Grand Prince Ferdinando de' Medici's apartment. It lies under a vault painted by Anton Domenico Gabbiani with an Allegory of Time and the Arts. In that painted vault there's a tiny hole. At noon the sun shines through it, and a small bright spot falls on a line in the floor, at a different point for every day of the year. Time painted on the ceiling. Time measured on the floor.

The little palace itself came later. It was begun for Grand Duke Pietro Leopoldo, and only finished in 1830, under Leopold the Second. The Lorraine and later the Savoy lived here.

Today it holds Italy's first state museum devoted to the history of fashion, and to what clothes say about the people who wear them. The collection runs to more than fifteen thousand pieces, from the eighteenth century to today. Silk and lace don't like light, so only a part is shown at a time, and the displays change roughly once a year.

One thing never changes. The oldest and rarest garments here are always on show: the clothes three Medici were buried in, more than four hundred years ago. Look for them first.

## 042 · The funeral clothes of Cosimo I, Eleonora di Toledo and Don Garzia

*In front of the case with the three Medici funeral garments, which are permanently on display. The museum does not use room numbers.*

These clothes spent centuries in the dark, on the people who were buried in them. By rights, they shouldn't have survived at all.

Cosimo the First was the first Medici grand duke of Tuscany. Eleonora di Toledo was his wife, the daughter of the Viceroy of Naples, mother of eleven children, and one of the most powerful women in Italy. Don Garzia was one of their younger sons, born in 1547.

In the late autumn of 1562, Eleonora travelled into the Maremma, the marshy coast of southern Tuscany, with Garzia and his older brother, Cardinal Giovanni. All three fell ill. Giovanni died first, in November. Garzia followed in December. And Eleonora died five days after him.

People talked, of course. A rumour went round, and lasted for centuries, that Cosimo had killed his own son in a rage, and that grief had killed Eleonora. It's almost certainly false. Doctors' reports from the time point to malaria, and studies of Eleonora's bones show she also had tuberculosis.

Cosimo, Eleonora and Garzia were all laid in the Medici vault at San Lorenzo, dressed in the clothes you see here. The tombs were opened in the nineteenth century and again in the twentieth. What came out was badly decayed, and it took long and painstaking restoration before these garments could go on show.

Now, Eleonora's dress. You may have heard that it's the very dress she wears in Bronzino's famous portrait of her, in the Uffizi. It's a lovely legend. It isn't true. The portrait dress and the burial dress are two different gowns.

But don't be disappointed. The painted dress belongs to a picture. This one belonged to her, and she wore it on her last journey. Look at the cut of the bodice, the way the fabric was shaped to a real woman's body. More than four hundred and fifty years later, it still holds the outline of the person inside it.

When you're ready, walk on into the galleries, where the rest of the collection is shown in rotation.

## 043 · Three centuries of dress — the rotating display

*Anywhere in the museum's galleries beyond the Medici garments. The displays rotate, so play this wherever you like; when you've finished, go back the way you came to the second-floor landing, then down one floor for the Royal Apartments, or down to the courtyard.*

Every museum has a problem with light. A fashion museum has a worse one. Silk fades, linings weaken, and a dress left out for too long simply starts to fall apart.

So this museum works in rotation. Its collection covers the eighteenth century to today: clothes, jewellery and accessories, even stage costumes worn by stars of film, theatre and opera. Only a part is on show at any one time, and every so often, a new selection comes out to replace it.

Which means I can't tell you exactly what you'll see. But I can tell you what to watch for, whatever it is. Watch the shape of the body.

In the seventeen hundreds, court dress was built like architecture, with hoops at the hips pushing skirts out sideways. Around 1800, all that collapsed. Waistlines jumped up under the bust, and skirts fell straight and light, like a classical statue. Then the nineteenth century put the structure back, until the crinoline made skirts as wide as doorways again. And the twentieth century threw away the corset, and kept reinventing the body every decade or so.

One chapter of that story happened right here. In February 1951, a Florentine businessman, Giovanni Battista Giorgini, showed Italian designs to a small group of American buyers in his own home. It went well. From 1952 the shows moved into the Sala Bianca, the White Hall of this palace. That's where the world started to take Italian fashion seriously.

When you've seen the galleries, go back the way you came to the landing, and take the stairs or a lift down one floor, to the entrance atrium of the Palatine Gallery. That's the meeting point for the Royal Apartments, and there's a track to play while you wait. If you're not visiting them, carry on down to the courtyard and follow the signs for the Palatine Chapel.

## 044 · Imperial and Royal Apartments — Before your visit

In the rooms you're about to visit, three ruling families lived one after another. And none of them ever quite cleared out the one before.

First came the Medici. In the late sixteen hundreds this wing was the apartment of Grand Prince Ferdinando, the brilliant, short-lived son of Cosimo the Third. And last the House of Savoy, kings of a newly united Italy, who moved in when Florence became the capital in 1865. They kept the palace for half a century, before handing it to the state.

So the visit is fourteen rooms and three layers of taste. Here's what to watch for.

In the Green Room, where you begin, look up at the ceiling canvas by the Neapolitan painter Luca Giordano, an allegory of peace between Florence and Fiesole. ## 045 · Imperial and Royal Apartments — After your visit

*Anywhere once your staff-led group has left the Royal Apartments, on the first floor. From here the main stairs and lifts go down to the courtyard and the ground-floor museums.*

So. Were they parrots?

They were eagles. The silk in that room is French, woven around 1810, and the bird in each medallion was meant to be Napoleon's imperial eagle. When the Lorraine grand duke Ferdinand the Third came home to Florence in 1814, after Napoleon's fall, he brought the hangings with him. The Florentines took one look at the proud imperial bird and called it a parrot. The name stuck.

That one room is the whole apartment in miniature. Nobody here ever started from scratch. Each family moved into the last one's rooms and changed only what it needed.

The Medici left their mark where you'd least expect it. Ferdinando's alcove, his private bedchamber, became a chapel under the Lorraine. Even so, its white and gold stucco, with mottoes for Ferdinando and his wife Violante, survived almost untouched. The Lorraine brought their silks and the little Oval Cabinet, all Rococo curves. The Savoy brought a throne under their own coat of arms. And Queen Margherita made a home in rooms hung with blue and yellow brocade. She's the queen the pizza is said to be named after, though that story may be better than it is true.

Then, in 1919, King Victor Emmanuel the Third handed the whole palace to the state. Three dynasties, one set of rooms, and almost nothing thrown away.

From here, take the main stairs or a lift down to the courtyard. The Russian Icons and the Palatine Chapel are on the ground floor, opening off it. Follow the signs for the Palatine Chapel.

## 046 · Museum of Russian Icons — Cosimo III's summer apartment

*Ground floor, off the Ammannati Courtyard: follow the signs for the Palatine Chapel and Russian Icons, and play this in the first frescoed room.*

For decades, these rooms were offices, and hardly anyone looked up at the ceilings. Then they were restored and opened as a museum in 2022, and the seventeenth-century frescoes came back to life. Now Russian icons hang in front of them.

The rooms themselves belong to a wedding. In 1661 the future Grand Duke Cosimo the Third married a French princess, Marguerite Louise d'Orléans, and this ground floor was decorated as the couple's summer apartment. The icons came by a slower road. A few had belonged to the Medici. Two were in the palace even before the wedding, kept in a chapel of relics by Cosimo's mother, the Grand Duchess Vittoria della Rovere. Most of the rest arrived after the Medici line died out, when the new Habsburg-Lorraine grand dukes kept collecting. Today there are seventy-eight. It's described as the oldest collection of its kind anywhere outside the lands where icons were made.

As you walk, notice the size. These are small panels, made for prayer at home or on a journey, not altarpieces for a church. And the gold isn't decoration. In the Orthodox tradition it stands for the light of heaven. The figures inside it are meant to be present with you, not painted at a distance.

Look out for a Mother of God of Tikhvin, signed by its painter, Vasily Gryaznov, and dated 1728.

But start with the earliest icons ever recorded in Florence. The small Beheading of Saint John the Baptist hangs in this first room, and its companion, a Mother of God surrounded by crowds, should be close by. Play their track in front of them.

## 047 · The two oldest icons — The Beheading of Saint John the Baptist and "All Creatures Rejoice in You"

*Room 1, in front of the small Beheading of Saint John the Baptist; its companion, the Mother of God "All Creatures Rejoice in You", should be nearby; its room is not stated officially.*

Nobody knows how these two little panels reached Florence. But we do know where they were in 1639. An inventory of that year lists them in the Chapel of Relics here in the palace, among the treasures of Grand Duke Ferdinando the Second and his wife Vittoria. That makes them the earliest icons recorded in Florence. They waited almost four centuries for a museum of their own. A diplomatic gift is the usual guess, but nobody has found the document that proves it.

Both came from workshops in Moscow. The Mother of God was painted in the last quarter of the fifteen hundreds. The Beheading is a little younger, from around the turn of the century. Both were dressed in precious metal, gilded silver, fine filigree and touches of enamel, laid over the painted surface.

Start with the Beheading, which tells the story in several scenes at once. First, a soldier leads John out of prison. Then he waits, bound, for the sword. And his head already lies in a golden basin. Look for a small tree with an axe at its root. It recalls John's own warning, that the axe is laid to the root of the trees. Nothing here shouts, because the story is told as something finished, and holy.

Now the other icon. Its title comes from a hymn, All creatures rejoice in you. Mary sits on a throne with the Child, who raises his hand in blessing. Angels pray around her, inside a church, with paradise opening beyond. Below, look at the ranks of the righteous, lined up like a choir. Two saints lead them, John the Baptist on the left, and on the right John of Damascus, who wrote the hymn. The poet is in the picture.

So the same John appears twice, in two small panels that were in this palace before Cosimo the Third was even born. When you're ready, walk through to the central hall, the largest room here, with an altar at one end. That's the Palatine Chapel. Play its track there.

## 048 · The Palatine Chapel

*The large central hall of the ground-floor apartment, beside the icon rooms. Stand near the middle, then look up at the vault and along the walls.*

This room began as a place for parties. When Cosimo the Third married in 1661, it was the grand central hall of the couple's summer apartment, a room for receiving guests. A century later the palace had new masters. In 1766 the Habsburg-Lorraine Grand Duke Pietro Leopoldo, the reforming ruler who later became emperor, turned the hall into the court chapel.

The paintings came a generation after that. Luigi Ademollo, born in Milan, spent his working life in Florence, and he decorated this chapel in the early 1790s for Grand Duke Ferdinand the Third. Thirty years later he came back to finish one of the choir lofts. On the vault is the Assumption of the Virgin. Along the walls run scenes from the New Testament, among them Christ's entry into Jerusalem and the Crucifixion.

Now find the altar. It's Florentine commesso, hard stones cut and fitted together like a mosaic. Some of its pieces were made for one of the grandest Medici projects, the altar of the Chapel of the Princes at San Lorenzo, which was never finished. So stone meant for the family's great mausoleum ended up here, in a converted reception hall.

Through the wall, Orthodox icons. Here, a Catholic court chapel. Two traditions of Christian devotion, side by side under one roof.

## 049 · The Ammannati Courtyard and the first view

*At the back of the Ammannati Courtyard, with the palace behind you and the garden rising ahead; the Artichoke Fountain is on the terrace above. The Bacchino is reached from the left, east, side of the courtyard; an accessible tunnel under the loggia leads to the same little square.*

Behind the grandest house in Florence, the Medici kept a whole hillside. The Boboli Gardens cover more than thirty hectares, terraces and avenues, fountains and woods, climbing away from the palace toward the old city walls.

It began with a woman. In 1549 Eleonora di Toledo, the Spanish-born wife of Cosimo the First, set the work in motion, with Niccolò Tribolo as her designer. Tribolo died the following year. Ammannati, who built this courtyard, took over, and then Buontalenti. For three centuries, Medici and then Lorraine rulers kept adding. The result became a model for court gardens across Europe. Eleonora's own burial clothes, by the way, are on display in the palace, in the Costume Museum on the second floor.

An honest word before you set off. The garden is going through a long restoration programme called Boboli Twenty-Thirty. You'll meet scaffolding and fenced-off areas along the way, but most of what's behind the fences can still be seen from the paths.

The design hangs on one idea, the axis. From here a single straight line climbs the hill, past the Artichoke Fountain above you, up through the amphitheatre, past the Neptune Fountain, to a colossal statue at the top. Almost everything in the central garden is arranged around it.

## 050 · The Bacchino — Morgante on a Tortoise

*In the small square at the Bacchus exit, on the east side of the garden by the palace and the Vasari Corridor wall; the figure on the turtle sits in the fountain.*

The little man on the turtle had a real name. He was Braccio di Bartolo, born in the countryside near Bologna, and he had dwarfism. He was sold by his parents to the court of Cosimo the First and Eleonora. The court renamed him Morgante, after a giant in a popular comic poem. It was a joke about his size, and he carried it all his life.

He served Cosimo from 1541, as jester and companion, and became one of the best-known faces at court. Cosimo eventually gave him a farm of his own near Arezzo. The painter Bronzino portrayed him in a famous double-sided portrait. Some years later the sculptor Valerio Cioli carved him in marble, in the 1560s.

It's hard to look at this honestly without holding two thoughts at once. A disabled man was made into entertainment, carved naked and turned into a fountain. That was the casual cruelty of the age. And yet the carving seems to come from someone who looked at him closely, as a person, not a type.

Now look at the details. The turtle isn't random. A turtle with a sail on its back was Cosimo's personal emblem, with the motto festina lente, make haste slowly. So Morgante is riding the duke's own badge. And the raised arm is a gesture of command. He's asking for silence before he speaks, just like the ancient bronze of the emperor Marcus Aurelius in Rome. A naked jester on a turtle, calling the crowd to order like an emperor.

What you see here is a modern copy. The original marble is kept indoors, safe from the weather.

The joke is sharp, and the carving is affectionate. Both are true, and that's why he's worth the stop. The Grotta Grande is just a few steps along this same wall. Play its track in front of its façade.

## 051 · Buontalenti — the Grotta Grande

*Beside the Bacchino, along the same east wall near the entrance to the Vasari Corridor. Stand in front of the façade; go inside only if the gate is open.*

This started life as a water tank. In the 1550s the Medici needed a reservoir for a new aqueduct, and they built one here. Then Vasari gave it a sober front of columns. And then, a generation later, Bernardo Buontalenti got hold of it and turned it into the strangest building in the garden.

Look at the façade. Two niches hold Apollo and Ceres, by Baccio Bandinelli, gods of light and of the harvest, all order and calm. Above and around them, the stone starts to melt. It drips. It swells into rocky crusts, as if the building were slowly turning back into a cave.

That was the whole idea. Inside, if the gate is open today, the first chamber is worked to look like a wet cave. Stalactites, sponge stone and shells, with shepherds and animals half emerging from the rock. In its four corners stand four of Michelangelo's unfinished Prisoners. Or rather, they did. The Medici set the originals here in 1585, figures struggling out of raw marble, placed in rock that only pretended to be raw. In 1908 they left for the Accademia Gallery, and what stands here now are replicas.

So where does nature end and art begin? The cave looks natural, and every inch of it is made. Florentines of the time loved that question, and this is their answer in stone and water.

Further in, the second chamber holds a marble of Theseus carrying off Helen, by Vincenzo de' Rossi. The third holds Giambologna's Venus, a young woman stepping from her bath, with four satyrs on the basin below her.

Even from outside, you can feel the joke. A god's calm face, and right above it, the rock beginning to drip. When you're done here, look for the small path that climbs behind and above the grotto. It leads to the Grotta di Madama, the oldest grotto in the garden. Play its track there.

## 052 · The Grotta di Madama

*Above and behind the Grotta Grande, in the small Giardino di Madama by the old convent wall, reached by the narrow side paths. The grotto is normally closed, so you see it from outside.*

You've just seen the famous grotto. This is the little one it grew from. The Grotta di Madama is the oldest grotto in the garden, built between 1553 and 1555 under Davide Fortini, the son-in-law of the garden's first designer, Tribolo.

It's usually closed, opened only on special occasions, so you'll most likely see it from outside. Look at the front. A wall of artificial rock, set inside a neat stone frame with a gable on top, and a small doorway framed in white marble in the middle. Wild nature, held in a classical picture frame.

Inside, the walls are rough like sponge, with stalactites hanging down and sculpted animals, goats and rams among them, emerging from the rock around a basin. Bandinelli carved the central goat. The vault was painted with grotesques.

Why did a Renaissance garden need fake caves at all? The answer goes back to ancient Rome. Roman villas had nymphaea, cool shaded grottoes with water, where the nymphs of springs and woods were imagined to live. Florentines, rediscovering antiquity, wanted their own. A grotto was a retreat on a hot day, a piece of theatre, and an argument about nature and art, all at once.

The name came later. According to the museum, the name probably comes from Maria Maddalena of Austria, wife of Cosimo the Second, whose Habsburg arms were on the grotto's wooden door.

Fortini made the modest first attempt. Thirty years on, Buontalenti took the same idea and let it run wild just below you. From here, make your way up and across to the great amphitheatre behind the palace. Play its track from the path at its edge.

## 053 · The Amphitheatre

*At the edge of the amphitheatre, ideally at its palace end with the palace behind you and the obelisk in front. The stone tiers are fenced off for restoration; view it from the paths around the rim.*

Here's a strange thought to start with. The palace behind you came out of this hole.

This hollow in the hillside was a quarry, and its stone went into the walls of Palazzo Pitti. When Eleonora di Toledo began these gardens around 1550, her architect Tribolo had a better idea than filling it in. He turned it into a theatre of greenery, carved out of the hill itself. Almost a century later, Giulio Parigi turned it into the walled amphitheatre you see now, finishing it in 1634.

And then the Medici threw parties here. Picture the tiers packed with spectators, the grand ducal family watching in state, and in the arena below, the kind of spectacle Florence did better than anyone. Music, dancing, costumes, painted stage machinery rolling across the grass. For the Medici, a show like this was a statement. It told every visiting ambassador exactly how rich, and how civilised, this little state was.

The shows didn't last forever. The last great performance here was in 1739, to welcome a new ruler, Francis Stephen of Lorraine, after the Medici line had died out. Not long after, the seats were planted over, and the music stopped.

Now the centrepiece. The obelisk in the middle is the oldest thing in this garden by a very long way. It was carved in Egypt for the pharaoh Ramesses the Second, more than three thousand years ago. The emperor Domitian brought it to Rome for a temple of Isis. A Medici cardinal bought it for his villa in Rome, and in 1790 it finally came here. The great red granite basin at its foot came from the same villa, and joined it fifty years later.

So in one view, you've got an Egyptian obelisk, a Medici villa, a Renaissance quarry, and a Baroque theatre. The path climbing the hill beyond the obelisk leads to the Neptune Fountain.

## 054 · Stoldo Lorenzi — the Neptune Fountain

*On the terrace above the amphitheatre, on the path around the Neptune basin. The basin area is fenced off during restoration; view the fountain from the path.*

Florentines have a habit of giving their great sculptures nicknames, and they aren't always kind. Down in the Piazza della Signoria, the big marble Neptune became il Biancone, the big white one, and nobody meant it as a compliment. This one got off lightly. Florentines call it the Fontana del Forcone, the fountain of the big fork. Look at the trident, and you'll see why.

The bronze is by Stoldo Lorenzi, made between 1565 and 1568 for Cosimo the First. At that point Cosimo was Duke of Florence and Siena, and he wanted more. In 1569 he would become Grand Duke of Tuscany. He was also building a navy, with ambitions in the Mediterranean. So a sea god, high on the hill behind his palace, was a message as much as an ornament.

The basin is fenced off for restoration at the moment, but the figure is easy to see from the path. Take him in from more than one angle if you can. He stands on a rock, swinging his trident down toward the water, as if about to strike. The beard, the hair and the long, stretched muscles all belong to the late Renaissance style called Mannerism, elegant and a little restless. He isn't a calm classical god. He's caught in motion, almost on stage.

And he was put here to be seen from below. Look back down the hill toward the amphitheatre and the palace, and you'll understand the choice. A duke's sea god, standing guard over his garden.

When you're ready, take the path up and to the left, to the east, toward a small pavilion with a domed, turreted roof on the terrace above. That's the Kaffeehaus. Play its track at the edge of its terrace.

## 055 · The Kaffeehaus and the view

*At the Kaffeehaus, the small pavilion with a domed, turreted roof on the eastern side of the upper garden. Stand at the terrace edge, facing the city.*

This is the view people climb the hill for. The whole of central Florence is spread out below you. In the middle distance rises Brunelleschi's dome, finished in 1436 and still the largest masonry dome ever built. Beside it stands Giotto's bell tower. Beyond the rooftops, the hills climb toward Fiesole.

Now turn around. The pavilion behind you is the Kaffeehaus, begun in 1774 for Grand Duke Pietro Leopoldo by the architect Zanobi del Rosso. It was a resting place for the court on its walks through the garden. The name promises coffee. The museum's own description says they came here for hot chocolate. With its odd, turreted roof, Florentines nicknamed it the Chinese pavilion.

It was restored in 2022, as part of the garden's long restoration.

Close by is the small Garden of Ganymede, with its fountain of the boy carried off by Jupiter's eagle to serve as cupbearer to the gods. A fitting neighbour for a building made for drinks.

From here, follow the path uphill toward the top of the garden. Just below the walled Knight's Garden, at the head of the central axis, stands a colossal woman carved in marble. That's Abundance. Play her track at her feet, or as close as any restoration fence allows.

## 056 · Giambologna and Pietro Tacca — Abundance

*At the foot of the colossal statue of Abundance, at the top of the central axis, just below the Knight's Garden. If the statue is fenced off for restoration, view it from the nearest path. Turn around to look back down the hill toward the palace.*

She may not have been meant as Abundance at all. The story goes that this colossal woman began as a portrait of a real one, Joanna of Austria, a Habsburg archduchess and the wife of Grand Duke Francesco the First. The face, at least, is still said to be hers.

The sculptor was Giambologna, the Flemish-born master who dominated Florentine sculpture in the late fifteen hundreds. He began the figure, and died in 1608 with it unfinished. His pupil Pietro Tacca took it on. Somewhere along the way, the portrait became an allegory. She was given a sheaf of wheat and a horn of plenty, and turned into Abundance, the promise of good harvests under a wise ruler.

In 1637 she was set up here, at the very top of the garden's central line. And that placement is the point. Turn around and look back down the hill. The garden was laid out along this one straight line. It runs from the palace courtyard, past the Artichoke Fountain, through the amphitheatre and its obelisk, past Neptune, all the way up to here. She's the full stop at the end of the sentence.

Imagine who stood here before you. A grand duke, looking down at his palace and his city, with a giant figure of plenty at his back. That is exactly how the Medici wanted to be seen. Rich, generous, and firmly in charge of the view.

From here, head west along the top of the hill to the head of the great cypress avenue, the Viottolone, and start down it. Play its track once you're walking under the cypresses.

## 057 · The Viottolone

*On the Viottolone, the long cypress avenue running down the south-western slope from the top of the hill to the Isolotto. Part of the labyrinth of paths beside it is fenced off for restoration; the avenue itself is open.*

Here the garden changes mood. The first Medici garden stayed close to the palace. Around 1612, under Cosimo the Second, the family began pushing it south, down this slope, and this long straight avenue became the spine of the new design. His son Ferdinando the Second carried the work on.

The line of cypresses on either side is centuries old. The trees themselves are not, or not all of them. They have been renewed again and again, and fifty-three new ones were planted in recent years, to keep the seventeenth-century design alive. A garden like this is never simply finished. It is kept.

Statues stand along the way. Most are Florentine works of the sixteen and seventeen hundreds. Don't worry about naming them. Many aren't labelled, and the point was the effect, a long walk through an open-air gallery.

What to notice is the light. The cypresses throw long shadows across the gravel, and later in the day they make dark bars you walk through. Every so often, look back over your shoulder. The view back up the hill is an easy one to miss.

Part of the labyrinth of paths beside the avenue is fenced off for restoration, but the avenue is open all the way down. At the bottom you'll reach the Isolotto, an oval pool with an island at its heart and a giant figure in the middle. Play its track at the edge of the pool.

## 058 · Giambologna — the Isolotto and Oceanus

*At the rim of the oval Isolotto basin, at the foot of the Viottolone. The island's inner area is closed for restoration, so view the fountain from the outer ring. The Porta Romana gate is right beside the pool.*

The giant in the middle of the island is a stand-in. The real Oceanus left over a century ago. In 1911, the original was moved indoors, to the Bargello museum across the river, and this copy took his place.

The original was carved in marble by Giambologna, and finished by 1576, for the Medici. Giambologna was born in Flanders and came to Italy as a young man. He never left. He became the Medici's own sculptor, and some of Florence's most famous public sculpture is his. The bronze Cosimo on horseback in the Piazza della Signoria. The Rape of the Sabine Women in the Loggia dei Lanzi. And this.

Oceanus, for the ancient Greeks, was the great river that circled the whole world, the source of every other water. At his feet recline three more rivers, the Nile, the Ganges and the Euphrates. Africa, Asia and the ancient East, gathered around one fountain. It's the whole world, with the Medici at its centre.

The fountain didn't start here. It was first set up higher on the garden's main axis, and came down to this island when the pool was made, in 1618.

Now look around the rim, not just at the middle. Perseus rides his horse up out of the water. Lower down are livelier groups, peasants playing their rough village games. After all the gods and emperors, the garden lets in some ordinary people. The island itself is closed for restoration, so enjoy them from the outer ring. And notice the lemon trees in their big terracotta pots. They have a winter home.

Two ways on from here. The Porta Romana gate is right beside this pool. To see that winter home, the Lemon House, walk back north along the Via Romana side of the garden, toward the palace. Play its track when you reach the long, low building.

## 059 · The Limonaia

*Outside the Limonaia, the long, low Lemon House on the western, Via Romana side of the garden, north of the Isolotto toward the palace. Its gates are closed for restoration; view it from outside. The Annalena gate is close by.*

Before this building held lemon trees, the site held a zoo. Cosimo the Third kept a menagerie of exotic animals here from the late sixteen hundreds. A century later, Grand Duke Pietro Leopoldo cleared it away, and his architect Zanobi del Rosso, the man behind the Kaffeehaus, built this in its place. The Limonaia, the Lemon House.

Its gates are closed for restoration at the moment, so you'll see it from outside. It's long and low, more than a hundred metres, and it faces south to catch the winter sun. Every autumn, some five hundred potted citrus trees are brought in out of the cold. Every spring, they go back out into the garden.

Why all the fuss? The Medici were obsessed with citrus. They built up one of the great collections of lemons, oranges and citrons in Europe. Citrus was a status symbol. It was exotic, hard to keep alive through a Tuscan winter, and it gave fruit, flowers and scent all at once. A ruler who could show you fruiting trees in January was showing off his mastery of nature.

And they loved the strange ones best. The most famous was the Bizzarria, a single tree whose fruit mixes citron and bitter orange. Some of these old varieties still survive in the Medici collections today.

From here, the Annalena gate onto Via Romana is close by. Or follow the avenue north, back toward the palace. Whichever gate you choose, play the Ways out track as you head for it.

## 060 · Ways out of the garden

*Play it on the way to whichever gate you choose: Palazzo Pitti through the Ammannati Courtyard, Annalena on Via Romana, Porta Romana beside the Isolotto, or Forte di Belvedere at the top of the hill.*

A garden this big has more than one front door. Boboli has four official gates, and each one leaves you somewhere different.

The main gate is the palace itself. Go back through the Ammannati Courtyard and you come out on Piazza Pitti, a few minutes from the Ponte Vecchio and the river.

On the western side, the Annalena gate opens onto Via Romana, in the Oltrarno, the old artisans' quarter south of the Arno. The name has a story. Annalena Malatesta was a noblewoman living in Florence whose husband was murdered in 1441. After his death she founded a convent on this street, and her name has stayed with it ever since.

At the far south-western corner, right beside the Isolotto, is the Porta Romana gate. It lets you out by the old city gate of the same name, on the road that once led south toward Rome.

And at the very top of the hill, the Forte di Belvedere gate leads out beside the hilltop fortress, where old lanes run down toward the river. The Bardini Garden, with one of the loveliest views in Florence, is reached from that side. It's a separate garden, with its own entrance.

One idea for another day. Some visitors who come back start at the bottom, at Porta Romana, and climb the garden in reverse. Up the cypress avenue, past the statues, and down at last to the palace. Early morning and late afternoon, when the light is long, are the loveliest times to try it.

Wherever you come out, there's one last short track to play once you're through the gate.

## 061 · Closing

*Anywhere you like once you've finished, at whichever gate or door you came out of: a bench, a café table, the street outside.*

So, here we are. You might be on the slope of Piazza Pitti, on Via Romana, under the old city gate or high up by the fortress. Wherever you've come out, your feet are probably telling you just how big this place is.

Think back to how it looked when you first arrived, a vast and rather severe stone building with a hill behind it. Now, depending on where your day took you, it's full of people. Raphael's young women, looking back at you from the walls upstairs. A little man on a turtle, with a real name, Braccio, carved by someone who looked at him closely. Two small icons that were in the palace before Cosimo the Third was born. A hillside of cypresses, renewed tree by tree. Three dynasties, layered one on top of another in the same rooms. The Pitti was never one thing, and it never tried to be. That's the whole pleasure of it.

And here's the part worth taking home. For nearly five centuries, everyone who held this place was sure they would hold it forever. The banker who built it, the Medici who took it over, the Lorraine grand dukes, and then the kings of Italy. None of them kept it. In 1919 a Savoy king, Victor Emmanuel the Third, simply gave it away, to the Italian State. Which, today, means it belongs to everyone. Including you.

There's no rush now. If you'd like more, the rest of Florence is close. Over the Ponte Vecchio are the Uffizi and the Duomo, where many of the artists you've met today have more to show you. Or stay on this quieter bank, the Oltrarno, where workshops still gild and carve by hand. A short walk away, on the same bank, the Brancacci Chapel holds Masaccio's frescoes, the paintings generations of Florentine artists learned from.

But first, do yourself a kindness. Find a bench, sit down for a minute, and let it all settle. You gave this place your full attention, and that's a generous thing to give anything. Thank you for spending it with me. Buona passeggiata.
