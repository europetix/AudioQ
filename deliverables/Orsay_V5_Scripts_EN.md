## 001 · A station that became a museum

*Arrival · Level 0 — Play this as you arrive, outside the building or just inside, before the galleries. Follow the museum's signs in; you don't need to stand anywhere in particular.*

In nineteen seventy-one, someone wanted to put a big international hotel on this spot. The building permit was refused. Without that refusal, there would be no museum.

It began as a railway station, built for the World's Fair of nineteen hundred. The Orléans railway company chose the architect Victor Laloux, and in just two years he raised a station and a hotel. They opened on the fourteenth of July, nineteen hundred.

Laloux had a delicate problem. His station had to sit beside the Louvre and the Tuileries without embarrassing anyone. So he built it of metal, some twelve thousand tonnes of it, and then hid all that metal behind cut stone. The disguise worked. The painter Édouard Detaille wrote, "The station is superb and looks like a Palais des beaux-arts."

Its life as a great terminus was short. Electric traction and longer trains made its platforms too short, and from nineteen thirty-nine it served only the suburbs.

The war gave it sadder work. Parcels for prisoners were sent out from here. And at the Liberation, this was where prisoners and deportees arrived home from Germany.

After that, it took whatever work it could find. Orson Welles filmed The Trial here. A theatre company moved in, and so did auctioneers. The big hotel plan was turned down, and the station's own hotel closed. Paris had just lost Baltard's market halls at Les Halles, and that loss changed how people saw buildings like this. The station was protected as a historic monument. And at the initiative of Valéry Giscard d'Estaing, the government decided to make it a museum.

A team of architects adapted Laloux's building, and Gae Aulenti led the design of the interiors. President Mitterrand inaugurated the museum on the first of December, nineteen eighty-six. Eight days later, the public walked in.

Forty years on, the station still hasn't quite left. The architects kept its metal structure and its great clocks in plain view. You'll meet both on the way.

## 003 · Couture — Romains de la décadence

*Central aisle · Level 0 — In the central sculpture aisle on level 0, in front of the huge canvas, nearly eight metres wide, of a Roman orgy in a classical hall watched by statues of the ancestors.*

In eighteen forty-seven, Parisians looked at this painting of ancient Rome and recognised themselves.

Thomas Couture spent three years on it, and the State had commissioned it before it was ever shown. It was a history painting, the noblest kind of painting of the day, and it was meant to carry a moral. Couture took his theme from Juvenal. In the Salon catalogue, he printed two of Juvenal's lines. "Crueller than war, vice fell upon Rome and avenged the conquered world."

So look at what he gives you. A great classical hall, and inside it, a party that has turned into an orgy. And all around, the statues of the virtuous ancestors, looking down on their descendants. The old Romans who conquered the world, and the new ones who lost it to pleasure.

But the critics didn't think this was really about Rome. France at the time lived under what's called the July Monarchy, and they quickly read the painting as a jab at their own rulers. In their eyes, it showed The French of the Decadence. The museum today calls it a realist allegory. A painting in togas about Paris.

There's one more reason to remember Couture. From eighteen fifty, a young man studied in his studio for six years. His name was Édouard Manet. You'll see, a few rooms from here, how differently the pupil chose to paint his own time.

For now, cross to the Lille side. In room 1, look for Ingres's tall painting of a standing nude, La Source, and start its track in front of her.

## 004 · Ingres — La Source

*Room 1 · Level 0 · Lille side — In room 1, the first room on the Lille side of level 0, in front of a tall, narrow canvas of a standing nude, set as if in an alcove.*

This calm figure took thirty-six years to arrive, and Ingres didn't finish her on his own.

He began the painting in Florence, around eighteen twenty. Decades passed. He only completed it in Paris in eighteen fifty-six, when he was seventy-six years old. And to get it done, he brought in two of his pupils, Paul Balze and Alexandre Desgoffe. They painted the accessories and the background. That was common practice for large formats.

Look at how he sets her. The canvas is tall and narrow, and she stands in it as if in an alcove. Between that frame and her pose, she takes on the stillness of a marble statue. Ingres's debt to sculpture is plain to see. She isn't caught in a moment. She is placed, like something carved.

Her first owner wanted the opposite effect. A year after it was finished, Count Duchâtel bought the painting from Ingres for twenty-five thousand francs. At his home, he surrounded it with large plants and water flowers, so that the nymph of the spring would look even more like a real person. A painted statue, given a little garden to make her breathe.

Countess Duchâtel later left the painting to the Louvre, and it came to this museum when it opened.

So stand back and see which way it works for you. A woman, or a statue. Ingres, I suspect, wanted you to feel both at once.

Two very different Venuses are waiting in room 3. Play their track once you're standing between them.

## 005 · Cabanel and Bouguereau — Two Venuses

*Room 3 · Level 0 · Lille side — In room 3 on the Lille side of level 0. Cabanel's Venus is the wide, low canvas, more than two metres across; Bouguereau's is the tall one, three metres high.*

In this room, the goddess of love is born twice, sixteen years apart. The first time, the buyer was an emperor. The second time, it was the State.

The wide, low canvas is Alexandre Cabanel's. He showed his Birth of Venus at the Salon of eighteen sixty-three, and it was a great success. Napoleon the Third bought it for his own private collection. Venus has just been born from the foam of the sea, and she's carried ashore. Her body is idealised, but her pose is lascivious. The handling is polished, with echoes of Ingres and of eighteenth-century painting.

Why a goddess? The museum is frank. A myth let artists introduce eroticism without offending public morality, under the pretext of a classical subject.

Not everyone was fooled. The writer Émile Zola wrote that "the goddess, drowned in a sea of milk, resembles a delicious courtesan." "But not of flesh and blood, that would be indecent, but made of a sort of pink and white marzipan."

The tall canvas, three metres high, is William Bouguereau's Birth of Venus, painted in eighteen seventy-nine. This time the State bought it from the painter after that year's Salon, for the Musée du Luxembourg. One Venus for an emperor's private rooms, the other for a public museum.

In the same year as Cabanel's triumph, Édouard Manet painted a nude of his own. No foam, no myth, no pretext. She's called Olympia, and you'll meet her shortly on the Seine side.

First, head back out to the central aisle and find Carpeaux's bronze group of a father with his children, Ugolin, and play its track beside it.

## 006 · Carpeaux — Ugolin

*Central aisle · Level 0 — In the central sculpture aisle on level 0, at the bronze group of a father with his children, nearly two metres high.*

Jean-Baptiste Carpeaux arrived in Rome almost two years late, which was not the best way to start.

As a winner of the Prix de Rome, he had a place at the Villa Medici. He got there in eighteen fifty-six, and from the very beginning, things were awkward with the director, the painter Victor Schnetz. Then Carpeaux chose his subject, and worked on it for four years, well beyond the time he was allowed to stay.

The subject came from Dante's Inferno. In thirteenth-century Pisa, Ugolino della Gherardesca betrayed his party. The archbishop, Ubaldini, had him shut up in a tower to starve to death. Carpeaux shows him with his children.

Look at the father. Everything in this group is about his pain and his anguish. Carpeaux had been studying the ancient Laocoön, Michelangelo, and Géricault's Raft of the Medusa. All three stand behind this one father's face.

The academy in Paris judged the plaster, and the verdict fell short of his hopes. Its remarks even appeared in the press. Then the State offered him a commission for a marble, at a fee he thought far too low. Carpeaux negotiated hard, and in the end the result was this bronze, shown at the Salon of eighteen sixty-three.

Then the bronze found a curious home. For some forty years, it stood out in the Tuileries Garden, as a partner to a Laocoön. Carpeaux had studied the Laocoön, and now his Ugolino stood beside one.

Now cross to the Seine side and find room 4, for Daumier's small painted clay heads of public men, the Celebrities of the Juste Milieu. Their track starts there.

## 007 · Daumier — The Célébrités du Juste Milieu

*Room 4 · Level 0 · Seine side — In room 4 on the Seine side of level 0, at the small painted clay busts of men of the July Monarchy. Some are barely the height of a hand; how many are on show may vary.*

The man who ordered these little heads is one of them. His nickname is the Toothless Laugher.

He was Charles Philipon, founder of the satirical papers La Caricature and Le Charivari, which attacked the July Monarchy. That's the same regime Couture would needle with his Roman orgy, fifteen years later. Philipon asked Honoré Daumier to model the public men of the day.

Daumier made them in clay and never fired them in a kiln. Instead, he painted the raw clay in oil, and used the heads as models for his lithographs. Of the forty or so that were made, thirty-six have survived, and every one of them is in this museum.

They are called Celebrities of the Juste Milieu, and not simply parliamentarians, for a good reason. Only twenty-six of these men actually held a seat.

Unbaked clay is fragile, and these heads have needed care. In the nineteen twenties a sculptor repaired them, before bronze casts were made. Several of them were bought for the collection in nineteen eighty.

Now look closely, because they are small. Philipon's head is about sixteen centimetres high, and Guizot's about twenty-two. The nicknames came down with them. Guizot is the Bore and Dupin is the Preacher, so look for them if they are on show. Daumier didn't need captions. The faces do the work.

Next, the mood changes completely. In room 5, look for Millet's painting of three women gleaning in a field, The Gleaners. If that wall is empty, the painting is away on loan, but play its track anyway. It will point you to another Millet close by.

## 008 · Millet — Des glaneuses (The Gleaners)

*Room 5 · Level 0 · Seine side — In room 5 on the Seine side of level 0, in front of a canvas about a metre wide of three women gleaning in a field, with the harvest behind them. From 14 November 2026 to 28 March 2027 it is on loan to Tokyo: if the wall is empty, listen in front of Millet's L'Angélus, in this room 5 or in Galerie Seine 1, the Alfred Chauchard collection gallery on the Seine side close by, instead.*

Three women in a field, working for leftovers. That is the whole subject of The Gleaners.

If this wall is empty — the painting travels to Tokyo this winter — walk to Millet's L'Angélus, which hangs nearby, in this room or in the Chauchard collection gallery along the Seine side, and listen there.

Women like these were allowed into the fields at sunset. They picked up, one stalk at a time, the wheat the harvesters had missed. That is gleaning.

Jean-François Millet didn't arrive at this picture quickly. It capped ten years of research on the theme of the gleaners. Ten years, for one moment of three women at work, which he showed at the Salon of eighteen fifty-seven.

He built the picture on a contrast. The three gleaners fill the front of the canvas, close to the ground. Far behind them, the harvest itself is going on. That's where the wealth of the field is, pushed back into the distance. Millet makes no speech about it. He simply lets you notice where he has put the plenty, and where he has put the women.

The painting passed through several collections. Then, in eighteen ninety, Madame Pommery gave it to the nation, keeping the right to enjoy it for the rest of her life.

If you are standing at L'Angélus, you're with a close relative. Millet began it in the same year, eighteen fifty-seven. Look at it now. Another field, a man and a woman, a fork, a basket and a wheelbarrow around them. Here too the work has stopped, but this time the pause is for a prayer.

Millet asks for nothing more than your attention, in either picture.

L'Angélus has its own story to tell: the small canvas of a man and a woman pausing in a potato field, in this room 5 or in the Chauchard collection gallery along the Seine side close by. If you're already in front of it, simply play its track now.

## 009 · Millet — L'Angélus

*Room 5 · Level 0 · Seine side — In the Millet room, room 5, on the Seine side of level 0, or close by in Galerie Seine 1, the Alfred Chauchard collection gallery: Chauchard left the painting to the Louvre, and it may hang with the rest of his collection. Look for a small canvas, about 55 by 66 centimetres, of a man and a woman who have stopped work in a potato field, with a fork, a basket, sacks and a wheelbarrow.*

When Millet explained this painting, he didn't talk about faith. He talked about his grandmother.

Years after he painted it, he put it like this, in the museum's translation. "L'Angélus is a painting I created thinking about how, when working in the fields long ago, my grandmother never failed, upon hearing the church bell ring, to make us stop our work to recite the Angelus for those poor souls."

The Angelus is a prayer that recalls the Annunciation. But what Millet painted was a childhood memory, not religious fervour. He himself was not a practising Catholic.

Look how little he gives you. It's a small picture, smaller than most people expect. A man and a woman have stopped in the middle of the potato harvest. Around them are their tools: a fork, a basket, sacks, a wheelbarrow. Nothing is happening. That's the point. The work has stopped because a bell rang.

And then this quiet picture had a very noisy life. In eighteen eighty-nine it was sold at auction to the American Art Association. Soon after, the collector Alfred Chauchard owned it, and he left it to the Louvre. In the twentieth century it became an icon around the world. Salvador Dalí venerated it. And in nineteen thirty-two, a disturbed man slashed it.

All of that, for two people standing still in a field, because an old woman had taught a boy to stop and listen.

Next, go on to room 7. Courbet's Un enterrement à Ornans, A Burial at Ornans, fills a wall there, more than six metres wide, with a crowd of life-size villagers at a funeral. You can't miss it, and its track starts there.

## 010 · Courbet — Un enterrement à Ornans (A Burial at Ornans)

*Room 7 · Level 0 · Seine side — In room 7 on the Seine side of level 0, in front of the enormous canvas, more than six metres wide, of a crowd of life-size villagers at a funeral. It is back on the wall after its restoration, finished in August 2026.*

Nobody knows whose funeral this is. And yet Gustave Courbet painted this village burial as if it were a major historical event.

That was the daring of it. In his day, figures this size belonged to history painting. Life-size figures were a privilege usually reserved for biblical and mythological figures. Gods, saints, heroes. Courbet gave that privilege to the people of Ornans, gathered for a burial. And he didn't flatter a single one of them.

So how did he get it into the Salon of eighteen fifty? He didn't have to ask. The year before, Courbet had won a medal, and that medal exempted him from the Salon jury. No jury could stop this painting. It walked straight in. The museum calls it a manifesto of the realist movement.

Take a moment to walk along it. Every figure is roughly your own size. None of them is idealised. These are faces you might pass in a market, gathered for someone none of us can name.

And you are seeing it fresher than anyone has for a long time. For fifteen months, until the summer of twenty twenty-six, it was restored in public, right here in this room, inside a glass enclosure. The aim was to remove old layers of varnish that had been poorly applied, had yellowed and thickened. Reports on the work describe the original colours coming back. They also describe a few centimetres of paint along the edges, hidden by earlier remountings, now visible again. It was the biggest restoration since the painting entered the national collections, a gift from Courbet's sister Juliette.

An unnamed villager, buried at the scale of history. That is what the word realism meant here, at the start.

Courbet's other giant hangs in this same room. Turn to L'Atelier du peintre, The Painter's Studio, and press play.

## 011 · Courbet — L'Atelier du peintre (The Painter's Studio)

*Room 7 · Level 0 · Seine side — In room 7 on the Seine side of level 0, the same room as A Burial at Ornans, in front of the other huge canvas, about six metres wide: a crowded studio, with groups of figures on either side.*

One of the men in this painting hated the way he looked in it. We'll come to him.

Courbet gave the picture a famously long title. The Painter's Studio, a real allegory determining a phase of seven years of my artistic life. And he summed it up himself. "It's the whole world coming to me to be painted."

He even explained the two halves. On the right, he said, are "all the shareholders, by that I mean friends, fellow workers, art lovers." Look for the collector Alfred Bruyas, the philosopher Proudhon, the critic Champfleury on a stool, and Baudelaire, absorbed in a book.

On the left is the other half. In his words, "the other world of everyday life, the masses, wretchedness, poverty, wealth, the exploited and the exploiters, people who make a living from death." There's a priest, a merchant, a beggar girl, an unemployed worker. And a hunter who, as the museum puts it, somewhat resembles Napoleon the Third. Draw your own conclusions.

This was Courbet's world in eighteen fifty-five, and an official jury turned it down. So he showed it himself, with the Burial, in his own Pavilion of Realism.

Now, the man who hated his portrait was Champfleury, the critic on the stool. "I was appalled at this image of myself," he wrote, "which made me look like the Jesuit General." And then, simply: "This damned portrait is monstrous."

The painting has an unusual bond with the public. In nineteen twenty, it was bought for the nation by a public subscription. Later, it was restored in public, behind glass, and some nine hundred donors raised eighty-six thousand euros to help pay for it. The public bought it once, and then helped to save it.

Courbet's small and very explicit painting L'Origine du monde hangs in the Courbet rooms, if it is on show. Its track is optional. Otherwise, go on to Manet's Olympia in room 14. Either way, play the matching track when you get there.

## 012 · Courbet — L'Origine du monde (optional)

*Courbet rooms 6–7 · Level 0 · Seine side — Optional stop. In the Courbet rooms, 6 and 7, on the Seine side of level 0; the plan does not place this work, so look for a small canvas, about 46 by 55 centimetres, a very frank painting of the female body. If it is not on show, go on to room 14.*

This track is optional. It is about Courbet's L'Origine du monde, The Origin of the World, a small and very frank painting of the female body. If you'd rather not, go straight on to Manet's Olympia in room 14.

Courbet painted it in eighteen sixty-six. It is small, about forty-six by fifty-five centimetres. Its first owner, who probably commissioned it, was a Turkish-Egyptian diplomat, Khalil-Bey. He built up a short-lived collection celebrating the female body, and he was ruined by gambling debts.

For most of its history, very few people saw this painting. It stayed in private hands. Among its later owners was the psychoanalyst Jacques Lacan. The museum describes it as the paradox of a famous work that is seldom actually seen.

It came to the nation in nineteen ninety-five, as payment of inheritance tax in kind. That is how a picture made for one private collection came to belong to everyone.

For a long time, the woman who posed had no name. Then, in twenty eighteen, the scholar Claude Schopp found a letter from the writer Alexandre Dumas the younger to George Sand. On the strength of that letter, the model was identified as Constance Quéniaux, a dancer at the Paris Opéra. Sylvie Aubenas, of the French national library, said she was ninety-nine per cent certain.

So a painting that was so seldom seen now belongs to a public museum, and the woman in it has a name.

## 013 · Manet — Olympia

*Room 14 · Level 0 · Seine side — Room 14 on this floor, Seine side, the Manet room. Stand in front of the nude who looks straight out at you, with a black servant holding a bouquet at the centre and a black cat at the right.*

In eighteen ninety, Claude Monet passed the hat. Nearly a hundred people chipped in, among them artists, collectors, dealers, writers and even politicians. They weren't buying a painting for themselves. They were buying this one, to give it to France.

Twenty-five years earlier, at the Salon of eighteen sixty-five, it had caused a scandal.

On paper, the subject was nothing new. A nude, a servant bringing flowers, the old tradition of the odalisque. Manet knew that tradition from the inside. He had copied Titian's Venus of Urbino, and his Olympia quotes it, along with Goya's naked Maja. But he moved the goddess into the Paris of his own day. The museum puts it in five words. "Venus has become a prostitute."

Now look at her face. The model was Victorine Meurent, and she looks straight back at you. It's a calculating look. She isn't waiting to be admired. She's sizing you up.

In the centre stands a black woman holding a bouquet. For a long time she was treated as almost invisible. In an exhibition in twenty nineteen, called Le Modèle noir, she got her name back. She was Laure.

And on the right, where Titian painted a little dog, Manet put a black cat. It may well be the most famous cat in the history of painting.

So the friends won, and Olympia went into the national collections at the Musée du Luxembourg, and in time it came here. The scandal of the Salon now belongs to everyone, and the woman in the painting is still looking straight back at you.

Two more Manets share this room: a boy from a military band playing the fife, and the writer Émile Zola at his desk. Find them here in room 14. One track covers both.

## 014 · Manet — Le Fifre and Émile Zola

*Room 14 · Level 0 · Seine side — Room 14, the same room as Olympia. Le Fifre is the tall portrait of a boy from a military band against a plain background. Émile Zola shows the writer seated at his work table, with pictures on the wall behind him.*

These two paintings tell one story. It begins with a rejection and ends with a thank-you note, painted in oils.

In eighteen sixty-five Manet travelled to Spain and saw the Prado for the first time. A portrait by Velázquez, of a man called Pablo de Valladolid, bowled him over. He wrote to Fantin-Latour that it was "the most astonishing piece of painting ever done."

Back in Paris, he painted Le Fifre, the Fifer. Look at him. An ordinary boy from a military band, with no name and no setting, just a plain ground behind him. Flat areas of colour, thick streaks of white. And yet, as the museum says, he carries the dignity of a Spanish grandee.

The Salon jury of eighteen sixty-six turned it down. But it was one of the paintings that fired the enthusiasm of Émile Zola, who went on to write a pamphlet defending Manet.

The portrait of Zola is Manet's way of saying thank you. Zola sits at his work table with a book, an inkwell and a quill. On the wall behind him hangs a reproduction of Olympia, the painting Zola thought Manet's best. Behind it, a print after Velázquez, and a Japanese print of a wrestler. A Japanese screen stands on the left.

Now find the blue pamphlet on the table. It's Zola's defence of Manet. And it carries Manet's name. That is the signature of the whole painting.

Fantin-Latour painted these friends too, with Manet among them. Find his big group portrait, Un atelier aux Batignolles, in Galerie Seine 2, the gallery of portraits of the 1860s, on the river side of this floor, and play its track there.

## 015 · Fantin-Latour — Un atelier aux Batignolles

*Galerie Seine 2 · Level 0 — In Galerie Seine 2 on level 0, the gallery of portraits of the 1860s. A very large canvas, about two metres high and nearly three wide, of eight men gathered in a studio, one of them a painter at his easel.*

Eight men fill this studio, and seven of them are here for the one at the easel.

Henri Fantin-Latour showed the picture at the Salon of eighteen seventy. Take the friends from left to right. At the far left, Otto Scholderer, a German painter. Then Manet himself, at the easel, and Renoir, in a hat. Next come Zacharie Astruc, sculptor and journalist, and Émile Zola. Edmond Maître, who worked at the Paris city hall. Then Frédéric Bazille, whose own studio you'll see in room 18, and at the right, Claude Monet.

Why gather them around Manet? Many of these men lived in the Batignolles district of Paris, and Fantin casts Manet as the head of their school. Zola put it this way. "Around the painter scorned by the public, a united front of painters and writers has formed, considering him their master."

Find Manet at the easel first. Then let your eye travel along the group, one friend at a time, and notice how they hold together around him.

Now the least famous name here, probably Scholderer, at the far left. In early twenty twenty-six the museum acquired a painting by him, and calls it a masterpiece. Fame can be slow, sometimes a century and a half slow.

Manet's own great provocation is on this floor too. Find Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe, the Luncheon on the Grass, in room 18, and play its track there.

## 016 · Manet — Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe

*Room 18 · Level 0 — Room 18 on this floor, where the museum lists it. If the painting is not there, it may have moved to room 29 on level 5, the Moreau-Nélaton collection room: listen wherever you find it. It is the large picnic painting, about two metres high and two and a half metres wide.*

In eighteen sixty-three, the Emperor did a remarkable thing. He gave the paintings the Salon jury had turned away a show of their own. By the decree of Napoleon the Third, the Salon des Refusés opened, the Salon of the Rejected. And this painting hung in it.

It wasn't called a luncheon then. Manet showed it under a plainer title, Le Bain, The Bath.

That same year, at the official Salon, the Emperor bought Cabanel's Birth of Venus for his own collection. You may have met her in room 3 earlier. One nude went to the Emperor. This one went to the rejects.

And yet Manet wasn't throwing tradition away. He was borrowing from it, from two sources. One is Titian's Concert champêtre. The other is an engraving by Marcantonio Raimondi, after Raphael's Judgement of Paris. You couldn't ask for more respectable ancestors than Titian and Raphael. Still, the result hung among the rejects.

So take your time with it. Try to sort what in this picnic is modern, and what is Renaissance. That tension is the whole point. The museum's own verdict is careful, and all the stronger for it. This painting "can perhaps be considered as the departure point for Modern Art."

It found admirers anyway. Its first owner was the opera baritone Jean-Baptiste Faure. Later the collector Étienne Moreau-Nélaton owned it, and in nineteen hundred and six he gave it to the national museums.

And it provoked at least one other painter. Claude Monet began a huge picnic of his own, meant as both a tribute and a challenge to Manet. What survives of it hangs in room 29 on level 5.

Monet's other great challenge, Femmes au jardin, Women in the Garden, hangs in room 18 on this floor. Find it, and play its track there.

## 017 · Monet — Femmes au jardin

*Room 18 · Level 0 — Room 18, the same room as Manet's Déjeuner sur l'herbe. A tall canvas, about two and a half metres high, of women in a garden.*

To reach the top of this canvas, Monet lowered it into a trench in the garden, using a pulley. It's a slightly comic picture. A painter, a hole in the ground, and a canvas going up and down like a bucket in a well.

There was a good reason for it. In eighteen sixty-six Monet was painting in the garden of a house he rented in the suburbs of Paris. It was a twofold challenge. First, he painted outdoors, in the light itself. Second, he chose a huge format, the kind usually kept for history painting, for scenes of gods and heroes. Monet gave that size to a few women in a garden.

Now look at the three figures on the left. Three women. And one model. Camille posed for all three.

In nineteen twenty-one, the State bought this painting from Monet himself, for the Musée du Luxembourg. He had painted it more than half a century before.

Much later, it needed care. Old repairs had deteriorated and the varnish had yellowed, so the museum and the national restoration centre for French museums cleaned the painting. The museum says the cleaning brought back "dazzling whites" against bluish-grey shadows. Let your eye move between the bright fabric and the cool shade. That light is what the trench was for.

## 018 · Bazille — L'Atelier de Bazille

## 019 · Who do these works belong to? The MNR room

## 022 · Carpeaux — La Danse

*Central sculpture aisle · Level 0 — In the central sculpture aisle on level 0. The large stone group, over four metres high, of six dancing women in a ring around the Genius of Dance, with a small putto.*

One night at the end of August, eighteen sixty-nine, someone threw a bottle of ink at this sculpture. It had only just been unveiled on the front of Charles Garnier's new Opéra, and plenty of Parisians were furious.

Carpeaux didn't outlive the quarrel by much. He died nine months after the Opéra finally opened, at forty-eight. The war, the fall of the Empire, his death. Between them, they saved his dancers from removal.

Now look at the ring. Follow it all the way round with your eyes, figure by figure. It swirls. This is stone, about eighteen tonnes of it, and it still seems to be moving.

## 028 · Monet — the Déjeuner sur l'herbe fragments

## 029 · Monet — Coquelicots

## 030 · Pissarro — Gelée blanche

## 031 · Monet — La Gare Saint-Lazare

*Room 31 · Level 5 — Room 31, level 5. Monet's painting of the inside of a Paris railway station, the hall filled with steam and light.*

Here's a small irony to enjoy. You're about to look at a railway station, and you're standing inside one.

When Monet painted this, the Gare d'Orsay didn't exist yet. Saint-Lazare did, and it was one of the busiest stations in Paris, as it still is. ## 032 · Caillebotte — Raboteurs de parquet

## 033 · Degas — La Classe de danse

*Room 31 · Level 5 — Room 31, level 5, the same room as Monet's Gare Saint-Lazare and Caillebotte's Floor Scrapers. A ballet class in a rehearsal room, the ballet master among his young pupils.*

This class never took place, not like this anyway. Degas invented it, the way a stage designer invents a set.

The teacher, though, is real: Jules Perrot, a famous ballet master, and Degas painted him as a portrait. But the scene around him is imaginary. That's his trick: he gives you the grand old rehearsal room and the famous teacher. And then he shows you what the end of a long class really feels like. Tired girls, and an itch.

The same year as that first exhibition, Berthe married Eugène Manet. So Édouard Manet, whose paintings you met on the ground floor, became her brother-in-law.

The picture stayed in the family for more than half a century. Then, in nineteen thirty, the Louvre bought it. And the person who sold it was Blanche. The baby in the cradle, all grown up.

Next, look in the Impressionist rooms on this level for Degas's Dans un café, better known as L'Absinthe. A man and a woman sit side by side at a café table, with a glass of absinthe in front of her. Its track begins there.

## 036 · Degas — L'Absinthe

*Impressionist rooms · Level 5 — In the Impressionist rooms on level 5; the exact room is not confirmed. Look for a man and a woman sitting side by side on a café banquette, a marble table in front of them and a glass of absinthe before her, the picture framed well off-centre.*

The two people in this café knew each other well. Degas painted them as if they'd never met.

She is Ellen Andrée, an actress who also modelled for painters. He is Marcellin Desboutin, a painter and engraver. And the café is real, La Nouvelle Athènes on the place Pigalle, a meeting place for the modern artists of Paris. It stood in the same district Monet had moved to before he painted Saint-Lazare. So these are friends from Degas's own circle, cast in a scene of total loneliness.

Look at her first, sitting on the banquette behind a marble table with a glass of absinthe in front of her. Then look at the two faces. They sit side by side, and yet each of them is sealed off in silence. Their gazes are empty and sad, and nobody is talking.

Now step back and look at the frame. The whole scene is pushed off to one side. There are empty stretches of table, and the man's pipe and hand are cut off at the edge. That kind of off-centre framing came from Japanese prints. Here it does something unsettling, and the museum's phrase for it is "a certain alcoholic imbalance". The picture itself seems to lean.

It may also be a warning against absinthe, a drink that was later prohibited.

Andrée and Desboutin went back to their lives. The painting kept them at that table.

Then go and find a much happier crowd. In the Impressionist rooms on this level, look for Renoir's Bal du moulin de la Galette, a large dance scene full of people, and play its track there.

## 037 · Renoir — Bal du moulin de la Galette

*Impressionist rooms · Level 5 — In the Impressionist rooms on level 5; the room is not confirmed, so look for Renoir's large canvas of a crowded Parisian dance, couples dancing and friends talking around a table.*

This is not a crowd of strangers. Several of the people dancing and talking here were Renoir's own circle, and some of them have names.

Renoir was thirty-five in the spring of 1876, and he painted this mainly on the spot, at the dance itself. Friends helped him carry the big canvas there from his studio on the rue Cortot. He probably finished it back in the studio.

So let's meet a few of them. Find the seated girl in the striped dress. That's Estelle. Her sister Jeanne, a dressmaker of sixteen, posed too. And out on the floor, dancing with the tall painter Solares, is Henriette-Anna Leboeuf, a model very close to Renoir, known as Margot. Rivière, who is in the painting himself, remembered her shaking poor Solares around in a polka, and teaching him Paris slang as they went.

It looks like pure happiness. And it carries a sad fact. Margot died in 1879, only in her early twenties. The young woman dancing at this party had three more years to live.

The painting belonged to Caillebotte, the painter and collector who also bought Monet's station. He left it to the nation, and it was shown with the group the year after it was painted. Rivière wrote that it was "a page of history, a precious monument of Parisian life."

This year, that page turns a hundred and fifty. Everyone on it is gone. The dance is still going.

## 038 · Sisley — La Barque pendant l'inondation, Port-Marly

*Impressionist rooms · Level 5 — In the Impressionist rooms on level 5; the room is not confirmed. Look for a small flood scene: a house with pink and yellow walls standing at an angle, wide sky above and floodwater all around.*

This quiet little flood scene began life in a household that would end up joined to Monet's. Its first owner was a man named Hoschedé, and his wife was called Alice. Years later, Alice Hoschedé married Claude Monet. The picture went its own way, to the collector Isaac de Camondo, who left it to the nation. You'll meet his name again with the cathedrals.

But the picture itself is about water. In the same spring that Renoir was painting his dancers, the Seine climbed out of its bed.

Sisley had moved to Marly-le-Roi two years earlier. When the river flooded Port-Marly in the spring of 1876, he went out to paint it, and not just once. He made six pictures of that flood, and this is one of them.

Look at how he splits the work in two. The sky and the water take up most of the canvas, and he paints them in light, broad strokes. Then the house. It stands at an angle, and its pink and yellow walls are painted with real precision. The boat of the title is out there on the flood.

Everything that moves is loose, and the one thing that stays put is firm. Give it a minute after the noise of the dance. A small picture, and a whole river out of place.

## 039 · Monet — The Rouen Cathedrals

*Room 34 · Level 5 — In room 34 on level 5, in front of Monet's five paintings of the doorway and front of Rouen Cathedral, hung together.*

Claude Monet once had a bad night in Rouen. He wrote about it afterwards. "I had nightmares all night: the cathedral was falling on me, it seemed blue or pink or yellow."

You can see why. For two years running, he stood in front of the same stone front of Rouen Cathedral and painted it, again and again, in every light he could catch. He worked on the spot in eighteen ninety-two and ninety-three, and finished the canvases at home in Giverny. In the end there were about thirty of them. In eighteen ninety-five, his dealer Durand-Ruel showed twenty of them together.

Five of them are here, and the trick is to stop seeing one cathedral and start seeing five different days.

The titles help, because each one is named by its colour, like a piece of music. Start with the two morning canvases. One is a harmony in blue, the doorway in morning sun. The other, with the tower of Saint-Romain beside it, is a harmony in white. Then full sun, a harmony in blue and gold. Then a grey day, a harmony in grey. And one seen straight on, from the front, a harmony in brown.

Now let your eye move from one to the next, and forget the stone, because the stone is the same in all five. What changes is the hour and the weather, and with them every colour on its surface. Monet wasn't really painting a building. He was painting the light that happened to be landing on it.

Four of these came to the nation in the bequest of Isaac de Camondo, the collector you met with Sisley's flood. The brown one was bought from Monet himself, while he was still alive.

A phrase of his sums it up. Tout change, quoique pierre. Everything changes, even stone.

In this same room, find a large square painting of water lilies, Nymphéas bleus. Stand close to it and press play.

## 040 · Monet — Nymphéas bleus (Blue Water Lilies)

*Room 34 · Level 5 — In room 34 on level 5, the same room as the Rouen Cathedrals. Look for a large, square canvas of water lilies, about two metres each way.*

While Monet was wrestling with that cathedral in Rouen, he was also making a pond. In 1893 he created a water garden at his house in Giverny.

For the last part of his life, from the nineteen tens until his death in 1926, that pond was his sole source of inspiration. He had spent years chasing light across a stone front. Now the light came to him, on the water in his own garden.

This canvas is one of those late pictures. It's big, two metres high and almost exactly as wide, and that square shape matters.

Try something. Let your eye look for a place to rest, a bank or a horizon or a patch of sky, and you won't find one. No point in the picture pulls your attention more than any other. There's no edge of the pond, no up or down to hold on to. Just the lilies, the water, and the colour of the title.

The museum describes the effect as a sense of infinity, of limitlessness, and standing this close it's easy to see why. The canvas stops at the frame, but the pond doesn't seem to.

The cathedrals were about one thing changing. Here, there's barely a thing at all.

## 041 · Cézanne — Les Joueurs de cartes (The Card Players)

*Cézanne rooms · Level 5 — In the Cézanne rooms on level 5; the room is not confirmed. Look for a small painting of men playing cards at a table, one of them smoking a pipe.*

Some of the most famous card players in the history of art may have worked for the painter's father.

The museum chooses its word with care. The men are allegedly peasants Cézanne saw at the Jas de Bouffan, his father's property just outside Aix-en-Provence. Cézanne had shown with the Impressionists in Paris. But after their exhibition of 1877, he worked more and more alone in the south, among the people and places he knew. These players come from that world.

One of them is more than a type. The man smoking the pipe has been identified as père Alexandre, old Alexandre, the gardener at the Jas de Bouffan. So somewhere in this picture, the gardener is taking a break.

In the summer of 2025, this little canvas went home for a while. It was lent to the Musée Granet in Aix for an exhibition about the Jas de Bouffan, close to where its sitters came from.

Cézanne painted it in the mid-eighteen nineties, and he came back to the subject more than once. There are five versions of the card players, and this is the smallest. It's less than sixty centimetres wide. You could carry it under one arm.

And yet it doesn't feel small. Stand back a little and look. There's no story here, no winner, no loser, no joke being shared. Just men at a table and a game, painted with the same weight and attention Cézanne gave to apples or a mountain. The gardener gets the seriousness of a portrait.

Close by in the Cézanne rooms, look for his still life of apples and oranges, with draped cloth, earthenware dishes and a flower-patterned jug. Speaking of apples, play its track there.

## 042 · Cézanne — Pommes et oranges (Apples and Oranges)

*Cézanne rooms · Level 5 — In the Cézanne rooms on level 5; the room is not confirmed. Look for a large still life of apples and oranges on dishes, with draped cloth and a flower-patterned jug. Orsay also owns views of the Montagne Sainte-Victoire, which may or may not be hanging nearby.*

For a man who painted fruit, Cézanne was remarkably loyal to his props.

In 1899, in his studio in Paris, he set up a still life. Some earthenware dishes, a jug patterned with flowers, a length of drapery. Then he painted it, and then, with the same props, he painted it again. This is one of six still lifes made from those same few objects.

Why keep the props? Here's a guess you can test with your own eyes. When the objects stay the same, the only things left to change are the arrangement and the painting itself. The subject quietly shifts from the fruit to the way the fruit is put together.

Look at the drapery. It recalls the great Flemish still lifes of the seventeenth century. So Cézanne is borrowing an old, grand way of setting a table, and making it serve a very modern kind of looking.

The museum doesn't hedge on this one. It calls Pommes et oranges "the most important still life produced by the artist in the late 1890s."

So give it the time he gave it. Start with the jug and its flowers, then the dishes, then the fruit, and let the cloth carry you round the table. He looked at these same things six times over. One long look from you seems only fair.

## 043 · Van Gogh in France

*Room 36 · Level 5 — At the entrance to room 36 on level 5, the Van Gogh room, at the end of the Impressionist gallery. You'll know it by the picture rails, painted blue.*

This room announces whose it is before you've seen a single painting. The picture rails, the thin rails the paintings hang from, are painted blue. When the museum rehung its Van Gogh paintings in 2024, it chose that colour to echo the colours Van Gogh used on his canvases. It's a small touch, and a lovely one.

Room 36 is called Vincent van Gogh in France, and it's laid out like a journey. It starts in Paris. Then it goes south, to Arles, and the sun. Then to the hospital at Saint-Rémy-de-Provence, where he still kept painting. And it ends in Auvers-sur-Oise, where he went on Theo's advice to be near a doctor named Paul Gachet.

The doctor has his place here too. Several works on these walls come from the collection Gachet built, which his children later gave to the nation.

Keep one number in mind as you walk. Van Gogh arrived in Auvers on the twenty-first of May, 1890. He died there on the twenty-ninth of July. In those two months, he made about seventy paintings. Roughly one a day.

So as you move along these walls, remember that the last stretch of the journey is the shortest in time, and one of the fullest in paint.

Start in room 36, with a self-portrait. He wears a suit, the background swirls in pale green and turquoise, and his hair and beard are bright orange. Play its track in front of him.

## 044 · Van Gogh — Portrait de l'artiste (Self-Portrait), 1889

*Room 36 · Level 5 — In room 36 on level 5, in front of Van Gogh's head-and-shoulders self-portrait in a suit, against a swirling background of pale green and turquoise, with bright orange hair and beard.*

This face spent decades in a doctor's house.

It came from the collection of Dr Paul Gachet, the doctor who looked after Van Gogh at Auvers-sur-Oise. It stayed in the doctor's family there until 1949, when Gachet's children, Paul and Marguerite, gave it to the nation.

He painted it in 1889, at the hospital in Saint-Rémy-de-Provence. That September, he wrote a line about the problem every self-portrait faces. "It's difficult to know oneself — but it isn't easy to paint oneself either."

So look at how he solved it. He gives himself a plain head-and-shoulders view, in a suit. The features are hard and thin. The eyes are rimmed with green, and they look anxious. The whole picture is tuned to two colours, an absinthe green and a pale turquoise, and against them, the fiery orange of his beard and hair.

Now watch what moves and what doesn't. The man himself is utterly still. But his hair and beard ripple, and the background takes those ripples and spreads them, curling across the whole canvas. The stillness sits in the middle of all that movement.

About two weeks later, writing about a self-portrait from those weeks, he said his "physiognomy has grown much calmer, although the gaze may be vaguer than before."

Antonin Artaud, writing about Van Gogh, described the face in his self-portrait as "inspecting and watching us, scrutinising us with a glowering eye." Stand in front of it for a moment, and let it scrutinise you back.

Somewhere in the Van Gogh rooms, find his bedroom in Arles: a small room with a yellow bed and chairs and a red floor. Play its track there.

## 045 · Van Gogh — La Chambre de Van Gogh à Arles (Van Gogh's Bedroom in Arles)

*Van Gogh rooms · Level 5 — In the Van Gogh rooms on level 5; the exact room is not confirmed. Look for a small painting of a simple bedroom with a yellow bed and chairs, a red floor and pale lilac walls.*

Van Gogh painted his bedroom to get his revenge. That's his word, not mine.

When he first painted this room in Arles, in the autumn of 1888, he wrote to Theo about it. He had been made to rest, and he said he painted it "to take my revenge for the enforced rest that I was obliged to take." Then he explained what he wanted the picture to do. "Looking at the painting should rest the mind, or rather, the imagination."

Look at how he goes about it. Nothing here is subtle. Yellow for the bed and the chairs. Red for the floor. Pale lilac on the walls. He wanted the colour, simplified, to do the work of calm on its own.

This canvas is not that first one, and a larger repeat hangs in Chicago. About a year later, he painted this smaller version for his family in Holland. In his letters that autumn, he talks about sending smaller canvases home, for his mother and his sister, and the bedroom is among them. So this is the room he slept in, shrunk to fit a family wall.

It came into the French national collection in 1959, under the peace treaty with Japan that followed the Second World War.

A painting made to rest the mind, for a mother far away, by a man who had been told to rest, and painted instead.

In room 36, find his night view of a river: deep blue sky and water, stars, and the orange gaslights of the town reflected below. Play its track there, even if the painting is away on loan. The track will tell you where to stand.

## 046 · Van Gogh — La Nuit étoilée (Starry Night over the Rhône)

*Room 36 · Level 5 — In room 36 on level 5, in front of Van Gogh's night view of the Rhône: deep blue sky and water, bright stars, and the orange gaslights of Arles reflected in the river. Note: it is on loan to Tokyo from 14 November 2026 to 28 March 2027; if the wall is empty, listen in front of the 1889 self-portrait in a suit, which hangs in the same room.*

This night sky was not painted from memory. Van Gogh painted it at night, with the stars overhead, by the light of the gas lamps.

If this wall is empty — the painting travels to Tokyo this winter — walk to the self-portrait in a suit, which hangs in this room, and listen there.

It's September 1888, in Arles. Van Gogh writes to Theo that he has painted a starry sky, and that it was actually painted at night, under a gas jet. Then he describes what he saw. "Against the green-blue field of the sky the Great Bear has a green and pink sparkle whose discreet paleness contrasts with the harsh gold of the gaslight."

On the canvas, the blues do most of the work: Prussian blue, ultramarine and cobalt. In the museum's words, he set down the colours he perceived in the dark. The town's gaslights glow an intense orange, and their reflections spill into the Rhône. Above it all, the stars sparkle like gemstones.

And right down at the bottom there's a couple, a pair of lovers, easy to miss. They change the mood. They make the whole night more serene.

The next year, Theo sent the canvas with the Irises to the exhibition of the Indépendants. One critic, Félix Fénéon, called Van Gogh "a diverting colourist even in eccentricities like his Starry Night." A backhanded compliment. It hasn't aged as well as the painting.

Now give the self-portrait a look, whether you're standing at it already or can see it across the room. He painted it a year after that night by the Rhône, at Saint-Rémy. The stars are gone. But the background still moves, in pale green and turquoise, curling round a man who sits completely still.

In room 36, find the church at Auvers, its windows deep blue under a cobalt sky, and listen to its track there.

## 047 · Van Gogh — L'Église d'Auvers-sur-Oise (The Church at Auvers)

*Room 36 · Level 5 — In room 36 on level 5, in front of Van Gogh's painting of the village church at Auvers, seen from its apse end, its windows in deep blue patches under a cobalt sky.*

Standing in front of a church in a French village, Van Gogh found himself thinking of a tower in Holland.

He painted this in the first week of June 1890, soon after arriving in Auvers. And on the fifth of June, in the middle of the work, he described it in a letter. He wrote that it was "almost the same thing as the studies I did in Nuenen of the old tower and the cemetery." Only now, he went on, "the colour is probably more expressive, more sumptuous."

Nuenen was a Dutch village where he had painted years before. Now the same kind of subject came back to him, in new colour. And this is the only painting in which he showed the church at Auvers in full.

After his death, it stayed with Dr Gachet, and then with the doctor's son. In 1952 it came into the national collection. It was not simply a gift. The son supported the purchase, and so did someone who has never been named: an anonymous donor from Canada. A stranger, helping a village church into the museum.

Now look at the windows. In the same letter, Van Gogh says the stained-glass windows "look like ultramarine blue patches," set against "a sky of a deep and simple blue of pure cobalt." So read them as he did. Two blues, one inside the other.

Stay in room 36 and find the portrait of the doctor himself: a melancholy man in cold colours, with a sprig of foxglove. Play its track there.

## 048 · Van Gogh — Le Docteur Paul Gachet

*Room 36 · Level 5 — In room 36 on level 5, the Van Gogh room. Look for Van Gogh's portrait of a melancholy man painted in cold colours, with a sprig of foxglove.*

Dr Paul Gachet had a second life, and a second name. He was an amateur painter and engraver, and he signed that work Paul Van Ryssel.

As a doctor, his speciality was melancholy and nervous illness. He was close to the Impressionists, and bought works by Cézanne, Monet, Renoir and Guillaumin. He was one of Van Gogh's first collectors. So when Van Gogh left the hospital at Saint-Rémy, Theo advised him to go to Auvers, where this doctor could keep an eye on his health.

A special bond grew between the two men. Within days of arriving, Van Gogh made his first and only etching, a portrait of Gachet. The museum still has the copper plate, dated the twenty-fifth of May, 1890.

Then came this painting. Look at the pose, heavy with melancholy, and at the colours, all cold. In a letter to Gauguin, Van Gogh said he had painted Gachet "with the deeply sad expression of our time." Not just one man's sadness. A whole era's.

But find the foxglove. It's a plant doctors valued for its healing properties, and the museum calls it the only touch of hope in the picture. A doctor's flower, for a doctor who was himself, perhaps, a little melancholy.

Long after the doctor's death, his children, Marguerite and Paul, gave his collection to the Louvre, between 1949 and 1954. This portrait, the self-portrait and the church you've just seen all came from that house in Auvers.

Then leave the Van Gogh room for room 38. Look for Seurat's large circus scene, a clown seen from behind in the front, inside a painted dark border. The next story is waiting there.

## 049 · Seurat — Le Cirque

*Room 38 · Level 5 — Room 38 on level 5, the Seurat and Neo-Impressionist room. Look for a large, tall canvas of a circus ring made of tiny touches of colour, with a clown seen from behind in the foreground and a painted blue border.*

Few painters have had worse timing than Georges Seurat. In March 1891 he showed this canvas at the Salon des Indépendants in Paris. A few days after the show opened, he died of diphtheria, at thirty-one.

So Le Cirque, Circus, was never finished, and the museum calls it his artistic testament. It was the third of his big pictures of Paris entertainment after dark, after two others called Parade and Chahut.

Seurat didn't paint by instinct alone. He studied the colour theories of Chevreul and Rood, and the ideas of Charles Henry on how lines and colours act on our feelings. His own name for the method was chromo-luminarism. And in this picture, lines that rise are meant to feel like joy.

So follow them up. In front, a clown with his back to you, standing where Chahut had its musician. Beyond him, a horsewoman and acrobats in the ring. A critic of the day saw the whole thing working towards gaiety, with rising lines and orange in command.

Now look at the edges. Seurat painted a dark border straight onto the canvas, and the flat frame around it is the same blue. The frame is part of the painting.

It never got its finishing touches. But it still does what Seurat asked of it. Everything in it climbs.

For almost a quarter of a century, this canvas belonged to the painter Paul Signac. His own work is just ahead. In room 40, look for his Femme à l'ombrelle, a woman with a parasol built from small separate touches of colour, and press play when you're there.

## 050 · Signac — Femme à l'ombrelle

*Room 40 · Level 5 — Room 40 on level 5, among the Neo-Impressionists. Look for Paul Signac's portrait of a woman with a parasol, painted in small separate touches of colour. More Signac hangs in room 39.*

Paul Signac met the woman in this picture at a cabaret, the Chat Noir, in the early eighteen-eighties. From then on, she lived with him.

Her name was Berthe Roblès, a distant cousin of the painter Camille Pissarro. They married in November 1892, after about ten years together. The next year he painted her here, as Femme à l'ombrelle, Woman with a Parasol.

That alone makes it unusual. Finished portraits are rare in Signac's work. This is the last of a small series of family and friends, which includes the writer Félix Fénéon, now in New York at the Museum of Modern Art.

And the subject is a nod to the generation before. A woman with a parasol was an Impressionist favourite. Monet painted two such studies of a figure out of doors, and both belong to this museum. Signac takes the same idea and does it, as the museum puts it, in a purely Neo-Impressionist manner.

Signac called the method divisionism. The colours go down as separate touches, meant to mix in your eye and stay luminous. Step close and Berthe breaks into colour. Step back and she comes together again. Try it a couple of times. The picture changes with every step you take.

Walk on to room 43, the Pont-Aven room. Look for a life-size young woman lying full length in a wood, Émile Bernard's Madeleine au Bois d'Amour. Her track is waiting for you there.

## 051 · Émile Bernard — Madeleine au Bois d'Amour

*Room 43 · Pont-Aven · Level 5 — In room 43 on level 5, the Pont-Aven room. Look for a large, wide canvas with a life-size young woman lying full length in a wood.*

The painter was twenty. His sister was seventeen. And the painter who led their little group had fallen in love with her.

The painter was Émile Bernard. In 1888 he painted his sister Madeleine life-size, lying in the Bois d'Amour, a wood on the edge of Pont-Aven. The two of them were very close to Paul Gauguin, then forty, at the head of the Impressionist and Synthetist group working there. Gauguin was pushing them to paint in masses and solid areas of colour.

So this isn't quite a girl resting in the woods. The museum calls it a portrait with allegorical overtones. A young woman lying in a place called the Wood of Love. Painted by her brother, in the circle of the man who loved her.

Take in the scale first. She is as big as you are, stretched out across the canvas. Then look at the wood around her. How much of it is an ordinary forest scene, and how much is the kind of painting Gauguin was preaching that year?

Keep the name of that wood in mind. Further on, you'll meet a tiny painting made there the same year, the one that made the Bois d'Amour famous.

Now for Gauguin himself. In this same room, look for La Belle Angèle, a woman's portrait set inside a circle, and play its track there.

## 052 · Gauguin — La Belle Angèle and Les Alyscamps

*Room 43 · Pont-Aven · Level 5 — In room 43 on level 5, the Pont-Aven room. La Belle Angèle is a woman's portrait set inside a circle on a decorative background, with a strange pot beside her. Les Alyscamps is a landscape in bright autumn colours with three small walkers; it may hang nearby.*

Gauguin offered this woman her portrait as a gift. She said no.

Her name was Marie-Angélique Satre. She kept a hotel at Pont-Aven and was thought one of the most beautiful women in the area. Gauguin painted her in 1889 as La Belle Angèle, The Beautiful Angèle. He has set her face inside a circle, on a mostly decorative background, with its forms outlined in a dark line, a method he took from Japanese prints. Beside her stands a pot in Peruvian style, which the museum calls an exotic version of a Breton idol.

But another painter saw a masterpiece. Edgar Degas bought it, and kept it for more than a quarter of a century, until his studio sale.

Now Les Alyscamps, painted a year earlier. In October 1888 Gauguin answered Van Gogh's invitation to Arles, and this is probably one of his first pictures there, in brilliant autumn colours. The Alyscamps was the old Roman burial ground of Arles, later made Christian by its first bishop, Saint Trophime. Gauguin almost leaves the history out. Fields, woods, a canal, and three walkers, two women of Arles and a man.

Gauguin found little charm in the women of Arles. So when he sent this canvas to Van Gogh's brother Theo, he titled it, with some irony, Landscape, or Three Graces at the Temple of Venus.

Tahiti is in room 44, Gauguin in Tahiti. Find Arearea, two women seated near a tree with a red dog in front. If its place is empty, it's away on loan. Play its track anyway, and it will point you to another Gauguin.

## 053 · Gauguin — Arearea

*Room 44 · Gauguin in Tahiti · Level 5 — In room 44 on level 5, Gauguin in Tahiti. Look for a Tahitian scene with two women seated at the centre, a tree cutting across the picture and a red dog in the foreground. On loan to Tokyo from 14 Nov 2026 to 28 Mar 2027 (it may leave the wall earlier): then use Femmes de Tahiti, if it is on display.*

When Paris first saw this painting, it laughed at the dog.

If this wall is empty — Arearea travels to Tokyo this winter — look in this room for Femmes de Tahiti, Tahitian Women, if it is on display, and listen there. You'll know it by its heavy, still women, the one on the right in a strict mission dress.

Gauguin showed Arearea in Paris in November 1893, with the pictures from his first stay in Tahiti. What people noticed was a red dog, and it drew a good deal of sarcasm.

The jokes didn't change his mind, for he thought Arearea one of his best works. In 1895, before he left Europe for good, he bought it back himself.

So picture what he couldn't let go of. Two women seated at the centre, and a tree cutting across the whole scene. The red dog, right in the foreground. And behind them, women worshipping a great statue.

That statue is invented. Gauguin took a small Maori motif, enlarged it to the size of a great Buddha, and imagined a sacred rite around it.

Keep that in mind with everything Gauguin told about Tahiti. His book Noa Noa reads like a memoir, but it's a romanticised account, reworked with Charles Morice. Even the ages he gave for the young Tahitian girls in his life come from his own telling. They should be heard that way, and not as fact.

If you're standing at Femmes de Tahiti, it comes from the start of that same first stay. Look at the women at their simple daily tasks, heavy and still, each in her own space. Their faces are like masks, touched with melancholy. In a later version he swapped the strict mission dress of the woman on the right for a flowered pareo.

He had come, he said, "to live there in ecstasy, calm and art". Arearea is that wish, painted. Perhaps that's why he wanted it back.

## 054 · Gauguin — Le Cheval blanc (The White Horse)

*Room 44 · Gauguin in Tahiti · Level 5 — On level 5 in the Galerie Françoise Cachin, in the Gauguin in Tahiti room, room 44 on the plan, in front of an upright canvas about 1.4 metres high: a pale horse drinking from a stream, with two riders behind it. The room number is inferred from the plan; check nearby if needed.*

This horse, so the story goes, was simply too green.

That is the museum's own account. A pharmacist in Tahiti had ordered the picture from Gauguin. He did not care for its daring colour, and he refused it, because the horse was too green.

Gauguin painted it in eighteen ninety-eight, during his second stay in Tahiti. It ended up with Daniel de Monfreid, and stayed in his collection until it came to the museum in nineteen twenty-seven.

Now look at what the pharmacist turned down. A white horse drinks, standing in a stream that runs straight down the picture. Its coat has taken on the green of the plants around it. Behind it, two nude riders ride bareback into the distance.

This isn't a scene Gauguin happened to see. It is an imagined Tahiti, a synthetic landscape rather than a view from life. The greens go from grass to emerald, set against deep blues. Small notes of orange and pink answer them, and the coppery skin of the riders.

A restoration, finished when these rooms reopened, gave the painting back its vibrant colours.

The horse is a sacred animal here, and it probably carries a meaning. In Polynesia, white was linked with death and with the worship of the gods. The lone horse may be tied to Tahitian beliefs about the soul's passage to another world. Too green for a pharmacist, perhaps. But not for a dream.

## 055 · Gauguin — Oviri

*Galerie Françoise Cachin · Room 44 · Level 5 — On level 5 in the Galerie Françoise Cachin, near the Gauguin paintings of room 44: a standing stoneware figure, about 75 centimetres high, over a dead wolf and a cub. Its exact place in the gallery is not confirmed; check nearby.*

Gauguin had a name for this figure. He called her his Murderess.

He had come to clay through the ceramist Ernest Chaplet, and at first he hoped the pots would earn him money. But during his last stay in Paris, his work in clay came to an end with this, Oviri, his largest piece in clay. It was fired in Chaplet's kiln in December eighteen ninety-four.

Look at what she is doing. She stands over a dead she-wolf, lording it over the body. And she is crushing the life out of the wolf's cub.

Death, savagery and wildness. Those are the themes. The stoneware is only partly glazed, and the figure is about seventy-five centimetres high. It weighs some twenty kilos.

Gauguin could not sell his Murderess. So in nineteen hundred he asked Daniel de Monfreid for her. He meant to put her on his tomb in Tahiti.

A scholar of Gauguin's sculpture, Christopher Gray, saw in this figure an expression of Gauguin's deep disillusionment and discouragement. Stand with her a moment, and you may feel some of that weight.

She came to the museum in nineteen eighty-seven, nearly a century after the kiln.

## 056 · Henri Rousseau — La Charmeuse de serpents (The Snake Charmer)

*Galerie Françoise Cachin · Level 5 — On level 5 in the Galerie Françoise Cachin, near the Gauguin rooms: a large, nearly square jungle scene, almost 1.9 metres wide, with a dark figure playing a flute and snakes around it. Its room is not confirmed. It was on loan until July 2026; if it is not on show, skip this track and go on to room 45.*

This jungle was grown in Paris, in a greenhouse.

The picture travels, though. If it isn't on the wall, go straight on to room 45 and Sérusier's Le Talisman.

Henri Rousseau did not make his jungles from travel. He made them from studies at the Natural History Museum, and in the hothouses of the Jardin des Plantes. Out of those, he built this.

It began as a commission. Robert Delaunay's mother ordered it after a trip to India. Delaunay was one of Rousseau's most fervent admirers. So were Jarry, Apollinaire, Picasso and Breton.

Now look. A dark-skinned figure, neither clearly man nor woman, plays a flute. The snakes answer, and so does the whole natural world around. The museum's commentary likens the figure to a black Eve in an unsettling Garden of Eden, charming a snake every bit as frightening as the serpent of Genesis was seductive.

See how the colour is lit from behind, bright and dense. One part of the picture is very bright, the other very dark. The drawing is precise and naïve at once. And notice how figure, animals and fantastic plants all get exactly the same treatment.

That strangeness carried a long way. The backlit colours seem to look ahead to Magritte. The painting went on to inspire whole generations of artists, the Surrealists above all.

The collector Jacques Doucet owned it, alongside Picasso's Demoiselles d'Avignon. It came to the national collections as his bequest, in nineteen thirty-six.

## 057 · Sérusier — Le Talisman

*Room 45 · Redon and the Nabis · Level 5 — In room 45 on level 5, Redon and the Nabis, after the Gauguin rooms. Look for a very small landscape painted on a wooden panel, about the size of a sheet of paper.*

Some of the most consequential paintings in this museum are huge. This one is not. It's a little panel of wood, twenty-seven centimetres by twenty-one, about the size of a sheet of paper.

It was painted in October 1888 by Paul Sérusier, in the Bois d'Amour at Pont-Aven. Yes, the same wood where Émile Bernard laid his sister down life-size back in room 43. Sérusier painted it under Gauguin's direction, the same advice about masses of colour you heard with Bernard's Madeleine.

We know of that lesson through the later account of the painter Maurice Denis. You'll meet his own work on level two, among the Nabis.

Its other title, L'Aven au Bois d'Amour, names a real place. Look at how Sérusier has built that place, patch by patch. Then see how much of the view is left, and how much is simply colour.

This little panel became the founding work of the group called the Nabis. It also made the Bois d'Amour famous. Not bad for something you could slip into a coat pocket.

## 058 · Odilon Redon — Les Yeux clos (Closed Eyes)

*Room 45 · Redon and the Nabis · Level 5 — In room 45 on level 5, Redon and the Nabis, in the Galerie Françoise Cachin: a small painting, 44 × 36 cm, of a head and shoulders with closed eyes, floating in a soft, undefined space. The room number is inferred from the plan; check nearby if needed.*

She has closed her eyes. And that, for Odilon Redon, was the whole point.

The face is probably his wife, Camille Falte, remade from an earlier drawing. But it's really a picture of what happens behind the eyelids. Dream, absence, an inner world, perhaps an apparition. Redon wrote about these things in his journal, later published as À soi-même, To Myself. There he spoke of the strange charm of "closed eyes".

Look at how little paint there is. It's very thin, almost immaterial, and you can see the grain of the support coming through. The head and shoulders seem to float, in a space with no edges.

Redon painted it in eighteen ninety, when he was about fifty. Behind the face are older models. Italian Renaissance busts, the marbles of Francesco Laurana above all. And Michelangelo's Dying Slave in the Louvre, a sculpture that deeply moved him. Today it is called an icon of Symbolism in painting.

It was also a first. In nineteen-oh-four, the director of the Musée du Luxembourg, Léonce Bénédite, chose it from Redon's studio. It was the first work by Redon to enter the national collections.

Redon also links back to the Gauguin rooms. After Gauguin died in the Marquesas, Redon very probably painted him in tribute, in profile, dark against a medallion, like a funeral relief or an ancient medal, with the long hair and the "Inca" nose Gauguin was proud of. In an article that same year, he praised Gauguin as refined and savage, grandiose and delicate. Look for that portrait nearby, if it's on show.

## 059 · Le cabaret du Chat noir — the shadow theatre

*Room 46 · Le cabaret du Chat noir · Level 5 — In room 46 on level 5, Le cabaret du Chat noir, after the Redon and Nabis room. Look for the flat silhouettes cut from zinc, once used in the cabaret's shadow theatre. Which figures are on show changes; check on site.*

At the foot of Montmartre, a showman once ran a cabaret called the Black Cat, Le Chat Noir.

His name was Rodolphe Salis. He created the place, ran it and played its host. Creators from every horizon met there.

The Chat Noir became famous for three things. First, its own journal, now seen as a key link in the history of comic strips. Second, the chanson of the chansonniers, which really was born there. And third, the shadow theatre.

Salis handed that theatre to Henri Rivière in eighteen eighty-six. Rivière was twenty-two. He took the technique as far as it could go, with forty-five plays.

What you see here are the actors. Flat figures cut from zinc, and painted. Behind a screen, stagehands slid them along while light cast them onto the screen. Coloured filters gave the backdrops their colour. Texts were read to music, and there were even effects of wind and smoke.

Rivière photographed his own backstage. The museum holds his pictures of stagehands sliding zinc figures for L'Épopée, a show by Caran d'Ache. In March eighteen eighty-nine, he went up the Eiffel Tower with friends from the Chat Noir, and photographed it from the top.

Light, a screen, moving images and an audience in the dark. It's hard not to see what was coming next.

## 060 · The birth of cinema, 1895–1914

*Room 47 · Cinema 1895–1914 · Level 5 — In room 47 on level 5, Cinéma 1895–1914, next to the Chat Noir room. A museum of the nineteenth century, and it ends here, with moving pictures.

Ever since this museum opened, it has kept a room for the birth of the cinematograph, in eighteen ninety-five. The shadow theatre next door was one step on the way. This room is where the road arrives.

Look at the screens. There are three, and between them they show about fifteen films or excerpts, from those first years up to nineteen fourteen. Step in front of one and listen. ## 062 · The Salle des fêtes — the hotel ballroom

*Room 51 · Salle des fêtes · Level 2 — In room 51 on level 2, the Salle des fêtes. Stand in the middle of the room with its mirrors and chandeliers, and look up at the painted panels high on the walls.*

This museum was once a railway station. And this room was the ballroom of its hotel.

The architect Laloux wrapped the station on two sides with a luxury hotel of four hundred rooms. It had a restaurant, and it had this, a salle des fêtes. Station and hotel opened together on the fourteenth of July, nineteen hundred, for the great Universal Exhibition.

Look around. Mirrors. Chandeliers. It's meant to make you think of life in a château, and of the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles. A dance floor fit for a king, a short walk from the platforms.

The décor borrows from every style of French classicism, from Louis the Fourteenth to Louis the Fifteenth. Stone, plaster, ornamental cast iron. And up on the walls, the paintings of Pierre Fritel, ordered by the railway company itself, the Compagnie des chemins de fer d'Orléans. Four panels on the east and west walls have sloping sides, with themes such as Music, Poetry and Dance. Four triangles sit on the north and south walls, one of them Summer.

This room is something rare here. When the museum was designed, period rooms were left out. The ballroom was the one exception. It is an authentic room of the old hotel, not reconstructed, but restored.

It has known more than dances. On the nineteenth of May, nineteen fifty-eight, General de Gaulle held a press conference here, in the hotel's salle des fêtes. Around five hundred journalists came. He had just said he was ready to take on the powers of the Republic, and many people feared a dictatorship. As it is usually reported, he asked them why they would want him, at sixty-seven, to start a career as a dictator. That press conference opened his return to power.

The hotel closed for good on the first of January, nineteen seventy-three. The ballroom stayed.

## 063 · Gustave Moreau — Orphée

*Room 59 · Symbolism · Level 2 — Room 59 on level 2, the Symbolism room at the west end of the floor; the painting's own page gives room 57, so look next door if it is not here. Look for an upright painting on wood of a girl in rich Oriental dress, holding a man's head that rests on a lyre, in front of a twilight landscape.*

Two faces, both with their eyes closed. One belongs to a living girl, and the other to a dead poet. And they look strangely alike.

This is Gustave Moreau's Orphée, Orpheus, painted in 1865 on a wooden panel. In the myth, the poet Orpheus was killed by the Maenads. Moreau doesn't show the killing, only what comes after. A young Thracian girl, dressed in Oriental finery, holds the poet's head, resting on his lyre. She gazes down at it with a melancholy air.

Look at the two faces together. Strangely similar, with their closed eyes, they seem absorbed in infinite contemplation. It's a gruesome subject, and yet the scene is calm, mysteriously free of morbidity.

Then let your eye travel past them. Behind the figures there's a twilight glow, over a fantastic landscape that the museum calls worthy of Leonardo da Vinci. Moreau gives you a long way to look, and very little noise.

He showed the painting at the Salon of 1866. That July, the State bought it from him, straight from the Salon. And it did more than sell. It established Moreau as a decisive figure in Symbolism.

In other hands, a severed head and a lyre would make a horror story. Moreau turned them into a silence.

## 064 · Camille Claudel — L'Âge mûr

*Room 55 · History painting · Level 2 — Room 55 on level 2, the history painting room at the west end of the floor. Look for a bronze group of three figures on a long base: an older man drawn away by an older woman, and a young woman on her knees, reaching after him.*

Her brother Paul said that what you are watching here is a woman having her soul torn out of her.

This bronze was made by Camille Claudel, after her break with Auguste Rodin. After the break-up, Rodin tried to help her, through an intermediary, and in 1895 he obtained a State commission for this group.

But the commission went nowhere. The bronze was never ordered, and the plaster was never delivered. In the end, the money came from a private patron, a Captain Tissier. He commissioned the first bronze in 1902. The group in front of you is that first bronze, cast in her lifetime.

Now look at the three figures. The museum reads the group as Rodin's hesitation between two women. One is his former mistress, the woman who was to prevail. She draws the man away. The other is Claudel herself, bent forward, trying to hold him. Claudel called that kneeling figure l'Implorante, the one who begs.

Follow the man's arm. He is at the end of his maturity, drawn away by age, and he holds out a useless hand towards youth. And notice the flying draperies. They make the whole group move faster than bronze has any right to.

Paul Claudel put it like this. "My sister Camille, beseeching, humiliated on her knees, this superb, this proud woman, and do you know what is being torn from her, at this very moment, before your eyes, it is her soul."

In 1913, aged forty-eight, Camille Claudel was interned. She never came out. She died thirty years later. But here, in bronze, the kneeling woman is still reaching out.

## 065 · Vuillard — Jardins publics

*Room 72 · Level 2 — Room 72 on level 2, the room with five large garden panels in beige, green and blue hung around a corner. Bonnard's tall, narrow panel of a woman in a cape hangs in the same room.*

Picture a dinner invitation in 1894, to a mansion on the Avenue du Bois, the avenue Parisians now call Avenue Foch. Your host is Alexandre Natanson. He runs La Revue blanche, the journal that published the Nabis. And around his room, a cycle of painted gardens.

That year Natanson commissioned nine panels from Édouard Vuillard, for a large room used for both living and dining. They're called Jardins publics, Public Gardens. In 1929 the set was broken up. Five of the nine are reunited here, hung around the corner of the room, as they were originally. So you're standing, more or less, in a corner of that lost room.

Each panel is its own scene with its own subtitle: girls playing, a questioning, nursemaids, a conversation, a red parasol. Notice too how matt the surface is, almost like a fresco. Vuillard painted in distemper, a paint bound with glue, and this was the first time he used it on this scale.

## 066 · Vallotton — Le Ballon

*Room 71 · Level 2 — Room 71 on level 2. Look for a small, wide painting of a park seen from above: a broad stretch of ochre ground, dark trees, and a child in a yellow hat chasing a red ball.*

Most of this painting is empty ground. And that's exactly where its power comes from.

Félix Vallotton was Swiss, and from 1891 he was in close touch with the Nabis, the group you met in the last room. He painted Le Ballon, The Ball, in 1899, in oil on cardboard, later mounted on wood. It's small, barely more than half a metre wide. Yet the museum counts it among the best known of all his paintings. It came to the national collections in 1953, in the bequest of the collector Carle Dreyfus.

Notice first where Vallotton has put you. You're looking down on a park from above, a bird's-eye view. A broad stretch of ochre ground fills much of the picture. Against it presses the deep shade of the trees.

And then, in all that space, one small child. Find the yellow hat with its red ribbon, the blonde hair, the orange boots. A white smock blows out behind as the child runs. A dark shadow goes on ahead. And the child is chasing a red ball.

The child is a bright patch of light in a great flat field of colour. Nothing else in the park competes for your eye. It's a game. Yet the longer you look, the larger the empty ground becomes, and the smaller the child.

Vallotton doesn't tell you what to feel about that. He just leaves the space there.

## 067 · Maurice Denis — Les Muses

*Room 70 · Level 2 — Room 70 on level 2, in front of a tall canvas of women in the dress of the 1890s, gathered among the straight trunks of great chestnut trees.*

In June 1893, a young painter called Maurice Denis married a woman named Marthe. That same year he finished this canvas. And she is in it.

Denis was one of the Nabis, the painters of the little Talisman you saw upstairs. This is what their ideas could look like a few years on, at full size.

The subject is the Muses of classical myth. But Denis leaves antiquity behind. His Muses are women in the dress of his own day, gathered on a terrace. And the place is real. It's the terrace at Saint-Germain-en-Laye, where Denis lived all his life, under chestnut trees already a century old.

Look at the tree trunks first. They rise straight through the picture and set its beat, like the bars of a piece of music. The museum speaks of the rhythm and decorative power they give the whole scene. The women are fitted in between them.

Then find the group of three women seated in the foreground. One of them is Marthe, the new bride. She went on inspiring his art until her death. So this is a painting of the Muses. It is also a portrait of his bride, the year they married.

## 068 · Toulouse-Lautrec — Jane Avril dansant

*Room 68 · Level 2 — Room 68 on level 2, the Toulouse-Lautrec room, « spectacles et coulisses ». It is a tall, narrow picture on cardboard of a woman dancing alone, painted in thin, quick strokes, with two figures behind her.*

Jane Avril discovered she could dance in a hospital.

She was born Jeanne Beaudon in 1868, and her childhood was hard, with a mother who beat her. As a girl she suffered from chorea, a nervous illness that brings convulsive movements, and at fourteen she was interned at the Salpêtrière hospital in Paris.

Then came a masked ball there, at mid-Lent. A waltz began, and she was carried away by it. She danced, and the others made a circle around her and applauded. In the museum's words, that was the night her vocation was born.

She went on to dance at the Moulin Rouge, the Jardin de Paris and the Folies-Bergère. Contemporaries called her "the incarnation of dance". In 1911, at forty-two, she married the poster artist Maurice Biais, and left the stage for a quiet life at Jouy-en-Josas.

Now look at how Lautrec paints her. The picture is tall and narrow, on cardboard, and the paint is thin. He diluted his oils with turpentine, so they flow, and in broad strokes he set down only the essentials of her movement. It feels less like a portrait than a glimpse.

Behind her stand two figures. One is an unknown woman in a hat. The other is the impresario Warner, who also posed for one of Lautrec's best-known prints, The Englishman at the Moulin Rouge.

But your eye keeps going back to her. The girl from the Salpêtrière ball, still dancing, in a few thin strokes.

## 069 · Toulouse-Lautrec — La Clownesse Cha-U-Kao and La Goulue

*Room 68 · Level 2 — Room 68 on level 2, the Toulouse-Lautrec room. Cha-U-Kao is a small picture on cardboard of a woman clown in a private room, fastening a large yellow ruffle, with green walls and a red sofa. La Goulue's two monumental booth panels may not be shown in this room.*

Her stage name sounds Japanese. It's actually a pun.

Cha-U-Kao was a dancer and clown at the Nouveau Cirque and the Moulin Rouge. Say her name aloud and you hear two French words. Chahut, a wild acrobatic dance that grew out of the cancan, and chaos, the uproar when she came on stage.

Lautrec doesn't show her performing. He shows her in a private room, struggling to fasten a large yellow ruffle to the top of her costume. The ruffle almost fills the picture. A yellow ribbon ties up her white toupee, almost ironically, the museum says. Green walls, a red sofa, and quick brushstrokes everywhere.

Now look above the little table. In a mirror, or perhaps a portrait, there's an elderly man. He could be a close friend, an admirer or a customer.

The other great star of Lautrec's Paris was La Goulue. In 1895 she wanted her own booth at the Foire du Trône, the Paris funfair, and Lautrec painted two monumental panels for its entrance. On one, she dances at the Moulin Rouge with Valentin le Désossé, the pair who made those dances famous. On the other, she performs the so-called Moorish dance she offered inside.

Those panels stood outside in the weather until a collector rescued them. Then a dealer cut them into pieces to sell. In 1930 the State put them back together. They aren't always shown beside Cha-U-Kao, so if you don't see them here, keep the story with you.

## 070 · Rodin — Balzac

*Terrasse Rodin · Level 2 — On the Terrasse Rodin at the east end of level 2, by room 67, overlooking the nave: Rodin's towering plaster figure of the writer Balzac, almost three metres high.*

A toad in a sack. A block of salt caught in a shower. A menhir. A snowman. Those are some of the things critics called this statue when Paris first saw it.

The story starts well. In 1891, Émile Zola, the writer whose portrait by Manet you may have seen downstairs, had just been elected president of the Société des gens de lettres, the writers' society. The society wanted a monument to Honoré de Balzac, almost half a century after his death. Zola chose Rodin.

Seven years later, Rodin showed this plaster at the Salon of 1898, and the critics poured scorn on what they called a formless block. The society refused it. It had asked for a realistic likeness, in the usual commemorative style, and Rodin had given it neither. The commission went to another sculptor, Falguière.

What Rodin did next tells you how much he believed in it. He kept the statue. He gave back the money. And he refused every offer to buy it.

Now look up at the whole figure, almost three metres of plaster. You can see what upset the critics. There's no tidy portrait of an author at his desk. There's a single great mass, as if the statue were still wrapped, and Balzac's head at the top of it.

And how did Rodin get that mass? François Pompon, whose polar bear waits further along the terraces, left an account. According to him, Rodin dipped his dressing gown in a big tub of plaster, and used it to clothe his study for the figure. That is Pompon's memory of it. But stand here for a minute, and it's not hard to imagine.

The monument did reach the street in the end. A bronze was unveiled on the boulevard Raspail in 1939, forty-one years after the Salon. The snowman, it turns out, never melted.

## 071 · Guimard and Majorelle — Art Nouveau

*Room 64 · Level 2 — Room 64 on level 2, among the Art Nouveau furniture. Look for Guimard's tall wooden smoking-room bench with a leg like a branching tree trunk, and Majorelle's Water Lilies bed with its gilt bronze.*

If you've travelled on the Paris Métro, you may already have walked under the work of the man behind this room. Hector Guimard designed the Métro entrances. The company turned to him after a competition that had gone nowhere. The museum even owns one of his entrances, from Montparnasse-Bienvenüe station.

Guimard was a leader of French Art Nouveau, and its central idea was simple. Architecture, furniture and decoration should be one single thing. For his Castel Béranger, in the sixteenth arrondissement, he designed every piece of furniture himself.

Now find his smoking-room bench, from 1897, in jarrah wood and carved metal. It stands over two and a half metres high. It isn't symmetrical. And one leg seems to be a tree trunk, sprouting supple branches that curve like vines. A bench, growing out of the floor like a tree.

Then look for the work of Louis Majorelle, from Nancy. He had enormous success with water lilies at the Universal Exposition of 1900. Here they are again, on a bed called Nénuphars, Water Lilies, in mahogany with marquetry and gilt bronze. Furniture, in other words, that blooms.

Guimard wanted no line at all between the building, the bench and the chair. In this room you can see what happens when designers take that idea seriously.

Next door, in rooms 63 and 65, look for a glass hand rising out of the sea, and a huge carved bookcase. Play their track there.

## 072 · Gallé and Carabin's bookcase

*Rooms 63 and 65 · Level 2 — Rooms 63 and 65 on level 2. In room 63, a glass hand rising among shells and seaweed. In room 65, a walnut and wrought-iron bookcase almost three metres high, covered in carved figures.*

Some artists know when they are making their last work. Émile Gallé, the glassmaker from Nancy, seems to have known.

In room 63, look for a hand rising out of the sea, made of glass. It's called La Main aux algues et aux coquillages, Hand with Seaweed and Shells, and it dates from 1904. It is very likely the last piece of crafted glass the master from Nancy produced. He made it knowing he was about to die. That year he showed it in Nancy, in a showcase called The Deep Sea. The museum reads it as a farewell, confident in the radiant existence that awaited him.

Look closely at the two textures. The shells are rendered just as they are in nature. The hand isn't. Its gelatinous surface makes it look less like human skin than like the body of a jellyfish.

Now for something louder. In room 65, find François-Rupert Carabin's Bibliothèque, a bookcase almost three metres high, in walnut and wrought iron. And it isn't really furniture. It's a sermon.

The critic Gustave Geffroy explained how to read it. At the bottom lie the base passions, the enemies of intelligence, beaten by the Book. On one side sits Ignorance. On the other, a stack of masks: Vanity, Greed, Intemperance, Anger, Folly and Hypocrisy. At the top stand three figures, with Truth in the centre and Reading on either side.

So the vices end up on the floor, and Truth keeps watch over the books.

Then go back out onto the sculpture terrace overlooking the nave, and find François Pompon's great stone polar bear. Play its track there, and take your time.

## 073 · Pompon — Ours blanc

*Sculpture terrace · Level 2 — On the level-2 sculpture terrace overlooking the nave. Look for a large stone polar bear, about two and a half metres long.*

François Pompon became famous at sixty-seven.

Until then he was one of the most sought-after practitioners in Paris, carving marble for other sculptors. Among them were Rodin, and Camille Claudel, whose bronze you saw in room 55. Then, from 1905, he turned against Rodin's expressive style, and away from the human figure altogether. He went to the Jardin des Plantes in Paris and watched the animals.

His polar bear was first shown in plaster at the Salon d'Automne of 1922. It gave him his first public success. This version is in stone, about two and a half metres long. The State commissioned it in 1927, and his own practitioner, Jean-Joachim Supéry, carved it at Malakoff. So the man who had carved for Rodin now had someone carving for him.

Pompon described his method like this. "I keep a large number of details destined to disappear. I do the animal with almost all its trappings. And then little by little, I eliminate…"

Take a slow walk along the bear with that in mind. What did he take away? And how much bear is still left?

And yes, this is the same Pompon behind the dressing-gown story you heard at Rodin's Balzac.

Stay on the sculpture terraces and look for a seated woman in marble by Maillol, and Bourdelle's gilded bronze archer drawing his bow. Play their track there.

## 074 · Maillol and Bourdelle — Méditerranée and Héraklès archer

*Sculpture terrace · Level 2 — On the level-2 sculpture terrace overlooking the nave. Look for Maillol's seated woman in marble, and Bourdelle's large gilded bronze of Hercules drawing a bow.*

When this seated woman first appeared in public, she didn't have a grand title. At the Salon d'Automne of 1905, she was simply called Woman, and she caused a stir.

Aristide Maillol made her for his German patron, Count Harry Kessler. The name she carries now, Méditerranée, The Mediterranean, came later. This marble is a copy made in Maillol's own lifetime, in the nineteen twenties.

Compare her with Rodin's Balzac, back on the Terrasse Rodin. Rodin gives you a storm. Maillol turned away from that expressive style, towards formal beauty and simplicity. No story, no drama, no name, just a seated woman and a block of calm. Walk slowly around her, and notice how little she moves.

Now turn to the opposite answer. Antoine Bourdelle's Héraklès, Hercules the Archer, dates from 1909. Its full French title tells you the myth. The hero is shooting the man-eating birds of Lake Stymphalus. It's gilded bronze, nearly two and a half metres high and just as wide.

Look at the bow, drawn tight, and then at the open air beyond it, where the arrow is about to fly. All that weight of bronze is gathered into one moment of tension.

Here are two ways to make a body speak in sculpture. One sits perfectly still. The other is a split second from letting go.

For the last stop, stay on the terrace and find a spot at the balustrade where you can look down over the whole nave. I'll meet you there for the final track.

## 075 · Out into Paris

*Level 2 terrace → exit — On the level-2 sculpture terrace, at the balustrade, looking down the length of the nave towards Rodin's Gates of Hell at the far end.*

In 1900, when this building was brand new, the painter Édouard Detaille wrote a line about it. "The station is superb and looks like a Palais des beaux-arts…" He meant a palace of fine arts. It took most of the century for him to be proved right.

Look down from the terrace. Down there, where the sculptures stand along the central aisle, the trains once pulled in. And look along the terraces themselves. On both sides of the nave, they hold sculpture from about 1880 to 1910.

And now you know all three floors. You began down there, on the ground floor, with the academic painters, Millet and Courbet, and Manet's Olympia. You climbed to the top, under the roof, to the Impressionists, Van Gogh and Gauguin. And you came down here, to the Nabis, Art Nouveau, Claudel and Rodin's Balzac.

Before you go, look all the way to the far end of the nave. Between the two towers stands Rodin's Gates of Hell, facing the whole length of the old station. You stood in front of it hours ago, at the end of the ground floor. From up here, you can take it in with everything else at once. Detaille only saw a station that looked like a palace. You've just walked through the palace.

Thank you for spending these hours with me. I hope a few of these faces come home with you: a black cat, a little dancer, a man in a sack of plaster, a girl holding a poet's head.

When you're ready, take the escalators or the stairs down to level 0. Walk back along the nave to the clock end, where you came in, and follow the SORTIE signs out into Paris. Goodbye, and enjoy the city.
