# Yo Tours · Real Alcázar de Sevilla · audio guide scripts (EN) · 30 Oct 2026 edition

## Opening

### 001 · Welcome to the Real Alcázar
*Outside · Puerta del León · 336 words*

**Where:** Outside the Puerta del León on the Plaza del Triunfo, the main visitor entrance: a battlemented wall with an arched gateway, and a tile panel of a lion above the arch. Stand clear of the entry line.

**What:** Real Alcázar de Sevilla, Puerta del León (Lion Gate), 14th-century gateway of Pedro I's time with a later tile lion; overview of the site and of how this guide works.

Above this gate, a lion made of tiles keeps watch. It gives the entrance its name, the Puerta del León, the Lion Gate. And behind it is a palace that has been a seat of power for eleven centuries.

The word alcázar comes from the Arabic al-qasr, the palace or fortress. In 913, Abd al-Rahman the Third, then emir of Córdoba, ordered a new seat of government here. Ruler after ruler built on this ground, and much of their work survives side by side. You will see part of an Almohad courtyard, a Gothic palace from the thirteenth century, and King Pedro's Mudéjar palace from the fourteenth. Then come Renaissance gardens, and gardens laid out in the early twentieth century.

It is still a royal palace. The Alcázar calls itself the oldest royal palace still in use in Europe, and its upper floor is the Royal Family's official residence in Seville. Together with the Cathedral and the Archivo de Indias, it is a World Heritage Site.

Now, how this guide works. Almost every stop has a number. These are the numbers on the Alcázar's own map, 1 to 53, and the route map that comes with this guide uses the same numbers. The signs on site use the Spanish names, so you will always hear the number with its Spanish name.

There are three lengths. The full tour takes about two hours, the essential version about an hour and a quarter, and the highlights about forty-five minutes, all with walking. Whichever version is on your phone, play it in order. The numbers may jump, and every stop tells you where to go next. Some stops have a short Extra, and we will always say so first. The Cuarto Real Alto, the upper royal rooms, is a separate timed visit, with two short optional tracks at the end of this guide.

Through the gate, into number 1 on the Alcázar map, the Patio del León: the courtyard just inside, with a triple arch at its far end.

## The first courtyards

### 002 · 1 · Patio del León — The Lion Gate and a lost theatre
*No. 1 · Patio del León · 318 words*

**Where:** Just inside the Lion Gate, in the Patio del León: an open courtyard with four garden beds, between the entrance wall and the far wall, which is pierced by a triple arch leading on to the larger courtyard beyond.

**What:** Puerta del León and the León–Montería portal: gate, wall and tower casing of one building campaign under Pedro I, 1350s (radiocarbon centred on 1368); later tile lion (maker and date not established); site of the Corral de la Montería theatre, 1620s–1691; largely cleared 1936–37 to designs by Juan Talavera.

For more than sixty years, this courtyard was a theatre. People crowded in here to watch plays, until a fire in 1691 brought the show to an end.

First, the gate you just came through. It was long taken to be Almohad, from the twelfth century. Archaeology says otherwise. Miguel Ángel Tabales and his team found that the gate, the wall beside it and the casing of its tower went up in a single campaign, in the time of King Pedro the First, in the middle of the fourteenth century. A radiocarbon sample from the wall points the same way.

The name came later. For centuries this was the Puerta de la Montería, the gate of the royal hunt. People began calling it the Lion Gate after the tile lion over the arch. Who made that lion, and when, has not been established.

Inside, the courtyard had a second life. From the 1620s a playhouse stood here, the Corral de la Montería, one of the city's theatres. In May 1691 a fire broke out in the stables next door and destroyed it. Stables, storerooms and houses followed. In the 1930s, to designs by the architect Juan Talavera, most of them were cleared away to make the entrance you used today.

Now look at the triple arch that leads on. Its three openings are not evenly set out. The middle arch has been shifted to one side, which is why one side of the wall is wider than the other. One reading is that this was deliberate, to line up a direct view from the Lion Gate to the door of Pedro's new palace.

So the first thing you walked through is not a relic of the Almohads. It is a king's work, perhaps aimed at his own front door.

Stay in number 1, the Patio del León, for a short look at eleven centuries before we go in.

### 003 · 1 · Patio del León — Eleven centuries on one site
*No. 1 · Patio del León · 266 words*

**Where:** Still in the Patio del León, the first courtyard inside the Lion Gate, with the triple arch leading on.

**What:** A timeline of the Alcázar: 913 (Abd al-Rahman III), the Abbadid palace al-Mubarak, the Almohad residence, 1248, Alfonso X's Gothic palace, Pedro I's palace, the Casa de la Contratación, the 1526 wedding, the 19th-century Bourbon rooms, the 1931 cession to the city, the royal residence today.

Eleven centuries is a lot to carry round a palace. Here is the short version.

It begins in 913. Abd al-Rahman the Third, then emir of Córdoba and later its first caliph, ordered a new seat of government here. In the eleventh century, Seville's own Abbadid kings had a palace here called al-Mubarak, the Blessed. The oldest wall archaeologists have found on the site belongs to that century, and it may be Almoravid.

Late in the twelfth century the Almohads built a residence here. Part of it survives a few steps away.

In 1248, after a siege of more than a year, Fernando the Third of Castile took Seville. His son, Alfonso the Tenth, called the Wise, built a Gothic palace. In the middle of the fourteenth century King Pedro the First began the palace at the heart of this visit. The Catholic Monarchs set up their House of Trade for the Americas here. Charles the Fifth was married here. And in the nineteenth century, Bourbon monarchs added and refurbished the rooms upstairs.

In 1931 the new Republic handed the Alcázar to the city of Seville, which still runs it through its own board, the Patronato. Yet the upper floor remains the King's official residence when he is in Seville.

So as you walk, ask of each wall: whose is this? The answer is usually more than one name.

Number 2 on the Alcázar map, the Sala de la Justicia, opens off the far left-hand corner of this courtyard as you face the triple arch: a small square hall with a fountain set in its floor.

### 004 · 2 · Sala de la Justicia — Alfonso XI's hall
*No. 2 · Sala de la Justicia · 225 words*

**Where:** Inside the Sala de la Justicia, off the far left-hand corner of the Patio del León as you face the triple arch: a small square hall with plaster-covered walls and a fountain set into the middle of the floor.

**What:** Sala de la Justicia, a square Mudéjar hall built at the time of Alfonso XI of Castile, 14th century (around the 1340s); plasterwork with plant motifs and heraldic shields; floor fountain.

In 1340, at the Río Salado, Alfonso the Eleventh of Castile and Afonso the Fourth of Portugal defeated a great Marinid army from Morocco. It was the Marinids' last attempt to invade the peninsula, and the last time Castile and Portugal fought them side by side.

This hall was built in Alfonso's time, in the fourteenth century, possibly to mark that victory. Nobody can prove the link. But the room tells another story, and that one is certain.

Alfonso was a Christian king, and he was Pedro's father. Yet look at what he chose: a square hall in the Islamic tradition, its walls covered in plasterwork of plant-like patterns and heraldic shields. The city's own description calls it the earliest Mudéjar building in the Alcázar. Mudéjar is the word for this mix: Islamic forms of art, made for Christian patrons. So a Castilian king's hall speaks the artistic language of al-Andalus.

Then look down, at the fountain set into the floor. Water in the middle of a room, a piece of garden brought indoors.

Within a generation, his son Pedro would take this taste and build a whole palace in it. This small room is where that story starts.

Through the far side of this hall to number 3 on the Alcázar map, the Patio del Yeso: a lattice of arches over a long pool.

### 005 · 3 · Patio del Yeso — What the Almohads left
*No. 3 · Patio del Yeso · 292 words*

**Where:** In the Patio del Yeso, reached only through the Sala de la Justicia: a small courtyard with a long pool, small garden beds beside it, and a portico of arches carrying a lattice of interlaced plaster arches.

**What:** Patio del Yeso, part of an Almohad residence of about 1190 (the Islamic "Palacio del Yeso"); sebka lattice; residence of Pedro I in 1358; rediscovered 1855; restored 1918–20 and 1969–71.

King Pedro lived in this courtyard before his famous palace was ready. In 1358 this was his residence. And even then, it was old.

This is what remains of an Almohad residence from around 1190. The Almohads were a Berber dynasty from North Africa who ruled al-Andalus from the twelfth century into the early thirteenth. They also built the minaret we now call the Giralda. The Alcázar's own map simply calls this the Palacio del Yeso, from the Islamic period.

Most of their palace is gone. Later palaces swept much of it away. But this courtyard survived. Forgotten for centuries, it was rediscovered in 1855, and restored twice in the twentieth century.

Now look at the portico. Above the arches, a net of interlaced arches climbs the wall, each one flowing into the next. This pattern is called sebka. It is the same family of ornament you can see high on the Giralda. Below it, a long pool fills most of the courtyard, with a small garden area on each side.

The richness here is all in the pattern itself, repeated until the wall seems woven.

Picture Pedro here, a young Christian king of Castile, living under Almohad arches. Soon his own palace, a few steps away, would speak the same language. The Alcázar's school notes call this courtyard a forerunner of the Mudéjar style. You are standing in the first draft.

Back the way you came, through number 2, the Sala de la Justicia. The triple arch of number 1, the Patio del León, is on your left as you come out. Go through it into number 4 on the Alcázar map, the Patio de la Montería: the big open courtyard, with a tall decorated palace front facing you across it.

### 006 · 4 · Patio de la Montería — Reading the courtyard
*No. 4 · Patio de la Montería · 279 words*

**Where:** In the Patio de la Montería, just through the triple arch from the Patio del León: the Alcázar's largest open courtyard, dominated by the tall, richly decorated front of King Pedro's palace, which faces you across it. The Cuarto del Almirante runs along the right-hand side.

**What:** Patio de la Montería, the Alcázar's main courtyard; the façade of Pedro I's palace (begun from 1356); the Cuarto del Almirante, seat of the Casa de la Contratación 1503–1717; the porticoed gallery to the Patio del Crucero.

Much of the Alcázar opens off this courtyard, sooner or later. The city's own description calls it the main courtyard, the one that leads to the palace buildings.

Its name points back to the royal hunt, la montería. The Alcázar's own notes for schools say this is where the king and his court gathered before a hunt. The Lion Gate you came in by was long known as the Puerta de la Montería, and the courtyard shares the name.

Now take your bearings. The tall decorated front facing you is number 5 on the Alcázar map, the Fachada del Palacio del Rey Don Pedro, the façade of King Pedro's palace. Pedro began his palace in 1356, and the Alcázar's map calls it the heart of the whole complex. The city compares this front to an enormous altarpiece, and you can see why: it is divided into three parts, with layer upon layer of ornament.

On your right, behind a row of columns, is number 6 on the Alcázar map, the Cuarto del Almirante, the Admiral's Quarter. For two centuries it housed the Casa de la Contratación, Spain's House of Trade with the Americas.

And in the far left-hand corner, by the palace front, a porticoed gallery leads to number 31 on the Alcázar map, the Patio del Crucero, the courtyard of the Gothic palace. You will come back to it later.

Kings, merchants and visitors have all had to cross this open space. Take a moment in the middle of it before going on.

Walk up to number 5, the Fachada del Palacio del Rey Don Pedro, close enough to see the bands of lettering high on its front.

### 007 · 5 · Fachada del Palacio del Rey Don Pedro — A king's name in two scripts
*No. 5 · Fachada del Palacio del Rey Don Pedro · 310 words*

**Where:** In the Patio de la Montería, close to the front of King Pedro's palace: the tall, richly carved façade with its central doorway, bands of lettering high on the front and blue-and-white tiles.

**What:** Façade of the palace of Pedro I, inscription dated Spanish era 1402 (= 1364); Gothic Castilian inscription framing the Nasrid motto "wa-la ghaliba illa Allah" in Kufic script on blue-and-white tiles; palace begun from 1356, finished by 1366.

A Christian king of Castile signed this palace in two scripts. One of them, in Arabic, uses the very words of the Muslim rulers of Granada.

The first inscription is in Castilian, in Gothic letters. It calls him "the very high, very noble, very powerful and very conquering Don Pedro, by the grace of God king of Castile and León", and it says that he ordered this work. It is dated in the old Spanish era, the year 1402. In our calendar, that is 1364.

Framed by that Castilian text is a second inscription, in angular Kufic letters on blue and white tiles. It reads wa-la ghaliba illa Allah: "there is no victor but God". It was the motto of the Nasrid sultans of Granada.

Why would Pedro borrow it? The historian Julie Marquer reads it as propaganda. The Castilian court took the Nasrid motto and made it work for a Castilian king. Pedro had craftsmen from Seville and Toledo, and probably from Granada too.

It is tempting to think he simply copied the Alhambra. But the Alhambra's Palace of the Lions was built from 1362 until near the end of that century. Pedro's palace was begun a few years before it, and finished within two years after this inscription. These were not master and copy. They were neighbours, growing up at the same time.

Now look up at the bands of lettering. The Gothic Castilian and the Kufic tiles belong to one message, the king's name and the motto read together.

Two scripts, one king. And a motto that, however you read it, puts God above him.

Number 6, the Cuarto del Almirante, opens off this courtyard: the range along its right-hand side as you face the palace front, behind a row of columns, where the Casa de la Contratación worked. Look for the sign Casa de la Contratación.

## Casa de la Contratación

### 008 · 6 · Cuarto del Almirante — The Casa de la Contratación
*No. 6 · Cuarto del Almirante · 294 words*

**Where:** Inside the Cuarto del Almirante, the range behind a row of columns along the right-hand side of the Patio de la Montería as you face the palace front, signed as the Casa de la Contratación: the first rooms of the Admiral's Quarter, before the old audience hall with the painting of the Virgin.

**What:** Cuarto del Almirante (Cuarto de los Almirantes), seat of the Casa de la Contratación from its founding by Real Provisión of the Catholic Monarchs, Alcalá de Henares, 20 January 1503, until its move to Cádiz in 1717.

For two hundred years, if you wanted to sail legally from Spain to the Americas, your papers passed through this house.

It began with a royal order. On the 20th of January 1503, at Alcalá de Henares, the Catholic Monarchs founded the Casa de la Contratación, the House of Trade, in Seville. The first plan was to put it in the city's shipyards, the Atarazanas, by the river. That was rejected: they were too damp, being so close to the river. So the new office moved into the Alcázar, into these rooms, known as the Cuarto de los Almirantes. It stayed here until 1717, when it moved to Cádiz.

What did it do? Almost everything the Atlantic trade needed. It dealt with trade with the Americas and with fitting out and dispatching the fleets. It licensed ships, cargoes and passengers. Its reach took in shipbuilding, navigation and mapmaking.

That traffic also included human beings. Enslaved Africans were shipped to the Americas under royal licence, and the Crown required those shipments to be registered with the officials of this house. It is part of the story of these rooms, and it should be said plainly.

The Casa's papers did not stay here. Today they are in the Archivo de Indias, across the plaza, among the great archives of the Spanish empire in the Americas.

For two centuries, then, a royal palace was also the office of an ocean. And the officials who met here commissioned a painting that still hangs in their old audience hall.

Through to number 7 on the Alcázar map, the Capilla de la Virgen de los Navegantes, the old audience hall at the far end of this range: the Virgin spreading her cloak over a crowd, with ships at her feet.

### 009 · 7 · Capilla de la Virgen de los Navegantes — Alejo Fernández
*No. 7 · Capilla de la Virgen de los Navegantes · 342 words*

**Where:** In the old audience hall of the Casa de la Contratación, at the end of the Cuarto del Almirante: stand in front of the altarpiece whose central panel shows the Virgin spreading her cloak over a crowd, with ships on the sea below.

**What:** Alejo Fernández (attributed), Virgen de los Navegantes or Virgen de los Mareantes, 1531–36, central panel of an altarpiece for the chapel of the Casa's audience hall; side panels St Sebastian, St James, St Elmo, St John on Patmos; owned by Patrimonio Nacional, kept in the Alcázar.

The men who ran the Atlantic trade wanted protection, so they commissioned it in paint. The Virgin opens her cloak over the people of the voyages, their ships on the sea below.

The painting was made between 1531 and 1536 for this room, the Casa's audience hall, which was also its chapel. It is now attributed to Alejo Fernández, a Seville painter of German origin. It is also called the Virgen de los Mareantes, of the Seafarers. The Alcázar's map says Navegantes, the Navigators.

So who is under the cloak? Tradition has names for them. The man kneeling in gold is often said to be Columbus, though some see King Fernando. A young man in a red hat is usually taken for one of the Pinzón brothers. A red-haired man is often identified as Charles the Fifth. A man in black may be Hernán Cortés. And an old man with a cane is sometimes called Amerigo Vespucci, or else Sancho de Matienzo. Every one of these names is disputed.

Now look past them, into the background. Indigenous men and women of the Americas stand there too, in simple white wraps. The historian Carla Rahn Phillips links that dress to adult baptism. On the sea below: a big carrack, caravels, a galley and smaller oared craft. On the side panels are Saint Sebastian, Saint James, Saint Elmo, and Saint John on Patmos.

Phillips reads the altarpiece as the official picture of a Christian empire overseas. She also notes that the painter himself reportedly owned slaves, both Indian and African.

It is one of the earliest European paintings tied to the Atlantic voyages. Almost everyone in front has been given a name by someone. The people in the background have none.

Back out into number 4, the Patio de la Montería. The palace door, in the middle of number 5, the façade, is to your right. Go through it and along the bent passage beyond, into number 10 on the Alcázar map, the Patio de las Doncellas: a long pool between sunken garden beds.

## Palace of King Pedro

### 010 · 10 · Patio de las Doncellas — The king hailed as sultan
*No. 10 · Patio de las Doncellas · 271 words*

**Where:** At the near end of the Patio de las Doncellas, in the corner where the bent passage from the palace door brings you in: a large arcaded courtyard with a long central pool between sunken garden beds, its walls lined with tile and carved plaster.

**What:** Patio de las Doncellas, central courtyard of the palace of Pedro I (begun from 1356, finished by 1366); Arabic dado inscriptions naming Pedro "sultan"; the life and death of Pedro I (1334–1369) and his remains' journey to Seville Cathedral.

In this palace, a Christian king of Castile is called a sultan. Over and over, in Arabic, on almost every lower wall panel in the building.

The words read: "Glory to our lord the sultan Don Pedro, may God help him." The historian Julie Marquer has traced them across the palace. Wherever you see bands of Arabic lettering low on these walls, that greeting is likely to be among them. Pedro built this palace from the 1350s and finished it in the 1360s.

His reign was short and violent. He became king at fifteen, and much of his reign was spent at war, in the end against his own half-brother, Enrique. Pedro lost his throne, won it back, and in 1369, at Montiel, he was killed. By most accounts, Enrique struck the blow himself. Enrique's family, the Trastámaras, took the crown.

So history gave Pedro two nicknames. His enemies called him the Cruel, and among their charges was that he favoured Muslims and Jews. Others called him the Just. Both names are propaganda, from two sides of a civil war.

What happened next is stranger. Pedro was buried far from here, in the church of Puebla de Alcocer, in Extremadura. Seventy-seven years later he was moved to a convent in Madrid, Santo Domingo el Real. When that convent was demolished, his remains went to the National Archaeological Museum. Only then did they come to Seville Cathedral, where they lie today.

The king hailed as sultan on these walls took centuries to come home.

Stay in number 10, the Patio de las Doncellas, and walk to the edge of the long pool.

### 011 · 10 · Patio de las Doncellas — The buried garden
*No. 10 · Patio de las Doncellas · 315 words*

**Where:** At the edge of the long central pool in the Patio de las Doncellas: the pool runs down the middle of the courtyard between two sunken garden beds, below the level of the walkways and arcades.

**What:** The sunken garden of Pedro I's Patio de las Doncellas: walks, pool and beds found on 22 Aug 2002 under a 16th-century level (excavations 2002 and 2004, directed by M. Á. Tabales); pool gone by 1584, courtyard paved with a central fountain under Felipe II; garden restoration by the Patronato, 2004–05; 16th-century Renaissance columns.

On the 22nd of August 2002, archaeologists digging in this courtyard began to find something no one living had ever seen: King Pedro's garden.

The team was led by Miguel Ángel Tabales. Under the later marble paving and a sixteenth-century level, they found walks, a pool and planting beds, laid out as a sunken garden. It had been hidden for more than four hundred years.

How did a royal garden disappear? By 1584 the pool was gone. Under Felipe the Second the courtyard was paved over, with a fountain in the middle. The arcades were remodelled too, and the columns you see around you are Renaissance ones, put up in the sixteenth century in place of the originals.

Then comes the twist. The scholars Antonio Almagro and Luis Ramón-Laca think the garden was buried soon after Pedro's murder. As far as they can tell, it was never used. It may never even have been finished.

So look down at it now. A long pool runs down the middle, with a sunken bed on each side, about a metre below the walkway. The Alcázar's own Patronato restored it after the excavations, with the work completed in 2005.

And the name, the Courtyard of the Maidens? It comes from a medieval legend of a tribute of a hundred maidens. It is only a legend.

So this may be a garden King Pedro planned, and never walked in.

Number 13 on the Alcázar map, the Salón del Techo de Carlos Quinto, has a short Extra on the palace chapel and its Renaissance ceiling. Its door opens off this courtyard, in the long side on your left as you look down the courtyard from the corner where you came in. The next main stop is number 15 on the Alcázar map, the Salón de los Embajadores, the hall under the gilded dome, at the far end of the courtyard.

### 012 · 13 · Extra · The chapel under the emperor's ceiling
*No. 13 · Salón del Techo de Carlos V · 229 words*

**Where:** Inside the Salón del Techo de Carlos V, through its door in the long side of the Patio de las Doncellas: a long hall under a dark Renaissance coffered ceiling, with carved busts among the coffers.

**What:** Salón del Techo de Carlos V, in the palace of Pedro I: the king's oratory, designed as a chapel; Renaissance coffered ceiling installed in the early 1540s (1542 Pleguezuelo; 1543 andalucia.com), with busts of human figures; a tiled altarpiece of 1504 by Niculoso Pisano, now lost except for fragments. Next door, no. 14, Sala de los Infantes.

This room was a royal chapel before it was named after an emperor. And you are still inside King Pedro's palace, not in the Gothic palace.

It was the king's oratory, his private chapel, on the ground floor of the palace. In 1504 the ceramic artist Niculoso Pisano made a tiled altarpiece for it. Only a few scattered tiles that may come from it survive. His altar for the queen, made the same year, survives upstairs.

The historian Alfonso Pleguezuelo identifies this room as the place where, after midnight, the wedding Mass of Charles the Fifth and Isabel of Portugal was said. The vows themselves, the chronicler tells us, were exchanged in the great domed hall, number 15, the Salón de los Embajadores.

Now look up. The ceiling was put in during the early 1540s: a Renaissance coffered ceiling, with busts of human figures looking down from it. That is why the room carries the emperor's name. Yet Charles had left Seville long before, and he never came back to see it.

Next door is number 14 on the Alcázar map, the Sala de los Infantes, rooms said to have been used by the king's daughters.

Back into number 10, the Patio de las Doncellas, and on to number 15, the Salón de los Embajadores, at the far end of the courtyard: look up for the gilded wooden dome.

### 013 · 15 · Salón de los Embajadores — The dome of 1427
*No. 15 · Salón de los Embajadores · 303 words*

**Where:** Inside number 15, the Salón de los Embajadores: a square hall under a gilded wooden dome, with tiled and carved walls, wrought-iron balconies high up and a painted frieze of royal portraits below the dome. Stand near the middle, clear of the doorways.

**What:** Salón de los Embajadores (Salón de la Media Naranja), main hall and throne room of Pedro I's palace, finished 1366 (Arabic door inscription, era 1404 / year 767); gilded wooden dome by Diego Ruiz, 1427, said to replace an earlier dome; wrought-iron balconies under Felipe II, late 16th century; frieze of portraits of the monarchs by Diego de Esquivel, 1599.

The gold over your head is not King Pedro's. It went up about sixty years after this hall was finished.

This was the heart of Pedro's palace, his main hall and his throne room. Here he received the important people of his time. The city's own description reads the room like a Muslim qubba. The square floor stands for the earth. The dome stands for the universe. And a band of carved muqarnas, folded into a star, joins the square to the circle.

The dome is called the media naranja, the half orange, and the room was long known as the Hall of the Half Orange. A master named Diego Ruiz made it in 1427. It is said to have replaced an earlier dome from Pedro's own time. So you are standing in a fourteenth-century room under a fifteenth-century sky.

How do we know when the hall itself was finished? It tells us. An Arabic inscription on the doors of this hall records that the building of this fortress was completed by the grace of God. And it gives the year twice. Once in the old Spanish era, the year 1404. Once in the Muslim reckoning, the year 767. In our calendar, that is 1366. A Christian king, dating his throne room in Arabic, in two calendars at once.

Later kings added their own layers. The wrought-iron balconies high on the walls were made under Felipe the Second, at the end of the sixteenth century. Around the same time, the painter Diego de Esquivel filled the frieze below the dome with portraits of the monarchs of Spain.

One night in this room mattered more than all the others.

Stay in number 15, the Salón de los Embajadores. Picture it on a night in March, almost a century after Diego Ruiz finished the dome.

### 014 · 15 · Salón de los Embajadores — The night the emperor married
*No. 15 · Salón de los Embajadores · 332 words*

**Where:** Still in number 15, the Salón de los Embajadores, under the gilded dome. Stay where you stood for the last stop.

**What:** The marriage of Charles V (Charles I of Spain) and Isabel of Portugal, Seville, night of 10–11 March 1526: betrothal by words of present consent by Cardinal Salviati in the "media naranja"; nuptial Mass after midnight by the Archbishop of Toledo at an altar set up in a chamber of the palace (Sandoval; Pleguezuelo places it in no. 13); godparents the Duke of Calabria and the Countess of Haro.

Charles the Fifth rode into Seville on 10 March 1526. That same night, in this room, he married in person a woman he had never met.

She was Isabel of Portugal, daughter of King Manuel of Portugal and María of Aragon. She was also his first cousin. She was twenty-two, he was twenty-six. Because they were so closely related, the Church had to grant a dispensation.

Isabel reached Seville a week before him, on 3 March. The city welcomed her with triumphal arches celebrating the virtues a prince should have. Charles came south from Toledo.

He arrived that day, and nobody waited. That night the papal legate, Cardinal Salviati, married them by words of present consent. The chronicler Sandoval places it in "the great hall that they call the half orange". This hall. After midnight, now 11 March, an altar was set up in a chamber of the palace, and the Archbishop of Toledo said Mass there and gave the couple the nuptial blessing. One historian of the Alcázar places that Mass in the king's old oratory, number 13, the Salón del Techo de Carlos Quinto. Their godparents were the Duke of Calabria and the Countess of Haro.

The celebrations did not last long. News arrived that Charles's sister Isabel, Queen of Denmark, had died, and the festivities were cut short. The couple stayed on in Seville for a while, then travelled on to Granada.

The crew of Game of Thrones came to film the fifth season here, and the Alcázar played the Water Gardens of Dorne, home of House Martell. The scenes are often mixed up, so here they are as the Andalucía Film Commission lists them.

In the Salón de los Embajadores, under the golden dome, Jaime Lannister is brought before Prince Doran. At the Grutesco, in front of the Mercury pool, Doran appears with his guard Areo Hotah and with Ellaria Sand. Myrcella and Prince Trystane stroll together in the Jardín de las Damas. In the Baths of María de Padilla, the guards arrest Ellaria.

And right here, beside this pavilion, the Sand Snakes fight Jaime and Bronn over Myrcella. That one scene took more than a week to shoot.

Dorne was not the Alcázar's first screen role. Decades earlier, parts of the palace stood in for Damascus in Lawrence of Arabia.

### 030 · 45 · Cenador del León — The lion's pavilion
*No. 45 · Cenador del León · 272 words*

**Where:** By the pool in front of number 45, the Cenador del León, at the end of the open space beyond the Pabellón de Carlos V: a garden pavilion standing just behind a pool presided over by a lion.

**What:** Cenador del León, built 1644–1645 to designs by Diego Martín Orejuela, painted decoration by Juan de Medina, tiles supplied by Benito Valladares; its pool older, with a 14th-century phase and a 16th-century enlargement, rebuilt after the pavilion; archaeological watch during the 2017 restoration found late-medieval heraldic plaster, very probably of Pedro I's time (a working hypothesis), on the east and north walls of the pool's walkway, not on the single-phase pavilion.

When restorers worked here in 2017, archaeologists found an older story hiding in the walls of the pool.

The pavilion itself is from the seventeenth century. It went up between 1644 and 1645, to designs by Diego Martín Orejuela. Juan de Medina painted its rich decoration, and Benito Valladares supplied the tiles. Its name is simple. It is the Cenador del León, the lion's pavilion, because it stands right behind the pool that the lion presides over.

But the pool is older than the pavilion. The excavation found a fourteenth-century phase, and an enlargement in the sixteenth century. Once the pavilion was finished, the pool was rebuilt entirely to go with it.

Then came the surprise. On two walls of the old walkway round the pool, the team found traces of late medieval heraldic decoration in the plaster. The archaeologists, Miguel Ángel Tabales and Cristina Vargas, date it very probably to the time of King Pedro, the builder of the palace you walked through earlier. They are careful to call this a hypothesis. But if they are right, the walls round the pool are some three centuries older than the pavilion behind it.

So look past the pavilion to the pool. The seventeenth-century garden room is the newcomer here. The walls round the water may be much older.

### 031 · 46 · Extra · The twentieth-century gardens
*No. 46 · Jardín Inglés · 230 words*

**Where:** In number 46, the Jardín Inglés, the garden of winding paths just beyond the Cenador del León; look for the sign with its name.

**What:** Jardín Inglés (46) and Jardín Marqués de la Vega Inclán (50), early 20th-century gardens in the English and French styles; the Puerta de Marchena bought by the Marquis de la Vega-Inclán on behalf of Alfonso XIII for the new gardens.

Gardens follow fashion, just like clothes. And this one is from the start of the twentieth century.

The Italianate gardens near the palaces, of the Damas, the Danza, Troya, the Galera and the Flores, go back to the Renaissance, though they have been remade since. But the Alcázar's gardens kept growing. In the early twentieth century, two new ones were added on either flank. The Alcázar's leaflet calls them a sample of the styles that dominated at that time.

This is the first of them, the Jardín Inglés, the English garden. The second, number 50 on the Alcázar map, the Jardín Marqués de la Vega Inclán, is in the French style.

That name matters. The Marquis de la Vega-Inclán bought a Gothic gateway that came from the town of Marchena. He bought it on behalf of King Alfonso the Thirteenth, to place it in these new gardens. You will meet it in a few minutes.

Remember the floor of the Pabellón de Carlos Quinto, with the outline of the old labyrinth. The maze is an old idea in these gardens.

Back past the Cenador del León, go through number 47 on the Alcázar map, the Jardín del Laberinto. Follow the numbers round: 48 in the far corner, then 49 and 50. The next main stop is number 51, the Puerta de Marchena: the Gothic gateway with wild men holding shields.

### 032 · 51 · Puerta de Marchena — A gateway that travelled
*No. 51 · Puerta de Marchena · 269 words*

**Where:** In front of number 51, the Puerta de Marchena: a carved Gothic stone gateway standing in the garden, with wild-man figures holding shields.

**What:** Puerta de Marchena, former front of the Dukes of Arcos's palace in Marchena, built 1492 and reformed 1540; sculptures by Esteban Jamete, datable to 1544; design possibly linked to Juan Guas; bought by the Marquis de la Vega-Inclán on behalf of Alfonso XIII for the Alcázar's new gardens.

This gateway is a long way from home. It was made to be the front door of a palace in another town.

It comes from Marchena, a town east of Seville, where it was the entrance to the palace of the Dukes of Arcos. It was built in 1492, in the reign of Isabel the Catholic. Its design may be linked to the architect Juan Guas.

Then the gateway changed its coat of arms. In 1540, after a change in the family line, the heraldry had to follow. So the old arms were altered, and the carved figures you see were added. The sculptor Esteban Jamete made them, and they can be dated to 1544.

Look closely at those figures. They are wild men, a favourite figure of late medieval art, holding up the shields of the new owners. Around them the stone mixes three languages: Gothic decoration, later Renaissance elements and touches of Mudéjar work.

From the eighteenth century the gateway belonged to the Dukes of Osuna. Then the Marquis de la Vega-Inclán bought it, on behalf of King Alfonso the Thirteenth, to stand in the Alcázar's new gardens. So a door built for a duke's palace now opens onto a king's garden. The French-style garden of number 50, the Jardín Marqués de la Vega Inclán, carries the name of the man who brought it here.

From here, follow the Salida signs past the beds of number 52 on the Alcázar map, the Jardín de la Alcubilla, just beyond this gateway. They lead to number 53, the Apeadero: the columned hall that opens onto the Patio de Banderas.

## Closing

### 033 · 53 · Apeadero — The way out
*No. 53 · Apeadero · 299 words*

**Where:** Inside number 53, the Apeadero, or just beyond it: the columned hall at the end of the visit that leads out into the Patio de Banderas.

**What:** Apeadero, by Vermondo Resta, 1607; from the 17th century the Alcázar's main access, today the visitors' exit. Patio de Banderas: within the oldest walled enclosure; Roman building of 60–30 BC found about 5 m down in the 2009–2013 excavations.

You are leaving the Alcázar by its old front door. For a long time, this hall was the way in.

It was built in 1607 by Vermondo Resta, the same architect who dressed up the painted Grutesco wall by the Mercury pool. An apeadero is a place to step down from a horse or a carriage. And from the seventeenth century, this became the palace's main entrance. Look at the way it is laid out: three great spaces, divided by columns carrying round arches. One study points out that it is planned less like a civil building than like a church. Today it does the opposite job. It is the visitors' way out.

Beyond it lies the Patio de Banderas, in the oldest walled enclosure of the Alcázar. The walls around that square belong to the Islamic period. Its enclosure wall is the oldest archaeologists have found here, probably from the eleventh century, possibly built by the Almoravids. And deeper still, about five metres down, excavations found a Roman building from the first century before Christ.

So think back over the walk. An Almohad courtyard of plaster lattice. Alfonso the Tenth's Gothic palace and its sunken garden. King Pedro's palace, where an Arabic inscription calls a Christian king sultan. A painting of the navigators, in the house that licensed the Atlantic trade, the slave trade included. An emperor's wedding under a gilded dome. Renaissance gardens, and newer ones beyond. Every age kept what it found, and added its own.

The story goes on outside. In the Cathedral lie the remains of King Pedro, who took centuries to come back to Seville. And across the plaza, the Archivo de Indias keeps the papers of the Casa de la Contratación.

Thank you for walking these eleven centuries with us. Enjoy Seville.

### 035 · CRA 23 · Extra · A royal house still in use
*No. 23 · Comedor de Gala · 227 words*

**Where:** For the Cuarto Real Alto visit, at number 23, the Comedor de Gala, the state dining room, or at the foot of the stair afterwards if listening upstairs is not allowed. It also covers number 22, the Antecomedor, and number 24, the Sala de Fumar.

This state dining room belongs to a house that is still lived in, by a king, on his visits.

The Cuarto Real Alto is the upper floor of a medieval palace, but much of what it shows is far younger. In the nineteenth century, the Bourbon monarchs added rooms to this top floor and refurbished the old ones. They brought in tapestries, clocks, furniture and paintings, and crystal lamps from La Granja, the royal glassworks.

Three rooms belong together here. Number 22, the Antecomedor, is the anteroom. Number 23 is the Comedor de Gala, the state dining room. And number 24 is the Sala de Fumar, the smoking room, a very nineteenth-century kind of room.

The upper floor is not just a display. It is the official residence of the head of state in Andalucía, with bedrooms, a sitting room, an office, an oratory and original royal furniture. The King sleeps here on his visits and holds official events.

So the rooms you walk through are not frozen in the past. A medieval palace, a Bourbon makeover, and a modern monarchy, all on one floor.
