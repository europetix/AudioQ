## The square and the facade

### 001 · Welcome to Barcelona Cathedral
*The Pla de la Seu · on the square in front of the cathedral, to one side of the steps (heard before you go in) · 348 words*

**Where:** On the Pla de la Seu, the open square in front of the cathedral, standing to one side of the steps up to the main door so the way stays clear; heard before you go in.

**What:** Not the Sagrada Família: a basilica dedicated by Benedict XVI on 7 Nov 2010 (F16.5 OK, S37); this is the cathedral of the Holy Cross and Saint Eulàlia, la Seu (F7.1 OK M1, F16.1 OK, §19); "more than seventeen hundred years of history" (F1.18 OK, O6); the route in one breath, version-neutral (PLAN §1 route, Variant A, O1); practical lines once: headphones / working church (ROUTE §1.5), hat off and voice low (F17.6 DATED, A2), no photos during services (F17.7 OFF, O7), small lift so a wait (no capacity, no times; COMP §2.2), offline (COMP §4.2-2); lengths per lead's decision 1 and the route audit R5 (Full ≈ 95–97 with a 5-min lift wait: "a little more with a wait for the lift"). No Gaudí, no UNESCO, no left/right, no date but 2010.

If you came looking for the Sagrada Família, you're in the right city, and standing in front of a different church. You're in good company: plenty of visitors arrive with the same idea. The Sagrada Família is a basilica, dedicated by the Pope in 2010. This one is Barcelona's cathedral, the seat of its bishops: the Cathedral of the Holy Cross and Saint Eulàlia. The city simply calls it la Seu.

Christians have worshipped on this corner of the old Roman town for seventeen centuries. What stands here now is a Gothic church, built around a girl who became the city's own saint. And out in its cloister, you'll meet geese.

Here's the walk. We start out here on the square, with the front of the cathedral. Inside, we'll stand under the lantern tower over the door, and pause at the chapel of the Christ of Lepanto. Then on to the lift, and up onto the roof. Back down, the choir, and the crypt of Santa Eulàlia. Then behind the high altar, into the cloister and its geese, and the Chapter Hall museum. We leave by the cloister's own door, onto Carrer del Bisbe. On the shorter tours, some of these stops are skipped.

A few practical things. Headphones, please: this is a working church, and people come here to pray. Inside, keep your voice low, and take your hat off. Please don't take photos during services. The roof is reached by a small lift, so there may be a wait. And this guide works offline, so you won't need a signal.

With walking, the full tour takes about an hour and a half, a little more with a wait for the lift. The essential takes about an hour, and the highlights about half an hour. Play the stops in order. The numbers on your phone may jump, but every stop tells you where to go next.

Now walk along the square to the end where two Roman towers stand, and turn to look back at the cathedral: The Pla de la Seu · The bishops never moved.

### 002 · The Pla de la Seu — The bishops never moved
*The Pla de la Seu · at the end of the square by the two Roman towers, the cathedral in view · 277 words*

**Where:** At the end of the square where two old stone towers stand, close to them but clear of the crowd, turned back so the cathedral front is in view.

**What:** Roman Barcino, 10 BC under Augustus, "a small fortified city covering just ten hectares" (F1.1, F1.2 OK, A1); two walls, 1st and 3rd century (F1.3 OK, A1; not said which these towers are); the decumanus gate at Plaça Nova (G120 OK, GA4; "round" not used); the first official see in the corner of the walled town (F1.4 OK, A1, S6); the first church under the cathedral, never dug (F1.5 HEDGE, S6); the dedication to the Holy Cross in 599 (F1.14 OK, O1); Almanzor's raid of 985, about a hundred documents saved (F1.16 OK, O9); Brossa's Barcino letters where the aqueduct reached the city (F6.9 OK A11, G121 OK GS22; no date; O13). Praetextatus 343 (F1.13) left out for the date count.

When Barcelona began, it was tiny. The city's history museum describes Roman Barcino as "a small fortified city covering just ten hectares". It was founded in the time of the emperor Augustus, in 10 BC.

You're standing at its edge. These towers belong to the Roman town's walls. The museum says there were two walls, "one dating from the 1st century and the other from the 3rd". Here was a gate, where the town's main street, the decumanus, came in from the countryside.

Look down at the foot of the wall, where the Roman aqueduct once reached the city. Find the big letters standing there. They're a visual poem by Joan Brossa, and together they spell Barcino, the city's Roman name.

Now look back at the cathedral. When the Christians of the late Roman town built their first official church, the seat of their bishop, they put it in a corner of the walled town. That corner is where the cathedral stands now. The bishops never moved.

Somewhere beneath the cathedral lies that first church. It has never been dug. Scholars place it under the floor, but without an excavation, nobody can show it.

The name came before any building you can see. In 599 a document records the church's dedication to the Holy Cross. The Holy Cross came first.

Then came Almanzor. In 985 his army raided Barcelona, destroyed the city and wrecked the cathedral. From the cathedral's archive, about a hundred documents came through.

Turn now to the old stone house built into the Roman wall beside the cathedral, the Casa de l'Ardiaca: The Pla de la Seu · A square from after the war.

### 003 · The Pla de la Seu — A square from after the war
*The Pla de la Seu · with the Casa de l'Ardiaca in view, beside the cathedral · 227 words*

**Where:** On the square, turned towards the old stone house built against the Roman wall beside the cathedral, with its door in view; stay clear of the steps.

**What:** The square opened after the Civil War, avenue 1939–1958 (F6.1 HEDGE, safe wording "after the war, over almost twenty years"; A6, S23); the Barri de la Catedral "one of the most affected", Sant Felip Neri 30 Jan 1938, 42 dead incl. 20 children (G112 OK, GS20; no direction; never "the cathedral was hit"); the Casa de l'Ardiaca built around the 12th century "taking advantage of part of Barcelona's old Roman wall" (F6.3 OK, A6); rebuilt by Lluís Desplà, Gothic "with some Renaissance features" (F6.4 OK, A6); forward to 034 (safe); Picasso's friezes on the College of Architects, drawings ready Oct 1960, sandblasted by Carl Nesjar, the dust paraphrased (F6.8 OK, A12). Letterbox (G124) left out: conditional on O13 and over the aim.

This wide square is younger than it looks. Until the Civil War, a dense neighbourhood stood here. The broad avenue in front of the cathedral was opened afterwards, after the war, over almost twenty years.

The cathedral's neighbourhood was one of the worst hit by the bombing. The bombs fell on the streets around you. In the little square of Sant Felip Neri nearby, on 30 January 1938, 42 people died, 20 of them children.

The old stone house beside the cathedral is the Casa de l'Ardiaca, the archdeacon's house. The city's archive says it was built around the 12th century, "taking advantage of part of Barcelona's old Roman wall". Look at it: the house simply moved into the wall.

At the end of the 15th century the archdeacon Lluís Desplà rebuilt it in the Gothic style, "with some Renaissance features". You'll meet him again in the Chapter Hall museum, kneeling in a painting.

Across the square, the line drawings on the modern College of Architects are by Picasso. In October 1960 he telephoned to say the drawings were ready. The artist Carl Nesjar sandblasted them into the concrete, raising a formidable cloud of dust.

Now turn to the front of the cathedral. Stand far enough back to see it whole, from the door up to the spires: The facade · Drawn in 1408, built in 1887.

### 004 · The facade — Drawn in 1408, built in 1887
*The facade · on the square, far enough back to see the whole front · 303 words*

**Where:** On the square facing the cathedral, far enough back to see the whole front from the door up to the spires and the lantern tower behind it.

**What:** Neo-Gothic front, "following 15th-century plans" (F5.23 OK, M1); Carlí of Rouen's 1408 portal design, ink on twelve parchments of which four are lost, 3.11 m tall, in the archive, not on show (F5.1, F5.3 OK, S7, A7, S25); front closed with a plain wall in 1417, said as "nine years later" (F3.20 OK, O1, S7); lack of budget (Q13, F5.4 OK, S25); the 1882 competition rule, said as "in the 1880s" (F5.5 OK, A7); Manuel Girona, banker and former mayor, failed to raise the money and paid (F5.9, F5.10 OK; wording per COAC re-read, see notes); begun summer 1887, finished 12 Feb 1890 on her feast day (F5.12, F5.13 OK, S24, S7); lantern tower finished 1913 by his children (F5.16 HEDGE: 1913 only; O1); version-neutral Gaudí sentence (F5.7, F18.5); look: Christ on the central pillar, apostles either side (F5.21 OK, S24), the sober sides (F5.25 OK, M1). Agapit Vallmitjana (optional) left out.

The front you photograph is not medieval. It went up at the end of the 1800s, "following 15th-century plans", as the national art museum puts it.

The plans are far older than the stone. In 1408 a master from Rouen called Carlí drew the main portal. His drawing survives, in the cathedral's archive: ink on twelve sheets of parchment. Four are lost, and what's left still stands over three metres tall. It isn't on show.

The medieval builders never finished this front. Nine years after Carlí's drawing, they closed it with a plain wall, and it stayed that way for over four centuries. Why? A University of Barcelona report gives the reason: "The lack of budget in medieval Barcelona prevented this project" from being built.

In the 1880s came a competition to finish it, with one rule. Every design had to start from the medieval drawing.

Then the money. Manuel Girona, a banker who had been mayor of Barcelona, tried to raise the funds and failed. So he paid for it himself. Work began in the summer of 1887. The main front was finished three years later, on the twelfth of February, Santa Eulàlia's feast day. The lantern tower behind it was finished in 1913, by his children.

One name people expect here is missing. Gaudí built nothing here. As a young man he only helped draw a rival design, and it lost.

Now find Christ, standing on the central pillar of the door, with the apostles either side of him. Then let your eye travel out to the sides of the building. Beside this busy front, the old walls are plain and sober: buttresses and octagonal towers, and very little else.

Barcelona waited almost five centuries for its front door.

Stay here, facing the front: The facade · The design that lost.

### 005 · The facade — The design that lost
*The facade · same spot · 238 words*

**Where:** Same spot on the square, facing the front, far enough back to see the lantern tower rising behind it.

**What:** Joan Martorell's rejected entry, with popular and press support, turned down by the Royal Academy of San Fernando on "rather weak grounds" (F5.6 OK, S24, S25, A7); helped by the very young Lluís Domènech i Montaner and Antoni Gaudí, "drafted by Antoni Gaudí" (F5.7 OK, A7, S25); the winner under Josep Oriol Mestres's name, the paymaster's hand in it (F5.8 HEDGE, safe wording); critics: the lantern tower borrowed from the loser (F5.20 OK, S24, S25); Mestres died and August Font took over (F5.14 OK, S24, optional, used). Girona's marquisate (F5.11) left out: not re-read. No dates (004 has them).

The public loved the design that lost. Joan Martorell's entry was taller and richer, and it had the support of the people and the press. The Royal Academy in Madrid turned it down anyway, on "rather weak grounds", says the architects' college's catalogue.

Joan Martorell had help from two very young architects. One was Lluís Domènech i Montaner. The other was Antoni Gaudí. The University of Barcelona calls it a project "drafted by Antoni Gaudí". So Gaudí did draw for this front. On paper only. And he lost.

The design that won carried the name of the cathedral's architect, Josep Oriol Mestres. But the man paying the bills had a hand in the drawing too.

Then the turn. When the new front went up, critics said the winners had quietly borrowed from the loser. The complaint was loudest about the lantern tower, which, they said, had too much of Joan Martorell in it.

Mestres died while the work was still going on, and another architect, August Font, took it over.

Look up now, behind the front, at the lantern tower rising over the door. If the critics were right, part of what you're looking at began on the losing side.

Now climb the steps to the main door and go in. Stop just inside, out of the way of people coming in, and look ahead along the nave: Inside the main door · A tower over the door.

## Inside the main door

### 006 · Inside the main door — A tower over the door
*Inside the main door · just inside, to one side, looking along the nave to the choir screen · 295 words*

**Where:** Just inside the main door, a step to one side so people can come in, looking along the nave towards the marble screen of the choir.

**What:** The cimbori over the entrance, unusual (F4.9 OK, S7, O4), 41 m inside (F5.18 OK, O4; see notes on "high"); Gual and the carpenter Aigüents's eighteen-day trip to Valencia in 1421, the provisional roof until the early 20th century (F3.14 OK, S7; "almost five hundred years" our arithmetic, no 1913 here); the bay by the facade the longest, to carry it (F4.8 OK, O4); three aisles, one apse and ambulatory (F4.1 OK, O4); side aisles almost as high (F4.2 HEDGE wording), 26 m centre (F4.3 OK, O4); over 150 years without interrupting the services (F3.4 HEDGE wording, A4); begun behind the altar towards the door (F3.8 OK); 215 keystones found painted in the 1970 restoration (F4.10 OK, O4). Pere Joan's keystone (F4.11) left out until O15.

Most cathedrals put their lantern tower over the crossing, near the altar. Here it's over your head as you walk in. This is the cimbori, the lantern tower, and inside it rises forty-one metres.

In 1421 the master builder, Bartomeu Gual, and a carpenter made an eighteen-day journey to Valencia, to see how they had built their tower over the crossing. Back home, the money seems to have run out. So they put a temporary roof on the unfinished tower. The stopgap lasted almost five hundred years, into the 1900s.

Why over the door at all? The bay by the door is the longest in the church. It was built that length to carry the tower.

Now look along the nave. The church is laid out simply: three aisles, one apse, and a walkway behind the altar. The side aisles rise almost as high as the centre. The keystones at the top of the centre are twenty-six metres above you.

The city says the work took more than a hundred and fifty years, and never interrupted the services. They started at the far end, behind the altar, and worked their way towards this door.

Look up at the ribs of the vault, to the carved bosses where they meet. There are over two hundred of these keystones in the church. They were found to be painted when they were cleaned in 1970. Pick one out above you, and look for the colour.

Now find the chapel close to the main door, on the right as you face the high altar: the chapel of the Christ of Lepanto. Stop at its doorway. If Mass is on, don't go in. You can hear this from the door: The Lepanto chapel · The Christ older than its battle.

"The most venerated image in Barcelona." That's how the cathedral describes the crucifix in this chapel. This is the Christ of Lepanto chapel, also the chapel of the Blessed Sacrament.

Tradition says it sailed into battle at Lepanto, in 1571, aboard the flagship of Don John of Austria. And the story goes that when a Turkish cannonball came, the Christ swerved aside. That, they say, is why the body bends.

Now the evidence. A historian, Stefan Hanss, found that no archival document of 1571 seems to mention the crucifix at all. In the sources he cites, the cannonball story first turns up in the confraternity's statutes, written down in 1762, almost two hundred years after the battle. As he puts it, "it cannot be proven that the object actually was on the ship of Don John".

The cathedral now says the carving is "not from the 16th century, as was believed". It is older than the battle it's famous for.

If you can see it from here, notice how dark it is: candle smoke and wax, and varnish added later. Restorers have cleaned it, and saved paint that was beginning to flake.

Arnau Bargués built this room in 1407 as the canons' meeting room, their chapter house. They met here until the 1670s. It's not the Chapter Hall museum at the end of the tour. Beneath the crucifix lies a bishop-saint, Oleguer.

Most of the year it hangs here. In Lent, it's moved.

Here's a piece of the Renaissance, set into a Gothic church. This white marble wall is the trascor, the choir screen. It was begun by Bartolomé Ordóñez, a sculptor who came to Barcelona in 1517.

Look at the four scenes of Santa Eulàlia's martyrdom, two on each side of the door. The carving is in very low relief, almost drawn into the stone. One art historian praises its delicacy, and the virtuosity of its small details. Find the flames, and see how little depth he needed for them.

### 010 · The baptistery — Baptised here
*The baptistery · at the chapel's entrance, the font and its tall window in view · 271 words*

**Where:** At the entrance of the first chapel on the left as you face the high altar, beside the main door, with the font and the tall stained-glass window in view; stand aside if worshippers are there.

**What:** The early baptistery found in 1968 under the cathedral (F1.7 OK, A1, S6), later rebuilt as an eight-sided pool (F1.8 safe wording, S6); not visible, never "go and see"; the window of Saint Michael and Noli me tangere, Gil de Fontanet, 1495, from a design by Bartolomé Bermejo, in place, "the most important of all the Cathedral windows" (F4.14 OK, O4; scene not described before O14); forward to 034 (safe); Antoni Canet's carved stone washing cupboard (levador) of 1406, his first surviving work, the vault closed c. 1420, on corbels as heads: a girl with a low neckline and a necklace, a hooded man with a full beard (G44 OK, GS10; O14); "a 15th-century font" (F10.28 HEDGE, S34; no maker, no 1433); the plaque of April 1493 and its doubtful date (F10.29 HEDGE, S34; ONLY if O14 finds the plaque, else cut that paragraph).

In 1968, under the cathedral floor, archaeologists found the font where Barcelona's first Christians were baptised. It was a pool inside its own small building, later rebuilt with eight sides. You can't see it today. It lies under the floor, out of sight.

This chapel is the baptistery, and its font is from the 15th century. Look up at its tall window. It shows Saint Michael and the scene called Noli me tangere, Latin for "Do not touch me". Gil de Fontanet made it in 1495, from a design by the painter Bartolomé Bermejo, and it's still in the place it was made for. The cathedral calls it "the most important of all the Cathedral windows". Now look lower, for a carved stone washing cupboard from the early 1400s. It's the first surviving work of the sculptor Antoni Canet, older than the chapel's vault above it. Under this organ there once hung a carved Saracen's head, a carassa. The city dates the cathedral's one to the 16th century. A newspaper tells how, on three winter feasts, Saint Stephen's day, the Holy Innocents and New Year's Eve, its eyes and mouth moved, worked by strings from inside the organ. And in 1892 its beard was remade from three donkey tails.

"It was the organists that made their eyes move and got sweets out of their mouths", says the city's popular-culture service. That's told of these carved heads in general, and the newspaper puts the sweets at another church.

By the middle of the 20th century the heads had been taken down. One reason was the noise. Another, the city explains, was what they showed: a severed head, which came to be seen "as an apology for xenophobia and racism".

The organ above was first built around 1540 by Pere Flamenc and Antoni Carbonell. Gabriel Blancafort rebuilt it inside its old case in the 1980s and 1990s: fifty-seven stops, four keyboards and pedals. A joiner of the same name, Antoni Carbonell, had worked on the choir for the knights of the Golden Fleece.

Outside this door, in the street, an inscription records the day the Gothic works began: the first of May 1298, while King Jaume of Aragon reigned.

Look up at the organ, high in its gallery over the door, and at the space below it, where the face once rolled its eyes.

The lift to the roof leaves from the chapel beside this door, the chapel of the Holy Innocents. Join the queue there. If there's no queue, ride up and play the next stop at the top, to one side of the lift: The lift · Eulàlia strikes the hours.
