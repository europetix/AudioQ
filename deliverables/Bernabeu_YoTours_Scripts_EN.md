## Outside · The building and the man

### 002 · Outside · The steel skin
*Outside · Castellana · 328 words*

**Where:** Outside on the Paseo de la Castellana side, far enough back from the stadium to see the long curved bands of stainless steel running right around it.

**What:** The 2019 to 2026 rebuild of the 1947 stadium: gmp with L35 and Ribas & Ribas, competition won 2014, works from June 2019; home games at the Alfredo Di Stéfano stadium at the training ground until September 2021; the old building largely kept and wrapped in a stainless-steel louvre facade with gaps for natural ventilation; cost of about 1.4 billion to 30 June 2026 in the club's accounts.

The stadium in front of you is really two buildings, one inside the other. The inner one opened in 1947. The outer one was wrapped around it, mostly while the football carried on.

The design that won the competition for the job came from the German architects gmp, working with two Spanish studios, L35 and Ribas & Ribas. They chose not to knock the old stadium down. Instead, they kept most of it standing, and closed it inside a new skin. Building work began in the summer of 2019.

The football carried on, but for a while it had to move. For more than a year, through the pandemic seasons, Real Madrid played its home games at the small Alfredo Di Stéfano stadium. That's at the club's training ground on the edge of the city. Then the team came back here, and the work simply went on around the matches, season after season.

It was not a cheap trick, and it got dearer as it went. By June 2026, the club's own accounts put the total cost at about one point four billion euros. The club describes the works as practically finished.

Now look at the skin itself. It isn't a wall. It's a curtain of stainless-steel louvres, long curved bands with gaps left between them. Those gaps are there on purpose. They let air move through, so the stadium can ventilate naturally. You'll read wildly different counts of how many bands there are, so we'll leave the number alone. What you can see is how the steel takes on the colour of the sky. The architects also planned lighting to wash the facade at night.

Stand here long enough and it hardly reads as a football ground at all. Yet somewhere behind all that steel, the concrete stadium of 1947 is still standing.

### 003 · Outside · Don Santiago's bet

In the early 1940s, Real Madrid's ground held about twenty-five thousand people. The club's new president wanted one for a hundred thousand. The club's own history calls the plan pharaonic.

His name was Santiago Bernabéu. He had played for the club from boyhood until the late 1920s, and he became its president during the Second World War. Spain was only a few years out of its own civil war, in which he had fought on Franco's side. Even so, the first stone of a new stadium was laid here on the 27th of October, 1944.

It opened on the 14th of December, 1947, under the name Nuevo Chamartín, the New Chamartín. Real Madrid beat the champions of Portugal, Os Belenenses, three goals to one. The first goal ever scored in this stadium came from Sabino Barinaga, in the fifteenth minute.

And the dream of a hundred thousand? The stadium kept growing upwards. Less than seven years after it opened, the club says it could hold a hundred and twenty-five thousand people, more than it has ever held since. A year later, the club gave the stadium his name, while he was still in charge.

He stayed president for almost thirty-five years, until his death. In that time the club won six European Cups and sixteen Spanish league titles. You'll meet several of those cups in the museum.

One more thing about that name. Since late 2025, the club has branded the stadium with a single word. Not the full name. Just his surname, Bernabéu.

## Museum · Origins and the first Cups

### 009 · Museum · Into the museum
*Museum · 226 words*

**Where:** The first part of the museum, where the club's history begins: old photographs, early shirts and screens of archive film, before the route reaches the trophy hall.

**What:** The club's founding as Madrid Football Club, 6 March 1902; the royal title "Real" and the crown on the badge, granted by Alfonso XIII on 29 June 1920; ownership by the members, the socios; what kind of museum this is.

For its first eighteen years, this club was not "Real" at all. It was plain Madrid Football Club.

It was formally constituted on the sixth of March, 1902, with a board led by Juan Padrós. The royal title came later. On the twenty-ninth of June, 1920, King Alfonso the Thirteenth granted it by letter, and with it the right to put a royal crown on the badge. More than a century later, that crown still sits on top of the badge.

The second thing worth knowing is who owns all this. Not a billionaire, and not a company. In the club's own words, Real Madrid is a club owned by its members. They're called socios, and it is they who elect the president.

And the third thing is what kind of museum you're in. The club calls it an audiovisual and interactive journey through its trophies. In other words, this is Real Madrid telling its own story, proudly, on screens, in old film and in glass cases. It's well made, and it isn't neutral. You don't need to read every panel. Let the pictures carry you, and I'll fill in the stories that matter, along with one or two the walls tend to skip.

### 010 · Trophy hall · The fifteen
*Museum · trophy hall · 310 words*

**Where:** In the trophy hall, at the long row of European Cups, the big silver trophies with looping handles. Stand back far enough to take in the whole line at once.

**What:** The fifteen European Cups / Champions Leagues, 1956–2024; the latest at Wembley, 1 June 2024, 2–0 v Borussia Dortmund; the honours board (Milan 7, Liverpool 6, Bayern 6, Barcelona 5); the original trophy kept after 1966; PSG's 2025 and 2026 titles (dated).

There are fifteen of them. Fifteen European Cups, the trophy now called the Champions League. And nobody else is close.

AC Milan come next, with seven. Liverpool and Bayern Munich have six each. Barcelona have five. Put simply, Real Madrid have won Europe's greatest club competition more than twice as often as any other club on the continent.

The first five came one after another, at the very start of the competition, in the late fifties. UEFA still lists that run as the record. Then the wins thin out, and later they come back in a rush. Nine of the fifteen have been won since the old European Cup became the Champions League, in the early nineties. The most recent came at Wembley, in 2024, two goals to nothing against Borussia Dortmund.

Here's a detail most people walk straight past. The trophy's nickname is Old Big Ears, for those enormous looping handles. But the very first cup, the one presented at the first final, had much smaller handles. And that original ended up with Real Madrid for good. After their sixth win, in 1966, UEFA let them keep the original. Today's winners take home a full-size replica, while UEFA keeps today's original for the presentation on the night.

Now let your eye run along the whole line. Every cup in this row is a final won, a season, a city and a night to remember. Close to seventy years separate the first from the most recent.

To be fair to everyone else, the story has moved on. As of autumn 2026, the count still stands at fifteen. Paris Saint-Germain won both of the finals played since Wembley.

For most clubs, one of these would define a century. Here, they needed a very long shelf.

## Museum · Galácticos to now

### 014 · Trophy hall · Zidane's volley
*Museum · trophy hall · 261 words*

**Where:** At the row of European Cups, by cup number nine, the 2002 trophy won in Glasgow.

**What:** Cup eight, Paris 2000, 3–0 v Valencia; cup nine, Hampden Park, Glasgow, 15 May 2002, 2–1 v Bayer Leverkusen: Raúl 9', Lúcio 14', Zidane 44' left-foot volley from Roberto Carlos's looping cross from the left; the club's centenary year; Casillas on for the injured César at 68' and his late saves.

Some goals are remembered for what they won. This one is remembered for how it looked.

First, a quick catch-up. Two years after La Séptima, cup number eight came in Paris, three goals to nothing against Valencia. Then, in 2002, the club turned one hundred. And the final that year was in Glasgow, at Hampden Park, the very ground where Di Stéfano and Puskás had beaten Eintracht seven goals to three.

The opponents were Bayer Leverkusen. Raúl scored early, and Leverkusen equalised five minutes later. And then, just before half-time, came the moment.

Roberto Carlos, out on the left, sent a high, looping cross towards the edge of the penalty area. Zinedine Zidane, who had joined from Juventus the year before, waited for it. He adjusted his body and met it on the volley with his left foot, supposedly the weaker one. Goal.

The French magazine France Football later named it the most beautiful goal in the history of the Champions League.

But the final wasn't over. In the second half, Madrid's goalkeeper César was injured. On came a twenty-year-old substitute called Iker Casillas. In the last minutes Leverkusen had chance after chance, and he made the saves that kept the lead. Twelve years later, that same young keeper would be the captain lifting cup number ten.

So when you look at cup number nine, picture two things: a volley that is still replayed today, and a twenty-year-old in goal who made sure it counted.
