# Yo Tours · Poble Espanyol (Barcelona) · audio guide scripts (EN) · 31 Oct 2026 edition

## The gate

### 001 · Welcome to the Poble Espanyol
*Plaza Castellana · just inside the gate · 359 words*

**Where:** In Plaza Castellana, the square just inside the Ávila gate, a few steps in and out of the flow of people coming through.

**What:** The Poble Espanyol de Montjuïc ("Spanish Village"), built for the 1929 Barcelona International Exhibition; 117 full-size buildings modelled on originals all over Spain (OFF-op); construction from January 1928, opened 20 May 1929 (OFF-op); meant to be demolished, kept. Official plan Feb 2026: houses numbered 1–116, four region colours, a marked step-free route.

Almost every house in this village comes from somewhere else. In the Poble's own words, these are "117 buildings that are actually full-scale recreations of other existing ones". Life-size, not miniatures.

Welcome to the Poble Espanyol, the Spanish Village, on Montjuïc. It was built for the great Barcelona International Exhibition of 1929. Building began in January 1928, and the village opened about sixteen months later. The Poble's own history says that "the initial idea was for it to be demolished". It never was.

So what is it? Not a museum of copies. A 1961 architecture journal put the aim, in Spanish, as "interpretation more than the reproduction of particular models". Nor, later researchers add, was it about "making a collection of monuments". It's an invented town, built from real pieces. So this guide tells you, house by house, where the original stands and what the builders changed.

On the official plan the houses come in four colours: North green, Centre pink, South yellow, Mediterranean purple. Many stops name a house by its number on that plan, so pick one up at the entrance.

Our walk is one loop, uphill first. Up Calle Caballeros to Aragon's brick tower, then through Catalonia to the monastery at the top, with its view of Barcelona. Then down through the white streets of Andalusia and the Santiago Steps to the Plaza Mayor, and out through this gate.

The full tour takes about an hour and a half, walking included, nearer two hours if you also go round the Fran Daurel art museum. The Essential version takes about an hour and a quarter, the Highlights about three quarters of an hour. In the shorter versions the numbers may jump. Every stop tells you where to go next.

The official plan marks a step-free route. Ask for it at the entrance, and this guide warns you where there are steps.

Craftspeople work in the workshops here: glassblowing, pottery, stained glass. If a door is open, look in.

Stay in Plaza Castellana and face the gate you came in by, its two round towers with battlements, joined by a walkway: Plaza Castellana · The towers of Ávila.

### 002 · Plaza Castellana — The towers of Ávila
*Plaza Castellana · facing the gate's two towers · 352 words*

**Where:** In Plaza Castellana, facing the inner side of the Ávila gate: two round towers with battlements, joined by a walkway; stand back far enough to see both towers and the walkway, clear of people coming in.

**What:** The gate, modelled on the Puerta de San Vicente in the medieval walls of Ávila (DOC, PLAN drawing); 1928 archive caption; the 1929 opening by Alfonso XIII and Victoria Eugenia. Around the square, by the Poble's own list: Casa del Sol, Cáceres = house 3 (DOC, EST); Golfines de Abajo, Carvajal, Mayoralgo, Hernando de Ovando (Cáceres), Almaraz (Plasencia), Palacio del Obispo (Miajadas) (PIN+COL, numbers not spoken).

In 1929, the Exhibition's official visitors were photographed right here. The Poble's own history says so: "Several official photos were taken in front of the Torres de Ávila." The towers of Ávila. The King and Queen, Alfonso the Thirteenth and Victoria Eugenia, came to the village's opening.

What were they standing in front of? The Poble calls this gate "a representation" of the Puerta de San Vicente, a gateway in the medieval walls of Ávila, in Castile. The city's photo archive has a picture captioned: "Main entrance to the Poble Espanyol site. Gate of San Vicente of the wall of Avila. 1928."

Now look at it. A Catalan heritage catalogue describes the gate, in Catalan, as "flanked by two great cylindrical towers crowned with merlons and joined to each other by a bridge". Find the merlons, the square teeth along the top. Then find the walkway that bridges the gap between the two towers.

This is also where the hill begins. The village is entered at its lowest point and climbs from here. An architect's study of the village says, in Spanish, that "our visit obliges us to enter through the gate of San Vicente", and that this is what makes it the main entrance.

Around you is Plaza Castellana, with palaces from Extremadura. The Poble's own list places the Casa del Sol, from Cáceres, at house 3. Round this square it also places more noble houses from Cáceres, one from Plasencia and a bishop's palace from Miajadas. They're pink on the plan, for the Centre.

People sometimes think the village was built for the 1992 Olympics. It's sixty-three years older.

This gate was a stage entrance for the Exhibition. It is still the way in, and at the end of this walk it will be your way out.

## Calle Caballeros

### 004 · Calle Caballeros — Four men and a Hispano-Suiza
*Calle Caballeros · at its start, by Plaza Castellana · 356 words*

**Where:** At the start of Calle Caballeros, where it leaves Plaza Castellana between houses 115 and 2, on the step-free route; stand to one side so the street stays clear.

**What:** The research trips of 1927–28: Miquel Utrillo, Xavier Nogués, Francesc Folguera and Ramon Reventós; first trip 2 Sep – 2 Oct 1927 in a Hispano-Suiza (almost 700 towns passed, 137 studied); trips 2 and 3 (Nov 1927, May 1928); the method; the burnt clutch; only 9 preliminary models kept; the five travel notebooks, hidden in a desk drawer in the office of J. M. Reventós (ACE19 p. 239 n. 11; "Private archive of the Reventós family", EGA24), no duration sourced; about a thousand 1927 negatives in the Arxiu Fotogràfic.

In July 1927 Miquel Utrillo, the engineer and painter leading the project, wrote to Lluís Plandiura, one of the Exhibition's leaders. He wrote, in Spanish, that the journey was "so indispensable that a good part of the work done this month will, logically, be changed". Changed "by the living things that the architects and our friend Nogués will see".

That September four men set off in one car, a Hispano-Suiza: Utrillo, the painter Xavier Nogués, and two architects, Francesc Folguera and Ramon Reventós. A month on the road. They passed through almost seven hundred towns, and stopped to study a hundred and thirty-seven. Two more trips followed. Across all three, they passed through something like sixteen hundred places.

They had a method. In small towns Nogués sketched the outline while the two architects stood ready with the camera. In the important towns they split into two teams, an architect and a painter in each, and then they voted on every find, marks out of ten. Colours were matched against scales of tone that Nogués had drawn up.

It wasn't all smooth. Near Zaragoza the car broke down, and Utrillo recorded the moment in a caricature. On the second trip, that November, a letter was written while they waited for the Zaragoza express. It says, in Catalan, "we have the car in Tudela being repaired, because the clutch burned out".

Did the trip matter? Of the models in the first plan, only nine survived into the final village, and only three unchanged.

Then the record of the journey went into a drawer. More than a hundred photographs and sketches, in five travel notebooks, lay hidden there, in a desk in a Reventós family office, until the researchers found them. They wrote: "These notebooks, which have remained hidden until today, constitute unpublished material." The city's photo archive also holds about a thousand negatives from that year.

Look up the street: this is what all that voting produced.

Walk on up Calle Caballeros, slowly, and play the next stop as you go. Look at the doorways and the coats of arms above them: Calle Caballeros · Coats of arms.

### 006 · Calle Príncipe de Viana — The men who made it
*Junction of Calle Caballeros and Calle Príncipe de Viana · houses 102/103 · 360 words*

**Where:** Where Calle Caballeros becomes Calle Príncipe de Viana, by houses 102 and 103, with the Travesía del Maestrazgo going off beside house 41 (an accessible-route marker on the plan); stand to one side for a few minutes. Position-free.

**What:** The four makers: Miquel Utrillo (1862–1934), Xavier Nogués (1873–1941), Francesc Folguera (1891–1960), Ramon Reventós (1892–1976); Iberiona (early 1920s), halted by Primo de Rivera's coup of 1923, re-presented in 1926; Maricel (Sitges); Maurice Utrillo's surname; the Teatre Grec; the leftover plot; Mirador, 5 Dec 1929; Arquitectura, 1961. Puig i Cadafalch drew the Exhibition's general plan, not the village.

Nobody has ever quite agreed whose idea this was. The writer Carles Soldevila joked, in Catalan, that working out who deserved the glory was "as complicated as finding out the origins of the Great War".

The Poble's own history names four men. Miquel Utrillo, "engineer, painter and project leader". In the early 1920s he was already pitching a Spain-in-one-place idea to the mayor. He called it Iberiona. The Exhibition itself stalled after General Primo de Rivera's coup in 1923. Later, Utrillo brought his idea back.

He had done something like it before, at Maricel in Sitges, for the American Charles Deering. A 1961 architecture journal called Maricel, in Spanish, "a kind of first Spanish Village, built with fairly authentic elements". Utrillo also gave his surname to the son of his Paris friend Suzanne Valadon: the painter Maurice Utrillo.

Xavier Nogués was the painter and draughtsman. Then two young architects. Francesc Folguera, who later took charge of planning the resort of S'Agaró. And Ramon Reventós, who also designed the Teatre Grec on Montjuïc, in an old quarry. Reventós remembered, in Spanish, that "what really changed with our involvement was that the vague ideas about the layout of the streets became concrete." Look at the street ahead as it curves away. That layout is theirs.

The village was the Exhibition's last project to be awarded, and it got a leftover plot.

In December 1929 a gossip column in the Catalan magazine Mirador took a swipe at Utrillo. "Now it turns out," it began, "that the author of the Poble Espanyol was also, if not the first, one of the first excavators of Montjuïc."

Reventós outlived the others. The 1961 journal called him "an inexhaustible archive of the history and the ups and downs" of the village.

You may read that Josep Puig i Cadafalch had the idea. He drew the Exhibition's overall plan. The village was the work of these four.

Carry on up Calle Príncipe de Viana, the curving street that climbs toward Aragon's brick tower. Play the next stop as you walk, and look at the houses on both sides: Calle Príncipe de Viana · Navarre and the Basque hills.

## The brick tower

### 008 · Plaza Aragonesa — The tower they liked better
*Plaza Aragonesa · the whole tower in view · 359 words*

**Where:** In Plaza Aragonesa, at the top of Calle Príncipe de Viana, standing where the whole brick tower is in view; it rises between Plaza Aragonesa and Plaza del Carmen, at the end of the church (57–59 on the plan). The Plaza de la Iglesia is reached along the church front; the Fran Daurel Museum is the grey block on the plan behind the church.

**What:** The Poble's tower (DOC, PLAN drawing), modelled on the Mudéjar bell tower of Nuestra Señora de la Asunción, Utebo (Zaragoza), completed 1544 by Alonso de Leznes; the Poble's version slightly reduced, cast in whole plaster moulds; the church: Carmelite church of Alcañiz façade + Utebo tower + Torralba de Ribota façade (preliminary design Baroque: Amorebieta + Caldes de Montbui). DATED: FIESTA, 10 min, "inside the iconic Utebo Tower" (page mod. 3 Mar 2026).

On a journey in 1928, passing the real tower at Utebo again, one of them wrote a note, in Catalan: "We like the Exhibition's campanile better than Utebo's." A campanile is a bell tower. The researchers read it as meaning their own design, the one in front of you. If they're right, the travellers liked their version more than the original.

The original is the Mudéjar bell tower of the church of Nuestra Señora de la Asunción at Utebo, near Zaragoza. Its own inscription, in glazed ceramic, says it was finished in 1544, by the master builder Alonso de Leznes. It's known as "the bell tower of the mirrors", because of "the reflections and glints that the sun produces on the ceramics that cover it".

Why is an Aragonese tower here at all? Because of the first trip. The famous town on the travellers' list, Santillana del Mar, let them down. Aragon surprised them, above all the tower of Utebo. So they swapped the Baroque models they had planned for Mudéjar ones, in brick and glazed ceramic, like Utebo's tower.

The church beside the tower changed with it. The first design was Baroque, fusing churches from Amorebieta and Caldes de Montbui. What was built instead is three Aragonese buildings made into one church: the front of the Carmelite church of Alcañiz, this tower, and a façade from Torralba de Ribota.

And the tower itself is a cast. It's slightly smaller than the real one. The builders made a whole plaster mould for each of its four faces, and assembled them on site, as if it were a prefabricated piece.

Now look up at it. The real tower's ceramics flash in the sun. If the sun is out, wait a moment: does this one catch the light too? That's the effect that gave the original its nickname.

The Poble's ten-minute show of Spain's festivals, Fiesta, is, in its own words, "inside the iconic Utebo Tower".

## Catalonia

### 012 · Carrer de la Davallada — Seven steps, not fourteen
*Carrer de la Davallada · house 83, facing the outside stair · 256 words*

**What:** House 83, Can Banús, modelled on a house in Rupit (province of Barcelona; DOC: Banús, "el 83 amb el de carrer de la Davallada"). Someone from Rupit once stood where you're standing and did something very simple. They counted.

Rupit's own local journal places Can Banús here, at number 83: the Poble's version of a house in Rupit, a village in the province of Barcelona. It counts eight Rupit houses in the village, on five fronts: this one, and four more just above, on Carrer Mercaders.

The writer put it like this, in Catalan: "In the Poble Espanyol the stair has seven steps, half as many as here in Rupit, which has fourteen."

Half a stair. And you'd never know, unless someone from Rupit told you.

So count them, from the bottom step to the top.

That small number is the best answer to anyone who calls this village a set of exact replicas. You saw the brick tower, cast in plaster and a little smaller than the real one in Utebo. Here the stair is halved. Each house is the Poble's version of its original, not a copy.

The same writer noticed something else. Rupit once agreed to put a little sign on each of its houses here. In the article's words, "over the years, the signs gradually disappeared." If you can't find one here today, now you know why, and you know its name anyway.

Seven steps. Keep that number for the rest of the walk.

It was also born under a dictatorship, General Primo de Rivera's. The Catalan encyclopedia says the Exhibition was "organised by the Dictatorship's city council with the help of the state", and that it left the city seriously in debt.

There are two ways to read it, and both are argued seriously. The historian Jordana Mendelson, as researchers quote her in Spanish, sees Primo de Rivera "building in Barcelona an architectural representation of a unified Spain". Sirés says, in Catalan, that the project was "accepted and ideologically swallowed" by the dictatorship. The idea of "the indissoluble unity of the Spanish nation" found favour with the regime, and there were "strong pressures from the central government".

The same study gives the other side its full weight. The village's four makers, it points out, were Catalanists, and Utrillo's idea, it argues, came before the dictatorship. That, it says, "substantially complicates the simple, reductionist interpretation" that the Poble Espanyol was the dictatorship's work.

Born under one dictatorship, made by Catalanists, and later used by another regime, Franco's. All three are true.

The oldest-looking building in the village, and one of the most borrowed.

### 018 · Plaza de la Hermandad — Left out, then added for the King
*Plaza de la Hermandad · white walls round you · 359 words*

Andalusia, the region so many people picture when they think of Spain, was left off the map on purpose.

In 1929 Seville was holding its own Ibero-American Exhibition. So when the four makers planned their first long journey, the historians write, they left out Andalusia and Murcia, so as not to compete. The islands were left out at first, too.

Then the King took an interest. Alfonso the Thirteenth, write the researchers who found the team's notebooks, in Spanish, "asked about the Andalusian representation" and "made it plain how much it would be missed". Exactly when he said it, the sources don't agree.

In May 1928, with building already under way here, the team set off again: a third trip, twenty-one days in the south. An Andalusian quarter was added and moved within the plan, with what the historians call "whitewashed walls, and interiors grouped round courtyards". Murcia came in too.

Look round you. The Poble's own description, in Spanish, promises "typical streets with little white houses and blue flowerpots". See if you can spot the blue pots. Then look for a front with three balconies. From the Casa-Palacio García de Veas, in Arcos de la Frontera, "the façade and the three balconies have been recreated", the Poble says. The two side ones have enclosed railings, the middle one a railing of wrought iron. On the original, in Cádiz, the front carries seven family names, García and Veas among them.

The Poble's list adds Úbeda, Córdoba, Écija and Ronda: with Arcos, five Andalusian provinces. So it's the Andalusian quarter, never the Sevillian one.

Since February 2024 the Vidre art gallery at 67 and 68, "the iconic former glassblowing workshop" in the Poble's words, has shown contemporary art.

The south came last. The historians credit a king who asked where it was.

### 020 · Plaza del Carmen — War, prisoners and a folk museum

**Where:** In the small Plaza del Carmen at the foot of the brick tower, or walking on past the tower and along the church to the Plaza de la Iglesia and the top of the Gradas de Santiago; the track needs no view. While the Exhibition lasted, this was a village of fiestas, with folk dances, pageants, even riding contests. What came after is told less often. You can keep walking while you listen, past the tower toward the Plaza de la Iglesia.

First, a library. The National Art Museum of Catalonia records that in 1934 its library opened "in the Casa de la Junta de Museos in the Poble Espanyol". When the war came, it was sent to Olot with the museum's art and documents. After the war, it came back.

Then the war itself. The Poble's own history calls it "the darkest episode in its history", and says: "the brutal war saw its conversion into a prisoner internment camp." That is the Poble's account. The sources we have read don't say who held the prisoners, how many, or when. A count of the Francoist camps in Catalonia, sixteen of them, published by the newspaper Ara, does not mention the Poble.

"In the aftermath of the war," the same history goes on, "the Poble was in danger of falling into ruin."

In 1942 the empty houses found a use, as a museum of popular industries and arts, the Museu d'Indústries i Arts Populars. Today's Museu Etnològic says it was "dedicated to Catalan and Iberian Peninsula culture", with collections on "trades, traditions and everyday life". Its ethnological section was designed by Ramon Violant i Simorra. The Poble's own history adds another line: "Franco's regime used the museum to disseminate violently anti-Catalanist messages." Two institutions, two descriptions. We give you both.

From the late fifties to the early seventies, the city tried to relaunch the village and the hill of Montjuïc. But, the Poble's history admits, "the venue still failed to function." The folk museum stayed, in one form or another, until the end of the century.

A village built for a fiesta: a library, then prisoners, then a folk museum.

### 023 · Town hall — Valderrobres, and a building site
*Town hall (21) · under the arcade, by the exhibition door · 349 words*

**Where:** Under the arcade along the front of the town hall, house 21, the big arcaded building on the Plaza Mayor, beside the door of the photographic exhibition.

**What:** House 21 (DOC): the town hall, modelled on the Ayuntamiento of Valderrobres / Vall-de-roures (Matarranya, Teruel, Aragon), Mannerist, finished 1599 (date on the coat of arms), by Antonio de Champanyach (LPMB; not spoken). On the first of October 1927, near the end of a month on the road, a message from the trip reads, in Catalan: "We set off on the last stage: Alcañiz, Valderrobres, Tortosa."

Valderrobres, Vall-de-roures in Catalan, is a hill town in Aragon. Its town hall is a late-Renaissance, Mannerist building, finished in 1599. That date is carved on the coat of arms on its front, and the coat of arms has two griffins, a male and a female. Look up: does the Poble's version carry a dated coat of arms too?

Because this isn't Valderrobres as it stands. The historians who found the team's notebooks write, in Spanish, that the reproduction is "clearly larger than the original". It stands on its own, where the original sits on a corner of its square. It was given a staircase from the monastery of Orihuela, and a ceiling on the main floor with the same interlaced pattern as Valencia's old court building, the Audiencia. The team even went to Valderrobres to check details and take measurements.

If the door is open, the Poble says you'll find inside "A photographic journey. The construction of Poble Espanyol": photographs, letters and drawings from the trips.

The contract went to a company called Material y Obras, "with several votes against", as a Catalan study puts it. Work began in January 1928. More than a thousand workers from across the country built it, the Poble says, and it opened in May 1929, about sixteen months later. A Madrid architecture journal later wrote that the structures and fronts were built "on an entirely provisional basis". A temporary set, then, but solid enough to survive. ### 024 · Plaza Mayor — An irremediably trivial idea
*Plaza Mayor · sitting on the steps · 360 words*

**Where:** Sitting on the steps of the Plaza Mayor, anywhere quiet with the square in view (under ALT-X, anywhere quiet at the edge of the square).

**What:** What critics thought of the Poble, 1929 to 1961, and its later life: the 1929–30 critics in D'Ací i d'Allà (Just Cabot, Domènec Carles, Carles Soldevila); the "merry-go-round" years; the unsigned 1961 article in Arquitectura (COAM), both halves; Bohigas's "psychological habitability" (not named); 1986 municipal plan and private management (Maragall, 30-year concession); the 1990 bar in the gate; losses and the 1996 suspension of payments; the 1996 take-over; 2017 visitors (DATED).

In 1961 an architecture journal quoted a colleague on this village, in Spanish: "Here is an irremediably trivial idea, expressed, miraculously, with such acute sensitivity."

It hadn't always been so grudging. The journal recalled how three Barcelona critics, Just Cabot, Domènec Carles and Carles Soldevila, "admiringly asked" for the village to be kept. They wrote in a fine issue of the Catalan magazine D'Ací i d'Allà. The Poble's own history agrees: it was "such a success" that it was not demolished.

Then fashion turned. In the years when architects were inventing zoning and vertical green cities, the journal wrote, the village could only be seen as "a merry-go-round for tourism". "As nicely decorated as you like by four sensitive artists," it went on, "but only, and simply, a merry-go-round." It "very soon fell into general disregard."

The 1961 verdict cuts both ways. The idea of a Spanish village at a world's fair, it said, is "neither very original nor very good", even "a rather crazy and extraordinarily dangerous idea". And yet it found these streets "perfectly, deliciously habitable", just as it found so many new developments "scandalously uninhabitable". More than half a million people had come the year before. Later critics studied it as a lesson in what one called "psychological habitability".

In 1986, with democracy back, the city set out to make it, in the Poble's words, "a centre for cultural, leisure and commercial activities". Under the mayor, Pasqual Maragall, its running went to private managers on a thirty-year concession. A night bar opened in the gate you came in by. But the losses kept coming, and a decade on, the consortium running it went into suspension of payments. Then "a group of businessmen" took over its management, and the Poble's history says it began to recover.

In 2017, by the city's figures, about 1.3 million came: the fifth most-visited place of interest in Barcelona.

Trivial idea or deliciously habitable? You're sitting in the evidence. You decide.

"You could put multimedia attractions anywhere," the Poble's director general, Anton Vidal, said in 2018, "but you cannot build a town like this."

This town was built as a temporary set, meant to come down when the Exhibition was over. It never did. Today it is protected heritage in its own right, listed as a Bé Cultural d'Interès Local, a cultural asset of local interest.

Why did it last? The researchers who found the team's notebooks give an answer, in Spanish. The journeys, they write, stressed the popular character of the project. Then they go further. Without the journey, the Poble Espanyol would not just be very different: "it would probably no longer exist. It would have disappeared long ago."

Think back over what you've seen. A gate modelled on the walls of Ávila. A brick tower cast in plaster moulds. An outside stair with seven steps, where Rupit's has fourteen. A Romanesque monastery that is several places in one. A quarter the historians credit to a king who asked where Andalusia was. And this square, laid out like Riaza's, its fronts gathered from across the country. Interpretation, not copies.

In May 2029 the village turns a hundred.

The Exhibition's centenary is coming too, and Montjuïc is being remodelled, with work timed toward it.

Before you go, if you have time. The Poble shows photographs of the building site in the town hall. The glassblower works at house 81, on Carrer Mercaders. Fiesta, the ten-minute show, is in the brick tower on Plaza Aragonesa, the Poble says. And the Fran Daurel Museum is on the Plaza de la Iglesia.

One last borrowing, as you go. The historians write that, on this side of the square, you come in through the porticoes of Sangüesa's town hall, from Navarre. If this arcade is the one they mean, even the way in was borrowed.
