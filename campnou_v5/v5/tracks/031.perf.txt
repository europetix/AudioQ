[amused] Some signings happen in an office. László Kubala signed his at half past eight in the evening, on the 15th of June 1950, straight after a friendly at Sarrià, against Espanyol. In Catalonia he would be Ladislau.

The man who got the signature was Josep Samitier, the star of Barça's team in the 1920s, by then the club's technical secretary. Kubala was Hungarian, and on the run, touring with Hungaria, a team of footballers who had fled from behind the Iron Curtain. The club's history pages call him a political refugee "being pursued by the Hungarian federation". At first he couldn't play competitive matches at all. His coach was his own brother-in-law, Ferdinand Daučík.

Then he played. In February 1952 he scored seven goals against Sporting Gijón, in a nine–nil win. That equalled the league record, which had been set against Barça. No one has matched it since. [wry] Afterwards, his coach was not impressed: "Kubala scored goals today, but he didn't have a great game."

That season his team won five trophies: the league, the Spanish cup, the Latin Cup, the Copa Eva Duarte and the Copa Martini Rossi. [lightly] You've probably heard of two of them. People still call that side the team of the Five Cups.

[pause] And people say this stadium was built because of Kubala. The club's own historians say no: the plans were in place before he signed. What he did do was fill Les Corts, the old ground. It held tens of thousands, and it still wasn't enough.

A statue of him stood at the main stand's entrance from 2009, the work of the sculptor Montserrat Garcia Rius.

Look at the stadium in front of you. It wasn't built for him. It was built for crowds like the ones he drew.

He didn't build it. He filled the one before.
