Real Madrid eleven, Barcelona one. It was the 13th of June 1943, in Madrid, the second leg of a cup semi-final, and it was eight–nil at half time. Barça had won the first leg three–nil, at home at Les Corts. [pause] The score is the one thing nobody disputes.

The build-up was bitter. After the first leg, a Madrid paper, Ya, accused Barça's fans of having whistled Madrid's players "with the clear intention of attacking the representatives of Spain". According to a Guardian writer, Madrid's fans were given free whistles at the gate for the return.

Barcelona's players later told a darker story. Before kick-off, they said, Spain's director of state security came into their dressing room and reminded them who allowed them to play. A historian, Joan Barau, told Goal that Barcelona "were overcome by military pressure". But he also says that the dressing-room story is one nobody has ever been able to prove. Barça finished the game with ten men.

Real Madrid's own history tells the night another way. It says nothing about whistles or visitors. In Spanish, it calls the second leg an extraordinary comeback, and it adds: "This is where the eternal rivalry between the two clubs was born."

So there are two readings, and neither can be settled now. Barcelona's players remembered pressure. Madrid remembers a comeback. And Barcelona's own record is less simple than the legend. As the club itself has set out, Barça gave General Franco three honours over the decades, and its members only voted to withdraw them in 2019.

[lightly] Both cities still tell this match differently. So if you ever hear someone say eleven–one, it's worth asking which city they're from.
