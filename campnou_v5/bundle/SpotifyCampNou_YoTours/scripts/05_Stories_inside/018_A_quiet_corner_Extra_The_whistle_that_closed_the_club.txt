On Sunday the fourteenth of June, 1925, Barça played a friendly at Les Corts, the club's ground in those years. The afternoon was a tribute to the Orfeó Català, a Catalan choir.

That afternoon, a band of sailors from a British ship in the port struck up the Spanish national anthem. Most of the fourteen thousand people in the ground whistled and booed it. Then the band played God Save the King, and the crowd applauded.

Spain was living under the dictatorship of General Primo de Rivera, and the club's centenary history reads the whistles as a protest against it. Ten days later, the civil governor of Barcelona shut the club down. The decree said six months.

The man who paid most was the founder. Gamper had been the club's president five times. Now he was forced out of his own club and, in the club's words, temporarily exiled to Switzerland. He never led Barça again.

It was not the first time the club had been in danger. In 1908, when it was close to folding, Gamper had stood up at a meeting and taken charge. The club's history pages give his words that night: "Barcelona cannot die and must not die."

In 1930, Gamper took his own life. It was probably, the same history says, because of his business problems, and the forced closure of the club he loved.

The dictatorship that closed it is long gone, and so is the founder. But the club he said could not die is all around you.
