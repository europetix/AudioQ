Track 00 · Welcome and Navigation
Guide
Before you walk in · How to use this tour

  4 min


  ◆ STOP & LOOK

  Anywhere convenient before you enter — on the metro, in a café, or walking up from the Palau
  Reial station. You don’t need to be at the stadium yet. The entrance you’re looking for is the
  former ice rink, on the south side of the complex, off Carrer d’Arístides Maillol — not the main

Welcome to Barcelona. And welcome to Camp Nou — or what’s left of it, and what’s
rising in its place.

This is the largest football stadium in Europe. Or it was, before they started taking it
apart in June 2023. Right now, in 2026, you’re visiting a building site — a 1957 concrete
bowl with its roof off and its third tier half-demolished, while a 105,000-seat stadium is
rebuilt around the empty pitch. The team plays here at reduced capacity. The traditional
stadium tour — down the tunnel, into the dressing room, out to the grass, the
photograph beside the manager’s bench — doesn’t exist anymore. If you’ve seen photos
from a friend’s 2019 visit, set those aside. The stadium experience you’ve already
booked is a different and, honestly, stranger thing. A building in the middle of becoming
itself.

What exists today is the Barça Immersive Tour — a temporary museum in the former ice
rink next door. Eighteen rooms. A 360-degree video chamber. And a viewing deck
pointed straight into the construction site. That’s what we’re going to walk together.

Finding the right entrance
First thing. People get lost here. The complex is large and the signage still points at
parts of the old stadium that no longer function. So walk toward the south side, along
Carrer d’Arístides Maillol. You’re looking for the low building marked Barça Immersive
Tour or FCB Museum. It used to be the Palau de Gel — the ice rink. Not the main stadium
gate. Not the megastore door, which is the exit. The ice rink is where you go in.

If you find yourself standing in front of a tall stadium façade with no obvious entrance,
you’ve drifted too far north. Turn around and walk south until you see the queue under a
glass canopy. That’s it.

A one-way route

Now, an important thing about how this museum is laid out. The route is one-directional.
Once you pass through the 360-degree immersive room, two-thirds of the way through,
you cannot go back. The doors behind you lock. So if there’s something you want a
second look at — the napkin Messi was signed on, the Ballons d’Or, the Cruyff jersey,
the women’s team display — take that second look the first time. You won’t get another
chance without buying a new ticket.

Where to linger, where to move
A few hard-earned tips from people who’ve done this. Most visitors spend three-quarters
of their time in the first few rooms — the entry video, the trophy gallery, the Cruyff
space — and then rush the construction viewpoint at the end. Don’t do that. The
construction viewpoint is the single most unusual thing in this complex right now, and it
will not exist a year from now. Give it ten full minutes. Two minutes of looking; eight
minutes of letting your eye actually settle into the scale of what you’re seeing.

Two specific things people miss. The Sunyol monument — a small memorial to the club’s
president executed during the Civil War — sits outside the museum building, on the
perimeter walk. We’ll talk about him in Track Two. After the tour, walk the outer ring and
find him. And the napkin Messi was first contracted on. It’s in a small display case in the
Messi room and most people walk past it. We’ll point to it when we get there.

Practical bits
Layers. The immersive rooms are kept cold for the projectors; the construction
viewpoint is outdoor and exposed. You’ll want a thin jacket on hand. Photography is fine
almost everywhere; the two exceptions are the 360-degree room itself (during the
show) and the Espai Barça model gallery, where flash isn’t allowed because of the
lighting design. Headphones aren’t required — you’re listening to me — but if you have
a pair, the immersive room sound mix is noticeably better with them in.

One real warning. The 360-degree immersive room has strong, fast, strobing light
effects — long sequences, bright pulses. If you have epilepsy or any photo-sensitivity,
skip it. There’s a bypass corridor and the stewards know to wave you around. Don’t be
shy about asking.

How long this takes
The narration on this tour runs about 70 minutes. Add walking, stopping, looking, the
inevitable shop browse at the end, and you’ll spend about two hours in here. Eleven
tracks. The first is outside, before you go in. The last is at the megastore exit, in the
Barcelona sun.

Track 01 · The Gates of Les Corts
Outside · The cranes, the dust, and the scale of Espai Barça

  5 min


  ◆ STOP & LOOK

  Stand on the sidewalk of Carrer d’Arístides Maillol, just outside the security fencing. Look up. The
  bowl is in front of you. The cranes rise above it. The Barça Immersive Tour entrance is to your left,
  in the low building that used to be the Palau de Gel — the ice rink.


Stop here. Look up.

What you’re seeing isn’t a stadium. Not yet. It’s a stadium being rebuilt around its own
bones. The bowl in front of you opened on the 24th of September 1957. For 66 years it
stood essentially the same shape — a deep, oval concrete crater, three tiers, the largest
seating capacity in Europe. Then, on the 1st of June 2023, the demolition started.

Look at the third tier. Look at the top ring of seats. It’s gone. The entire upper rim of the
stadium has been taken down. Where you can see raw concrete edges and exposed
steel — that’s the cut. The cranes you’re looking at are putting the new third tier back,
taller and wider, with the final capacity set at 105,000 seats. When the stadium opens
fully, it will be the largest in Europe again. By a long way.

What you’re actually looking at
This project is called Espai Barça. The total budget started around 1.5 billion euros and
has climbed since. The main contractor is a Turkish firm called Limak Holding. The site
runs 24 hours a day, three shifts, every day of the week. You’re hearing some of that
now — the rumble of diesel engines, the high beep of reversing trucks, the clang of
steel.

The original reopening was supposed to be November 2024. It slipped to 2025. The
team returned to a partially open stadium on the 22nd of November 2025 — four-nil
against Athletic Bilbao, with about 45,000 fans in the two open tiers. Capacity has crept
up since. The roof — the great tensioned ring that will eventually cover every seat —
won’t go on until 2027. So what you’re seeing today is somewhere in the middle. A
working construction site that occasionally has a football match in it.

The neighborhood under your feet
Look down. The asphalt here is Les Corts — a working-class district of Barcelona that
used to be its own village before the city absorbed it. The stadium’s real name on official
paperwork is just Camp Nou. New field, in Catalan. Because the previous one — Camp

de Les Corts, just a few streets from here — had become too small for the crowds. The
club needed something bigger. They got it.

The sponsor name was added in 2022. Spotify Camp Nou. The Spotify logo now sits on
the front of the shirt and on the building. Locals — if you listen to them in the queue for
tickets — just call it Camp Nou.

Now walk to the entrance on your left. The Barça Immersive Tour. Through the doors.
Through the bag check. Stop in front of the large video wall that opens the exhibition —
black-and-white footage, packed terraces, voices in Catalan. That’s where Track Two

Track 02 · Més que un club
Inside · Sunyol, the language, and a club that became a country

  8 min


  ◆ STOP & LOOK

  You’re inside now. The first room of the Immersive Tour is a large dark space with a long curved
  video wall showing archive footage — 1920s, 1930s, packed terraces, the old flag of Catalonia.
  Stand in front of it. Take your time.


Look at the faces on the screen. Black and white, grainy, mostly men in flat caps. The
crowd is packed. They’re shouting, but you can’t hear what. Some of them are dead
now. Some of them were killed.

The club’s motto is on the wall behind you. Més que un club. More than a club. It’s the
line everyone knows. The slogan on the scarves, the banner unfurled at every home
match. But it isn’t a marketing line. It was first said out loud by a club president named
Narcís de Carreras in 1968, and it meant something specific. It meant that when you
have no country, your football team can become one.

Where the club came from
FC Barcelona was founded on the 29th of November 1899, in a gymnasium on Carrer
Montjuic del Carme, by eleven men. The founder was a twenty-two-year-old Swiss
accountant named Joan Gamper — Hans Gamper at birth, in Winterthur — who had
placed a small advertisement in a sports weekly called Los Deportes a few weeks
earlier. The ad asked anyone interested in playing football to get in touch. Eleven people
did. They met at the gymnasium. They voted Gamper president. They chose blue and
garnet for the colors. Nobody is sure why — there are theories about a Swiss school
crest, theories about the colors of Gamper’s hometown, theories that they just liked
them.

A Swiss man, in Barcelona, founding a football club for foreigners and locals together.
That’s the origin. Within a generation, it had become something else.

Why this place is political
Catalonia — the region you’re standing in — has its own language, its own flag, its own
thousand-year history of being not-quite-Spain. Through the twentieth century, two
dictatorships tried to erase that. Primo de Rivera in the 1920s. And then, much more
brutally, Francisco Franco from 1939 until his death in 1975. Under Franco, the Catalan
language was banned in public. The flag — the senyera, four red stripes on yellow —

MÉS QUE UN CLUB                                                     Spotify Camp Nou · Barcelona




was forbidden. Catalan place names were changed. Catalan first names were refused at
the civil registry. The Catalan government was abolished. Its president was executed.

And the football stadium — first the old Camp de Les Corts, then this one after 1957 —
became one of the only places in the city where you could be Catalan in public. You
could speak the language in the stands. You could sing in it. You could shout in it. The
regime knew, and didn’t intervene, because how do you arrest 90,000 people at once.

So when Barcelona played Real Madrid — the team most associated with the Spanish
state and, fairly or not, with Franco himself — it wasn’t just football. It was the only legal
Catalan demonstration in Spain. That’s the context for what people mean when they say
més que un club. The club was a country in costume.

Josep Sunyol
Now I have to tell you the hardest part of this story, because the man the motto really
belongs to is buried in a roadside grave outside Madrid.

His name was Josep Sunyol i Garriga. Born in Barcelona, 1898. Wealthy Catalan family.
He was a lawyer, a journalist, a left-wing politician of the Esquerra Republicana, a
member of the Spanish parliament, the founder of a sports weekly called La Rambla
whose slogan was “sport and citizenship”. And in 1935, he was elected the
twenty-eighth president of FC Barcelona.

He held the office for one year.

In late July 1936, the Spanish Civil War broke out. Sunyol travelled from Barcelona to
Madrid — first to Valencia, then on to the besieged Spanish capital — to meet with fellow
Republican deputies. On the morning of the 6th of August 1936, he left Madrid in his
chauffeur-driven car, flying the Catalan senyera from the bonnet. He drove north, into
the Sierra de Guadarrama. The hills above the road were a confusing patchwork of
Republican and Franco-aligned positions. Sunyol’s car crossed a line without realising it.
It was stopped at a checkpoint manned by Falangist troops. He was identified — the
senyera on the car gave him away. He was taken out and shot. Without trial. He was
thirty-eight years old.

The news took a week to reach Barcelona.

Under Franco, his name was scrubbed. For nearly sixty years the club he led didn’t
officially mourn him. His body was only exhumed in the 1990s. In 1996, on the sixtieth
anniversary of his death, the club held its first formal tribute. In 2003, a corner of this
complex was dedicated to him — the Monument a Josep Sunyol. If you walk the
perimeter after this tour, you can find it.

He is the reason the motto means what it means.

Sixty years later, a referendum
One more thing before we move. Skip forward to the 1st of October 2017. The Catalan
government held an independence referendum that the Spanish state had declared
illegal. Spanish national police were sent to Catalonia to stop people voting. They raided
polling stations. They beat voters. Hundreds were injured.

That same afternoon, Barcelona were scheduled to play Las Palmas, at this stadium, in
front of 90,000 fans. The club asked La Liga to postpone the match. La Liga refused. So
the club played the game with the stadium doors locked and the stands empty. The only
competitive match at Camp Nou in its history played to no one. The players ran out to
silence. The match was streamed live. Lionel Messi scored a hat-trick into an empty
bowl.

The empty stadium that day said exactly what the motto says. Més que un club.

Now walk left, into the trophy gallery. The lights brighten. The next room is silverware.

Track 03 · The Silverware
Inside · Wembley 1992 and the five European nights

  6 min


  ◆ STOP & LOOK

  Walk into the long, brightly lit trophy gallery. The display cases run along both walls. Find the line
  of larger trophies on the right — the Champions League cups, distinctive for their size and the
  heavy wraparound handles. There are five of them.


You’re looking at five replicas. UEFA keeps the originals; every winner gets a copy to put
in their museum. But each one represents a night the club won the European Cup. Five
nights, scattered across twenty-three years, that rewrote what this team meant in the
world.

Wembley, 20th May 1992
Start with the first one. Look at the plaque. London, 1992. Wembley. Final against
Sampdoria. The team you’re looking at on the photograph below is the Dream Team —
Cruyff’s side. Stoichkov, Laudrup, Bakero, Guardiola at the base of midfield, Koeman at
the back.

The match went to extra time, nil-nil. In the 112th minute, Barça got a free kick about
twenty-five yards out. Koeman — the tall Dutch centre-back with a freakishly hard right
foot — stepped over the ball. Bakero rolled it sideways. Koeman struck it. The ball flew
low, hard, just inside the post. Sampdoria’s goalkeeper Pagliuca didn’t move.

It was the first European Cup in the club’s ninety-three-year history. Until that night,
Barcelona were the great loser of European football — five final appearances before
1992, five losses. The Dream Team broke the curse. The party in Barcelona ran for a
week. Cruyff said the line he used before every important match. Salid y disfrutad. Go
out there and enjoy yourselves.

Paris, Rome, Wembley again, Berlin
Now look at the other four. 2006 — Paris, Frank Rijkaard’s side, Ronaldinho at his
impossible peak, two-one against Arsenal. 2009 — Rome, Pep Guardiola’s first year as
manager, two-nil against Manchester United, with Eto’o and Messi scoring. 2011 —
Wembley again, Guardiola again, three-one against United again, the most complete
performance the competition has ever seen. And 2015 — Berlin, Luis Enrique’s side,
three-one against Juventus, the front three of Messi, Suárez, and Neymar.

Five trophies. Three of them in a span of six years.

And there’s something the men’s display doesn’t mention. The women’s team — the
Barça Femí — have won the Champions League three times in the last five years. 2021,

Bonmatí has won it three times in a row, 2023, 2024, 2025. They are the dominant

Track 04 · Cruyff
Inside · The Dutchman who taught a club how to think

  9 min


  ◆ STOP & LOOK

  Step into the dedicated Cruyff space. There’s a display of his jerseys, his quotes lining the walls,
  archive footage of him as a player and as a manager. Stand in front of the number 14 shirt.


The most important person in the history of this club never grew up here. He grew up
across the road from the Ajax stadium in Amsterdam, the son of a greengrocer who died
when he was twelve. His name was Hendrik Johannes Cruijff. In English we spelled it
Cruyff. He died on the 24th of March 2016, in a hospital in this city, from lung cancer. He
had chain-smoked for forty years. He quit and switched to lollipops; the lollipop is on the
statue of him outside this complex.

He came to Barcelona twice. First as a player, in 1973. Then as a manager, fifteen years
later. The first time he changed the club. The second time he changed football.

The player, 1973
When Cruyff signed from Ajax in August 1973, Barcelona had not won the Spanish
league in fourteen years. He cost about two million dollars, a world record at the time. In
his first season he won the league — his debut included a five-nil win at the Bernabéu,
which the city of Barcelona still talks about. He scored a goal there nobody had ever
seen before, where he hooked his leg behind a ball mid-air and redirected it into the net
while almost facing away from goal. The papers called it el gol imposible. The impossible
goal.

He played five seasons here. He led the league in goals. He won the Ballon d’Or in 1973
and 1974. He didn’t win another European Cup. But he did something else.

The son named Jordi
In February 1974, his wife gave birth to their third child. Cruyff named the boy Jordi — in
Catalan, after the patron saint of Catalonia. Saint George.

Under Franco, the Spanish civil registry was officially banned from accepting
non-Spanish first names. Jordi was rejected. The boy was registered in the Netherlands
instead, then brought back to Spain. The name was forbidden on paper. Cruyff used it
anyway. He insisted his son be called Jordi, in interviews, on team sheets, in the press.

It is hard to overstate what that did. A foreign superstar, recently arrived, refusing the
Franco regime’s naming law on behalf of his Catalan newborn. Within a year of that
gesture, Franco was dead. Two years after that, the name Jordi was legal again in Spain.
The story is the reason a lot of Catalans, in their seventies now, will tell you Cruyff is
theirs.

The manager, 1988
He came back in 1988 as manager. The club was a mess. Crowd violence, internal
politics, no recent trophies. Cruyff arrived with a single philosophical idea — that football
is the simplest game made unnecessarily complicated.

His premise was this. If you have the ball, the other team cannot score. So keep the ball.
Move it quickly. Move yourself even faster than the ball moves. Pass it short. Build
triangles, always triangles, three players in shifting geometric relation to each other, so
there are always two passing options. Use space the way a painter uses canvas. The
pitch is wide; make it wider when you have the ball, make it narrower when you don’t.

The label tiki-taka came later. It was coined by a Spanish broadcaster named Andrés
Montes during the 2006 World Cup. Cruyff actually disliked it. He thought it sounded
trivial. But it stuck.

Four leagues and Wembley
Cruyff’s team won four straight La Liga titles, 1991 through 1994. And in 1992, the
Wembley night you just heard about, the European Cup. Before the players walked out
of the tunnel, he told them his line. Salid y disfrutad. Go out there and enjoy yourselves.
He believed football was an entertainment art form. The point was joy, not survival.

When the final whistle blew at Wembley and Barça had won one-nil, Cruyff stood on the
touchline with both hands in his trouser pockets, watching his players celebrate. He
didn’t run on. He didn’t cry. He just stood there. He had been waiting nineteen years for
that moment.

What he leaves behind
He was sacked in 1996 after a fight with the club president, Josep Lluís Núñez. He never
coached anywhere significant again. But everything that happened next at this club —
Pep Guardiola, who Cruyff promoted from the youth team to the first team, who later
coached the 2009 and 2011 sides in the trophy room behind you; Xavi, Iniesta, Messi, all
of whom were trained on Cruyff’s ideas as children — all of it traces back to him.

When he died in 2016, the team played their next match wearing white armbands. The
fourteenth minute of the next home match — his shirt number — was held in silence.
Eighty-five thousand people, standing without a word. For sixty seconds.

Track 05 · La Masia
Inside · The 1702 farmhouse and the boys it raised

  6 min


  ◆ STOP & LOOK

  Move into the La Masia gallery. The centerpiece is a model or illuminated image of the original
  stone farmhouse, surrounded by touch-screens showing youth academy players. Stand in front of
  the farmhouse image.


The building you’re looking at is older than this club by 197 years. It was built in 1702. A
Catalan farmhouse — a masia — of stone and tile, with a wide arched gate carved with
the coat of arms of Santa Eulàlia, patron saint of Barcelona, and the date of its
rebuilding cut into the keystone.

It belonged to a farming family called Planes, who grew olives and grapes on the land.
For most of its history, nobody who lived there had heard of football. Then in 1950, the
last owner — Francesc Planes Buera — sold the building and the surrounding fields to FC
Barcelona. The club needed the land to build Camp Nou.

They didn’t demolish the farmhouse. They took it apart, stone by stone, and rebuilt it on
the other side of the stadium site. It still stands there, about a hundred metres from
where you’re standing now, just outside the construction zone, on Avinguda Joan XXIII.

1979 — Oriol Tort’s idea, Cruyff’s blessing
For the first twenty years after the move, the farmhouse was an architects’ office and
the club’s headquarters. Then, on the 20th of October 1979, it became something else.
The club’s legendary chief scout, a man named Oriol Tort, had proposed an idea to the
then-president Josep Lluís Núñez. The club should bring its most promising young
players — boys from outside Barcelona, often from rural Catalonia or from abroad — to
live full-time at the club. Eat there. Sleep there. Train there. Cruyff, back in Amsterdam
at the time, backed the idea publicly. So the farmhouse was converted into a dormitory.

That building, the converted farmhouse, became known simply as La Masia. The
farmhouse. The most famous youth academy in football.

What it produced
Look at the touchscreens around the room. They show some of the players who came
through. Pep Guardiola, who arrived as a thin thirteen-year-old from a Catalan village in
1984, slept in the farmhouse, and walked out fourteen years later as the captain of
Spain. Carles Puyol. Víctor Valdés. Andrés Iniesta, who arrived at twelve from a town in



                                              — 16 —
MÉS QUE UN CLUB                                                    Spotify Camp Nou · Barcelona




La Mancha and cried every night for a month from homesickness. Xavi Hernández, the
most intelligent passer of his generation. Cesc Fàbregas. Gerard Piqué. Sergio Busquets.
And a small Argentinian boy who we’ll meet in the next room.

In 2010, the three finalists for the Ballon d’Or — the award for the best player in the
world — were Xavi, Iniesta, and Lionel Messi. All three of them had grown up in the same
bunk beds, eaten dinner at the same long wooden tables, learned to pass the ball in the
same training pitches. No academy in the history of football had produced anything like
it. The Spanish national team that won the 2010 World Cup had seven Masia graduates
in the starting eleven.

The closing of the farmhouse
On the 30th of June 2011, the farmhouse stopped housing players. The club moved the
academy to a new, larger residential complex called the Ciutat Esportiva Joan Gamper,
in Sant Joan Despí, on the outskirts of the city. The boys go there now. The 1702 building
still stands, restored and quiet, used for ceremonial visits. You can walk past it after this
tour.

under bright spots. Follow the arrows.

Track 06 · Messi
Inside · The napkin, the 672 goals, and the day he left

  9 min


  ◆ STOP & LOOK

  Step into the darkened circular Messi room. In the centre, a low curved display holds his Ballons
  d’Or and Golden Shoes under spotlight. Footage of his goals plays on the walls around you. Stand
  in the centre and turn slowly.


Stop here. Just look.

Eight golden footballs on rough metal bases. Six Golden Shoes. The trophies of one
human career. Lionel Andrés Messi was born on the 24th of June 1987, in Rosario,
Argentina, to a steelworker father and a cleaner mother. He started playing for his
neighbourhood club, Grandoli, at four. He joined Newell’s Old Boys at six. By eleven, he
was the best player anyone in Argentina could remember seeing at that age. He was
also, by eleven, dangerously small.

The hormone, and the napkin
He had a growth hormone deficiency. His doctors said he needed daily injections of
synthetic hormone or he would not grow past four-foot-seven. The injections cost the
equivalent of about nine hundred dollars a month, which his family in Rosario could not
afford. Newell’s wouldn’t pay. Argentina’s public health system wouldn’t cover it for
sport. River Plate wouldn’t pay. The family was running out of options when a Barça
scout named Horacio Gaggioli watched a video of Messi playing a youth tournament.

He invited the family to fly to Barcelona for a trial. In September 2000, the
thirteen-year-old Messi played for fifteen minutes against the academy boys. He was so
much better than them it was uncomfortable. The club’s sporting director, a man named
Carles Rexach, asked his assistants for a contract. They couldn’t find one. So Rexach
took the only paper to hand — a napkin, at a tennis club restaurant called Pompeya —
and wrote on it. In Barcelona, on the 14th of December 2000, in the presence of Messrs.
Minguella and Horacio — then a few words committing the club, in informal Spanish, to
sign Messi and pay for his treatment. Rexach signed it. The napkin is in this museum
somewhere. Look for it on the way out.

The club paid for the hormone treatment. Messi grew to five-foot-seven. He made his
senior debut on the 16th of October 2004, aged seventeen, in a friendly at the Camp
Nou you cannot currently see. He stayed for twenty-one years.

672 goals
Look up at the walls. The footage cycling around you is some of what he did in those
years.

Six hundred and seventy-two goals for FC Barcelona. The most any player has ever
scored for a single club. Eight Ballon d’Or trophies, more than anyone in history. Ten La
Liga titles. Seven Copa del Rey trophies. Four Champions Leagues — the 2006, 2009,
2011, and 2015 cups in the trophy room you walked through. The Treble in 2009 — La
Liga, Copa del Rey, Champions League — and then the Club World Cup, the European
Super Cup, and the Spanish Super Cup the same year. Six trophies in twelve months. No
one had ever done that.

But the numbers are not really the point. The point is what it looked like. He played
left-footed. He carried the ball at a sprint as if it were attached to him by a string — not
lifted, not bouncing, just glued to the outside of his left foot, two centimetres above the
grass. He could change direction at full speed without slowing down. He never looked
up. He didn’t need to. He could feel the geometry of the field in his peripheral vision the
way other people feel temperature.

In April 2007, against Getafe, he scored a goal almost identical to Maradona’s 1986
World Cup goal against England — he picked up the ball in his own half, beat five
players, rounded the goalkeeper, and stroked it in. He was nineteen. In April 2011, in the
Champions League semi-final against Real Madrid, he scored a solo goal from the right
wing through the entire Madrid defense that ended the tie. There are compilations of
these goals that run for hours.

The end
And then, on the 5th of August 2021, the club announced he was leaving.

What happened was this. Barça, under the previous president Josep Maria Bartomeu,
had run up about a billion euros of debt. Salary commitments, ageing players on long
contracts, bad transfers, the COVID revenue collapse. La Liga had a salary cap. The club
had agreed a new contract with Messi, at a reduced salary. He had publicly accepted.
Then La Liga did the maths and said the club could not register the deal under the cap.
Even at reduced wages. Even with Messi accepting half his old pay.

The club’s new president, Joan Laporta, had a choice. Refuse to register him and let him
leave on a free transfer. Or sell the club’s long-term future to keep him for one more
year. He chose the first.

The press conference was on the 8th of August. Messi walked out in a black suit holding
a tissue. He sat down and tried to read his statement. He started crying before the first
sentence. His wife and three sons were in the front row. He said, in Spanish, that he had

Hold your ground. The screens around you are about to switch on. When they do, you’ll
be inside the old Camp Nou, on a match night, in 2010 or thereabouts. The capacity
then was 99,354. The roof of the stadium was a thin partial canopy that covered the

rolled.

What you’re about to hear is a recording of the club anthem, the Cant del Barça, sung
by the home crowd. The anthem was written in 1974 — the year Cruyff signed — for the
club’s 75th anniversary, with lyrics by the Catalan poets Jaume Picas and Josep Maria
Espinas. It is sung in Catalan. The line everyone joins on is the last one. Tots units fem
força. Som la gent blaugrana, tant se val d’on venim, si del sud o del nord. All united, we
make strength. We are the blaugrana people. It doesn’t matter where we come from, if
from the south or the north.

Why this room exists
The reason this room exists is that the real stadium is sealed. You can’t go in. The pitch,
the dressing rooms, the dugout — all of it is behind plywood and fencing right now. So
the club built a simulator. Floor-to-ceiling screens, surround sound, the visual feeling of
standing on the pitch at kickoff. It’s a substitute for the thing you came to see. It is also,
when the lights come up and the sound rolls, surprisingly close to the real thing.

What you can’t simulate is the bass. The old Camp Nou had a sonic footprint that
opposing players talked about for decades — a deep, low, rumbling roar that built up
under the open sky and pressed down on you from the upper tier. Iker Casillas, who
played here many times for Real Madrid, once said it was like being underwater. You
could feel the crowd before you could hear them. Not just the volume — the pressure.

The new stadium, when it’s finished in 2027, will have a roof. The sound will change.
People who know the old building well are bracing for it to be different. Probably
sharper. Probably less rolling. We’ll find out.

Open your eyes. Take a breath of outside air. You’re standing over the thing you came

Down there, in the crater, is where the pitch was. Where Messi scored the impossible
solo goal against Real Madrid in 2011. Where Ronaldinho played the no-look pass that
the Bernabéu crowd applauded — the rarest honour in football, an opposing crowd
standing for an opposing player. Where Maradona played 36 league matches in 1982
and 1983 before hepatitis and a leg-break ended his Barça career. Where Kubála, di
Stéfano never played but Messi did, and Cruyff did, and Xavi and Iniesta did. That grass
is gone now. The whole base has been excavated.

What you’re seeing
Look at the lower bowl, the part closest to where the pitch was. The original 1957
concrete is still there, exposed, with the seats stripped out. That tier was reinforced
rather than rebuilt — it was structurally sound. So what you’re seeing on the bottom
levels is a 68-year-old concrete shell, scrubbed and repaired.

Now look higher. The second tier is a mix — some of it old, some rebuilt. And the very
top — the third tier, the upper ring — is new. It’s the tier that will lift the final capacity to
105,000 seats. The outer concrete columns rising around the rim — the tall pale grey
pillars circling the bowl — those are the structural supports for the new tier and,
eventually, for the roof. The roof itself is not yet built. It won’t be installed until 2027.
When it goes on, it will be the largest stadium roof in the world.

The hard numbers
A few facts to anchor what you’re looking at. The main contractor is Limak Holding,
based in Ankara, Turkey — chosen on a low bid in late 2022 over Spanish firms, which
caused some local complaint. The site runs 24 hours a day. The budget started at 1.5
billion euros and is now reported as higher; the club has had to take on additional
financing. The original reopening date of November 2024 has slipped multiple times.

The team returned to a partially reopened stadium on the 22nd of November 2025 — a

unsafe conditions, with withheld passports in some cases. The club has said it is
reviewing its subcontracting chain. The story is ongoing. I mention it because the people
building the cathedral are doing the actual work, and the new stadium is being built on
their hours.

draw to clinch their second straight La Liga title with five matches still to play. They got
more than that. The match drew the biggest Clásico crowd at this stadium since the
renovation began — the 62,652 of Phase 1C. The rest of the bowl, the part that’s under
construction in front of you, stayed empty and silent.

Track 09 · The Model
Inside · The finished stadium, scaled to your hand

  5 min


  ◆ STOP & LOOK

  Step into the architectural gallery. The centerpiece is a large, illuminated 3D scale model of the

Here it is. The whole thing, finished, at one-hundredth scale. The stadium you cannot
see from outside because of the cranes.

Walk around the model. Start at the side facing the city — the side closest to the
Diagonal, the wide avenue that runs east into central Barcelona. The exterior of the new
stadium is open. Not closed. The concourses you can see on the model — the ringed
walkways wrapping the bowl on three levels — are open-air. No roof over them, no
enclosed tunnels, no dark interior corridors. Fans walk to their seats on balconies that
look out over the city. The architects — the Japanese firm Nikken Sekkei working with
the Barcelona studio Pascual i Ausíó Arquitectes — wanted the building to breathe like a
Mediterranean structure. Open courtyards. Sea breeze through the corridors. The
opposite of a sealed Northern European football fortress.

The roof
Now look at the roof. The pale curved disk that floats over the bowl on the model. It
looks light because it is. It’s a tensioned cable-stayed structure — essentially a giant
inverted dish held up by a compression ring of steel running around the top of the third
tier. Every seat in the stadium will be covered by it. Currently, nothing is. The old roof
covered about two-thirds of the seats; the new one will cover everything.

The top surface of the roof will be tiled with photovoltaic panels — around 30,000 square
metres of solar collection. The estimated annual generation is enough to make the
complex effectively self-sufficient for electricity, with surplus feeding back into the city
grid. There’s also a rainwater capture system that will irrigate the pitch and feed cooling
for the concourses. None of this is unique — the Mercedes-Benz Stadium in Atlanta and
a handful of European arenas have similar features — but at this scale it would be the
largest self-sufficient stadium in the world.

The motto, returned
