[curious] Frank Gehry remembered exactly how the job was put to him. "They said: 'Mr Gehry, we need the Sydney Opera House. Our town is dying.'"

The deal behind that plea began in early 1991, when the Basque government met Thomas Krens, who ran the Solomon R. Guggenheim Foundation in New York. Krens recalled that the first conversation lasted "no more than half an hour". He had a problem of his own. As one business-school case study tells it, the foundation could show only about three per cent of its collection in New York and Venice. It needed more walls.

His terms were blunt. "All costs, operating and capital" would be borne by the Basque side. The Guggenheim kept the "aesthetic decisions": the architecture, the collection, the programme. The Basques also paid the foundation a fee of twenty million dollars, which critics called a "rental fee", and pledged fifty million dollars more to buy art. [wry] Krens never dressed it up. "It was commerce, pure and simple, that brought together the Guggenheim and Bilbao."

[pause] Then the architect. That same year, three were invited to compete: Arata Isozaki, Coop Himmelblau and Frank Gehry. Each had three weeks and ten thousand dollars to produce a design. The jury met in Frankfurt. Krens said Gehry "provided the most unusual project", and that he "was chosen for the strength of his vision".

Now look at what that vision did with this spot. In the museum's words, "the Museum plaza and main entrance lie in a direct line with Calle Iparragirre". So the long straight street at your back lines up with the door. In front of you, that straight line meets the curves. Beyond the building is the river, and the site was "a former wharf with port and industrial use on a curve of the Nervión". And look at the height. Gehry made something spectacular without letting it rise above the buildings around it.

[quietly] A dying town asked for an opera house. What it signed was a deal its own author called commerce. What it got is in front of you.
