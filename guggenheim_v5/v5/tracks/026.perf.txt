[curious] Before he trusted titanium with a whole museum, Frank Gehry gave it a simple test. In the museum's words, he "chose titanium after ruling out other materials and seeing the behavior of a titanium sample pinned outside his office".

This is the skin up close. The museum counts "approximately 33,000 extremely thin titanium sheets". The builder gives their thickness as about a third of a millimetre, thinner than a credit card. That thinness, the builder says, is why the panels look gently quilted rather than flat.

Now look at the colour. The museum says the finish "provides a rough and organic effect", and that the metal's colour changes "depending on the weather and light conditions". Is it silver today, or grey, or something warmer? It won't look quite the same tomorrow.

Then there was the timing. As BusinessWeek told it, "In the early 1990s, the end of the Reagan-era military buildup meant fewer orders of titanium-laden jet fighters." Soviet-built titanium factories hit the same slump. "Suddenly, all that capacity was for sale," a titanium executive said. The price drop, Gehry's office said, let him choose titanium over stainless steel, copper or aluminium.

[wry] Which brings us to the legend. You'll hear that this skin came from old Soviet submarines. Even a writer who tells that story ends with: "We'll probably never know." Enjoy it as a legend.

The other two materials, in the museum's words, "limestone and glass, harmonize perfectly". The stone wraps the box-shaped galleries, and the titanium the free curves. Find where stone meets metal, and remember: no two joints in the building are the same.

[pause] One change you can't see was made to help this skin last. Gehry's office proposed a plywood backing. The executive architects, IDOM, swapped it for galvanised sheet metal: more durable, and more expensive.

[warmly] A test sample, a price slump, and a skin built to last.
