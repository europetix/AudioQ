[curious] You'll often read that this Atrium is fifty metres high. The museum gives a different figure: a height of fifty-five metres. The fifty seems to come from the builders' scaffolding, which reached close to fifty metres in here. The room itself goes higher.

The museum describes it as "an ample space flooded with light and covered by a great skylight". It has "curved volumes and large glass curtain walls that connect the inside and the outside". It calls the Atrium "the heart and soul of the Museum" and "a point of reference for visitors". That's practical advice, too. From here you can reach every gallery, and the terrace outside.

[pause] Everything hangs on this space. "The three levels of the building are organized around the Atrium and are connected by means of curved walkways, titanium and glass elevators, and staircases." Around it open about twenty galleries, of two kinds. Some are "orthogonally shaped and with classical proportions": boxes, wrapped in stone. Others have "organic, irregular lines", the titanium curves you saw from the plaza.

Now look up, and take your time. Follow the glass walls, where inside meets outside. Find the walkways hanging across the void, and the glass lifts sliding between the floors. At the very top is the great skylight that covers it all. You'll see it closer from the top floor.

[lightly] Look for one more thing. Those columns of moving red and blue words, in gallery 101, which you can see from the Atrium, are a work by Jenny Holzer, made for this building.

Not everyone was won over. In Sydney Pollack's film about Gehry, the critic Hal Foster argued that the building upstages the art it's meant to serve. Gehry, he said, had given his clients "a sublime space that overwhelms the viewer". [wry] Stand here a minute, and decide whether you agree.

Now find the spot in the Atrium from which you can see into gallery 101 and its tall columns of moving red and blue light: Atrium · Holzer's columns of light.
