[curious] Somewhere above this room runs a road bridge. And yet not one column holds up the ceiling. The museum puts it like this: "Stretching under the bridge, gallery 104—an enormous, column-free space." It's about 130 metres long and 30 wide.

What fills it is The Matter of Time, by Richard Serra: eight sculptures of weathering steel, made between 1994 and 2005, just over a thousand tonnes of it in all. Serra was born in San Francisco. He died in March 2024, at his home on Long Island, aged eighty-five.

[pause] How are you meant to see it? The museum's answer is that "the entire room is part of the sculptural field". The gaps between the pieces matter as much as the steel. Together they make corridors that are wide, narrow, long, compressed, high and low, "always unexpected", in the museum's words. There's no set route. Each visitor finds their own way through.

And the title is meant literally. There are two kinds of time in here. There's the time it takes you to walk through. And there's the time in which you remember what you felt, and piece it back together. Serra said he wanted this to be "a public space, open, where anyone can come, especially young people".

The eight run in a sequence, "from the relative simplicity of a double ellipse to the complexity of a spiral". They end in pieces made from "sections of toruses and spheres". The oldest, Snake, was here for the opening. The other seven came in 2005.

Now look down the gallery. Find the people walking among the pieces, and see how far the steel rises above their heads. Watch someone disappear into a curve, and come out somewhere you didn't expect. [warmly] In a minute, that will be you.

Walk in to the first sculptures at this end of the gallery. Step inside the one nearest you and follow its wall as it curves and leans: Gallery 104 · Walls that lean.
