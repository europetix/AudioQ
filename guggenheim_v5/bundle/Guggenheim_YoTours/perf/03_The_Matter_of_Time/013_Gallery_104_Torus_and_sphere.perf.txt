[curious] A torus is a ring, like a doughnut. A sphere is a ball. Richard Serra cut his steel from both.

These are the last two pieces of The Matter of Time: Between the Torus and the Sphere, and Blind Spot Reversed, both finished in 2005. In the museum's words, they are "created from sections of toruses and spheres". The museum's guide for teachers calls them "the two final works of this evolution", and warns that the pieces "transform in unexpected ways while the visitor is going around them".

[pause] So go around them. Walk the outside of one slowly, and watch its edge change shape against the room. Then step inside. A wall cut from a sphere curves two ways at once, over you and around you. Feel how different that is from a wall that only leans.

The museum also says the installation "includes a progression in time". This is where that progression arrives.

[warmly] Serra grew up close to steel. As AP told his story, he was inspired by the time he spent at a shipyard where his father worked as a pipefitter. Later he worked in steel foundries to help pay for his studies. And here his steel stands on an old riverside wharf, in a city that once built ships.

The curator of this installation, Carmen Giménez, called him, as AP reported her words in English, "beyond doubt the most important living sculptor".

[pause] Before you leave, look along the whole gallery. Eight pieces in a row, and the people moving among them, small against the steel.

At the far end of the gallery, away from the Atrium, there's an educational space with a video showing how the pieces were installed.

If you're on the full tour, there's a short Extra you can play here or on the walk back: the Serra that vanished. The next main stop is outside. Walk back through the gallery to the Atrium and out onto the terrace on the river side: Atrium terrace · Chillida and the river.
