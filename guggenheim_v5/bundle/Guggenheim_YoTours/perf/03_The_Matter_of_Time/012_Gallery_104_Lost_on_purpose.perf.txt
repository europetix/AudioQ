[quietly] "These pieces have an accelerated time," Richard Serra said. "It's a changed time."

You're among the spirals now. Here are two more Torqued Spirals. One is Torqued Spiral, Right, Left. The other is Torqued Spiral, Open, Left, Closed, Right. Torqued, remember, means twisted. Say those titles again, slowly. They sound less like names than directions for a walk.

The museum describes this gallery as a journey "from the relative simplicity of a double ellipse to the complexity of a spiral". These are the complex ones. And its guide for teachers is blunt about what happens inside these works: "Once inside the works, the viewer loses all reference and does not know what lies ahead."

[pause] So go in. Follow a spiral toward its centre. The steel rises well above your head and keeps on curving, and you can't see where the corridor leads until you're in it. Notice whether, at some point, you stop knowing which way you came in.

The same guide says that the spirals, like the twisted ellipses, "seem to be in continuous movement and include the viewer in their movement". Serra put the feeling in his own words: "It's like an abrupt leap through time. It is not linear. It is narrative."

[curious] On the way out, test yourself. Do you remember the turns? Or only the feeling of them?

[wry] Serra wasn't trying to make you comfortable. His rule was simple: "unless the work is formally innovative, nothing will change." A spiral you can't see out of is one way of making sure something does.

[warmly] The whole work is called The Matter of Time. In here, time is the material. You've just walked through some of it.

Carry on to the far end of the gallery, to the last two pieces, built from sections of rings and spheres: Gallery 104 · Torus and sphere.
