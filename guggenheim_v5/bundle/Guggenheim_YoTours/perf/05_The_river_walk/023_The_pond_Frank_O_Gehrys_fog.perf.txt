[curious] The title of this work hides a dedication. Its full name is a catalogue number followed by three letters: F, O, G. And those letters stand for Frank O. Gehry.

It's by the Japanese artist Fujiko Nakaya, and it dates from 1998. In this pond, a thousand nozzles and a high-pressure pump turn water into fog. Robert Rauschenberg invited her to make it for his retrospective here. Soon after his show opened, he bought it and donated it to the museum.

Her first fog sculpture came through a group called Experiments in Art and Technology. They designed the Pepsi-Cola Pavilion for the 1970 world's fair in Osaka, and Nakaya wrapped it in fog.

[warmly] There's a family story in it, too. Her father, Ukichiro Nakaya, was a physicist who, in the words of the Praemium Imperiale, "created the first artificial snow crystal in the world". The daughter of the man who made the first snowflake in a laboratory sculpts with fog. She later won that same prize herself, for sculpture. Gehry had won it, for architecture, in 1992.

In Nakaya's words, fog sculpture is "both a phenomenon and an artifact". She was drawn to forms that "repeatedly form and dissolve themselves".

[pause] It runs on the hour, for about eight minutes. If it's running now, watch the building soften and vanish. If you've just missed it, it comes back on the hour.

If you're on the full tour, there's a short Extra here, about the fire in this same pond. The next main stop is Maman, the giant spider by the footbridge. Follow the river walk toward the big road bridge to find her: River walk · Maman.
