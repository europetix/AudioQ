A big road bridge runs across one end of the museum's site. Many architects might have kept their distance. Gehry slid the long gallery of Serra's sculptures underneath it, and raised a tower to meet it.

Everyone calls it La Salve. Officially it's the Príncipes de España bridge. The engineer Juan Batanero began building it in 1968, and it opened in 1972. It's a cable-stayed bridge: cables from its masts hold up the deck.

Gehry, says a Bilbao guide to the city's bridges, "wanted to integrate, to a certain extent, the museum with the bridge". So he designed a tower with stairs that connect the two. The museum puts it more grandly. The building "ends in a tower, a sculpture gesture that brings the architectural design to a crescendo". And it "effectively incorporates" the bridge "into the building".

For the museum's tenth anniversary, in 2007, came the red frame. The French artist Daniel Buren called it Arcos rojos, in Basque Arku gorriak: red arches. Buren set out to make the bridge's structure "less brutal, but more visible".

Now look at it. A tall red frame stands across the bridge, with three circles cut out of it at equal distances. The middle one is a full circle around the road. The other two are half circles. The edges are striped in black and white. And the red, the museum says, was chosen "to contrast with the green of the bridge".

On the tower, the titanium works like a mirror: watch for a blush of red when the light is right. At night, the frame is lit with LED lights.

If you looked into Kapoor's silver spheres by the pond, these are the red arches you found in them.

This bridge, the museum notes, stands at "the immediate entrance to the city center". Engineers built a bridge to carry traffic into Bilbao. Gehry and Buren made it part of the museum.
