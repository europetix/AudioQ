You may have heard that Frank Gehry designed this building by crumpling up a piece of paper. It's a good joke, and it isn't true. In an episode of The Simpsons, a cartoon Gehry finds his inspiration in a crumpled letter. Years later he said in a television interview that the joke "has haunted me".

What he really did was slower. He built models by hand, and points on those models were digitised so the shapes could be worked on a computer. In the museum's words, he chose "an advanced software initially conceived for the aerospace industry, CATIA", to carry his concept faithfully into the structure and to help the builders. One of the museum's own educators put it more sharply: at the time, CATIA "had only been used in the French aeronautical industry". The titanium sheets outside were designed with it too.

It mattered, because almost nothing here repeats. Each piece of steel and stone had its own coordinates, its own thickness and its own curve. As the builder puts it, no two joints in the building are the same.

By now you're stepping out among the curves and the glass. Behind those curved walls hides a steel frame, engineered by the firm SOM. SOM's John Zils remembered Gehry's reaction: "If I had known that the structure was going to be this elegant and this beautiful, I would have exposed some of it."

Old skills built it alongside the new software. Climbers on ropes fitted titanium panels on the facade, in a project made, as one architecture magazine put it, at "the turning point between analog and digital practice". The builder's works manager went further. He called it "the first major project in the world built using BIM", building-information modelling. That's his claim.

And far below your feet, the whole thing stands on more than six hundred piles, around fourteen metres long on average.

No crumpled paper, then. Just thousands of pieces, every one of them different.
