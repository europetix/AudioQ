Four hundred thousand. That was the number to beat. The feasibility study said the museum needed 400,000 visitors a year to justify the cost.

In its first full year, 1998, about 1.3 million people came. In its first three years, almost four million.

Its record year so far was 2023. In 2025, almost seven in ten visitors came from abroad.

The money is harder to count. The museum's own studies credit it with more than four billion euros added to the Basque economy in its first twenty years. By the same studies, it sustained some fourteen thousand jobs in 2025.

Not everyone was convinced. The economist Beatriz Plaza recorded that critics "objected to squandering so much public money on something so irrelevant and exclusive". Local critics had a word for it: McGuggenheim. And as it opened, a Basque painter told AP: "It is a symbol of cultural imperialism, not Basque pride."

The architect had his own verdict. Gehry dismissed the whole idea of a "Bilbao effect" in a word we won't repeat here, and blamed journalists for it. He also said: "I apologise for having anything to do with it."

The museum's long-time director, Juan Ignacio Vidarte, put it more carefully. "For a cultural project to be transformative," he said, "it needs to have a number of ingredients." Such a project, he warned, "cannot be isolated". And to anyone who thought Bilbao was simply New York's branch office: "Despite the fact that we share the family name, we are a distinct institution."

As you come down among the people in the Atrium and the Hall, look around you. Every one of them is part of that number. Four hundred thousand was the target. You're one of the many more who came.
