The museum's own route promises "breathtaking views of the Atrium" from this balcony. Judge for yourself.

Above you is what the museum calls "a large skylight in the shape of a metal flower covering the Atrium". The galleries on this floor have skylights of their own, and their daylight, in the museum's words, "reaches the spaces on the floor below as well".

Why all these curves? Gehry's answer went back to his childhood. As the story goes, when he was a boy in Toronto, he watched live carp swimming in his grandmother's bathtub before they became gefilte fish. He was born there, into the Goldberg family. Decades later he said: "The curves were from the fish." He spoke of "a sense of movement with inert materials". And he said that "when a fish moves it's so beautiful".

Fish swam through his work before this building: fish lamps in 1984, and a stainless-steel fish sculpture for the Olympic port in Barcelona in 1992.

He also boasted that this building "does not have a single straight line". "It is not artistry," he said, "it is precision." It was a boast, not a measurement. The museum itself describes some of its galleries as orthogonal, with classical proportions. Right angles, in other words.

So look for the curves. Follow the petals of the flower overhead. Find the walkways slung across the open space, and the glass lifts moving between the floors. Then look down, all the way to the Atrium floor where you stood earlier.

He had won the Pritzker Prize, architecture's highest honour, in 1989.

A boy watching fish in a bathtub, and, a lifetime later, a roof of metal petals over all of this.
