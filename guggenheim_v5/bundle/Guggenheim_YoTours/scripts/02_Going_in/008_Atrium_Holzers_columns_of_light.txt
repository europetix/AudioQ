You may know Jenny Holzer's words already, even if you don't know her name. In 1982 one of them ran on an electronic board in Times Square: "Abuse of power comes as no surprise." Short, sharp one-liners like that are her Truisms. But that is not what these columns are saying.

This is Installation for Bilbao, made for this place in 1997, the year the museum opened. The museum describes it as "a row of nine vertical, double-sided LED signboards, each more than 12 meters tall", which "bisect one of the Gehry building's tall, irregular galleries". The words run in red and blue light, and in three languages: Basque, Spanish and English.

You'll sometimes hear that these are Truisms too. They aren't. The text comes from a piece of hers called Arno. She first wrote it for a project benefiting AIDS research, and then adapted it for an outdoor projection in Florence, in 1996. Its lines are short and personal: "I say your name." "I save your clothes."

Now give it a minute. Pick one line and follow it as it runs along the column. Then wait for it to come round again in another language. If you can, step to one side, and you'll see that every column has two faces, each carrying words.

Here's a thought to take with you. Much of this building's story is about money and spectacle. Yet in one of its galleries, it keeps words first written to help AIDS research. Whether that's a contrast or a balance is yours to decide.

Holzer has also made a second work for the museum, Like Beauty in Flames, which exists only in augmented reality.
