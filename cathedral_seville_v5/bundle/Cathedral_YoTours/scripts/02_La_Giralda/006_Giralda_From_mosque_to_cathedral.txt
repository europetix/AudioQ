In 1401, the canons of Seville made a decision that would take them more than a century to carry out. To understand it, go back to the mosque.

Eight and a half centuries ago, the Almohad caliph Abu Ya'qub Yusuf ordered a great Friday mosque for Seville, his capital in Spain. Its prayer hall went up in about four years. The minaret, the tower you're walking down now, was finished under his son.

In 1248, King Fernando the Third took Seville. The mosque was consecrated as the city's cathedral, adapted for Christian worship, and it served for a century and a half.

Then, in 1401, the canons voted to pull it down and build a new church. Tradition says one canon put it like this: "Let us build a church so great that those who see it finished will think we were mad." The decision itself, as it has come down to us, is more sober. It speaks of a church "so good that there is none its equal."

Building began in earnest some thirty years later. Stone by stone, the Gothic church rose where the prayer hall had stood. It opened in May 1507, a century after that vote.

But the builders didn't erase everything. They kept the minaret and made it their bell tower. They kept the courtyard where worshippers washed before prayer, now the courtyard of orange trees. And they kept its great gateway, the Puerta del Perdón, the Gate of Forgiveness. That's where you'll leave at the very end.

So as you come down these ramps, you're walking inside the part of the mosque that the cathedral never let go.

At the bottom, step just inside the Catedral, out of the way of the tower door. The next stop is at the foot of the Giralda.
