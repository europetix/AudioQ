This tomb was made for another cathedral, on the other side of the Atlantic. And there, it seems, it never held Columbus at all.

This is the Sepulcro de Colón, the tomb of Christopher Columbus. It is the work of the Spanish artist Arturo Mélida. In 1891 he won a competition for a monument in the cathedral of Havana, in Cuba, then still ruled by Spain. That is why it stands so high. It was designed for the crossing of Havana's cathedral, to be seen from all around.

It was finished just in time to be lost. The monument went up in Havana early in 1898. That same year Spain lost Cuba in war. According to an old Havana account, kept by the city historian's office, the new tomb never held his remains. When the old niche was opened, they were taken to the sacristy. In December they were shipped back across the ocean, and they reached Seville early the next year. The monument followed, packed in pieces, and was set up here in 1902.

Four heralds carry the coffin, one for each of the old kingdoms of Spain. Their tunics carry the arms of Castile, León, Aragon and Navarre. Look for the castle and the lion. In Mélida's own description, the two in front, Castile and León, carry trophies of the voyage to the Americas and of the Reconquest. The two behind, Aragon and Navarre, come, as he put it, to share in the mourning if they had no share in the glory.

The door beside you is the Puerta del Príncipe, the Prince's Door, also called the Puerta de San Cristóbal. It was built in modern times by Adolfo Fernández Casanova, in the Gothic style of the rest.
