[warmly] More than eight hundred years ago, nobody walked into the great mosque of Seville with dusty hands and feet. First, you washed. And this is where you did it.

You're in the Patio de los Naranjos, the Courtyard of the Orange Trees. It was the sahn of the Almohad mosque, its open courtyard, where Seville's Muslims made their ablutions before going in to pray.

The prayer hall itself is gone. The Gothic cathedral you have just walked through stands on its site, on almost the same rectangle, which is why the church is so wide. But parts of it survived. One is this courtyard. Another rises above it: the Giralda, which began life as this mosque's minaret, the tower that called the faithful to prayer.

[pause] Look at the arcades. Those pillars carrying pointed horseshoe arches are the mosque's original structure. There were three galleries once. Only two remain. The third was pulled down to make room for the Sagrario, the parish church built against the courtyard between 1618 and 1662.

Now the trees. They stand in a strict grid, and between them runs a network of little channels, drawing geometric shapes in the ground. They still carry water.

[curious] And in the middle, the fountain. It is modern, standing where the mosque's fountain stood. But the Cathedral says its upper bowl is Visigothic, and is the same bowl the Almohads used. If so, the oldest thing in this courtyard is older than the mosque. It's a basin from Christian Spain before the Muslims came, reused by the mosque, and kept by the cathedral.

[quietly] A mosque's courtyard, a cathedral's garden, and at its heart a bowl older than both.

Now find a spot in the Patio de los Naranjos where you can see the very top of the Giralda, and the bronze figure that crowns it: the Giraldillo.
