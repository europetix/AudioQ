[curious] Christopher Columbus never stopped travelling, not even after he died.

He died in Valladolid. His body was moved to a monastery in Seville, the Cartuja. Then it crossed the Atlantic to the cathedral of Santo Domingo, on Hispaniola. When Spain gave up that part of the island, remains were taken to Havana. And when Spain lost Cuba, those remains came back here, to this tomb.

[pause] But in 1877, workmen in Santo Domingo Cathedral found a lead box of bones inscribed with Columbus's name. The Dominican Republic says he is still there. In the words of the director of its Columbus Lighthouse, the remains have never left Dominican territory.

So scientists stepped in. A University of Granada team has studied these bones since the early 2000s. In 2006 it matched them with the DNA of Columbus's brother Diego. In 2024 its leader, José Antonio Lorente, said they are Columbus's, after comparing them with Diego and with Columbus's son Hernando.

[quietly] Here is what can fairly be said. The Granada studies support that the bones in this tomb are at least partly Columbus's own. Only part of a skeleton is here, and the remains in Santo Domingo have never been tested. The Dominican Republic still claims him. And the claims about his origins, made on television in 2024, have not been published scientifically.

[warmly] One Columbus is certainly in this cathedral. His son Hernando is buried here. He collected thousands of books, some of them his father's, and left each one for the use and benefit of all his fellow men. His library survives beside the courtyard where your visit ends, though it isn't open to visitors.

Into the sacristies. Go along this side of the church, away from the great doors at the far end, to the Capilla de los Dolores. Through it is the Sacristía de los Cálices, the chalice sacristy. Look for Goya's tall painting of two saints with a lion at their feet and the Giralda behind them.
