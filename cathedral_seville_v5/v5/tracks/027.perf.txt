[amused] There is a crocodile hanging from the ceiling of Seville Cathedral. People have been trying to explain it for centuries.

Look up in the Nave del Lagarto, the Gallery of the Lizard. Overhead hang a crocodile, the lagarto itself, an elephant's tusk, a rod and a bridle. If you came in by the Puerta del Lagarto, you may already have passed beneath them.

The story starts with something real. Around 1260, envoys of the Mamluk Sultan of Egypt reached Alfonso the Wise in Seville. The chronicle of his reign lists their gifts: an ivory tusk, a giraffe, and a she-ass with one band white and the other dark. [wry] In other words, a zebra.

[conspiratorial] What the chronicle does not mention is a crocodile. Tradition adds one, a Nile crocodile among the gifts. In the seventeenth century, Seville's annalist Ortiz de Zúñiga wrote that the animals died soon after they arrived. The king had their skins stuffed with straw and placed in the cathedral's cloister. The one that lasted longest, he said, was a deformed lizard, or Egyptian crocodile, and it gave this gallery its name.

[pause] The crocodile you see today is a wooden copy. The rod and the bridle have stories told about them too, but none that the records can confirm.

Above this gallery is the library of Hernando Colón, Columbus's son, which holds books that once belonged to his father. It is not open on this visit.

Last stop: the Puerta del Perdón, the tall arched gateway in the middle of the far side of the courtyard, opposite the cathedral.
