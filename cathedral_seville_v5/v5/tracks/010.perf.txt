[curious] Somewhere in these choir stalls is a signature. The King's chair bears the name of its carver, Nufro Sánchez, and the date 1478. That's almost thirty years before the cathedral around it was finished.

The work didn't stop with him. From the next year, Pieter Dancart, the first master of the great altarpiece behind you, carried it on. The result is a hundred and seventeen carved seats in two tiers, in the Gothic-Mudéjar style, where Gothic carving meets the craft traditions of Muslim Spain. They're carved with saints and scenes from the Old and New Testaments. And under the seats, the misericords, little ledges to lean on during long services, show the vices as monstrous figures.

This is the Coro, the choir, a room within the church. It's walled on three sides. Only its front is open, facing the altar, and it's closed by a Renaissance grille made by Fray Francisco de Salamanca, the friar who made much of this cathedral's ironwork.

Inside stands a great lectern of wood and bronze, made in the Renaissance by several sculptors. Its bronze reliefs were cast by Bartolomé Morel, the founder who also cast the figure that turns on top of the Giralda.

[pause] Above the stalls rise the pipes of a double organ, built into the choir stalls. The present organs were finished in 1903, and adapted electronically seventy years later.

[wry] The choir has had its own drama. In 1888 one of the great pillars beside the choir collapsed, and had to be rebuilt.

[warmly] Walls, a grille, its own furniture and its own music, all turned toward the altar.

Now walk along the side of the choir, into the side aisle on the side where you came down from the tower. Follow it toward the far end of the church, to the Capilla de San Antonio. Look for Murillo's huge canvas of a kneeling saint and a cloud of angels.
