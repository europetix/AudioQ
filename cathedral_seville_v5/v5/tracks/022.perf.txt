[curious] When the canons wanted a crowning image for this room, they did not have to look far. The painter they chose was already all over their cathedral.

His name was Bartolomé Esteban Murillo. The chapter commissioned him in 1667, and he worked on this room into the following year. Up in the vault he set eight saints of Seville, each on a round canvas. And above them, in a carved frame, presides the Immaculate Conception: the Virgin Mary, conceived without sin.

[pause] Look up, follow the ring of saints around the oval, then rise to her. The canons sat at floor level, among the Virtues and the Latin mottoes. Above their heads were the saints of their own city. Above the saints, the Virgin. Seville's own tourist office counts her among the most beautiful Immaculates Murillo ever painted.

[warmly] Murillo is the painter who keeps finding you in this cathedral. Its own list of paintings names him again and again, from 1655 to 1674. His San Isidoro and San Leandro hang in the Sacristía Mayor, the great sacristy. His Saint Anthony, the canvas a thief once cut the saint out of, is in the Capilla de San Antonio. More of his work is in the Capilla de Santiago, the chapel of Saint James, and at the altar of the Guardian Angel.

He was an admirer, too. Remember the story from the Sacristía Mayor: Murillo before Campaña's Descent from the Cross, waiting for them to finish taking Our Lord down. Here is what he could do himself, given the canons' ceiling.

[quietly] Below, the canons argued and voted. Above them, for more than three and a half centuries, Murillo's Virgin has had the last word.

Now back into the church and on to its far end, behind the Capilla Mayor: the Capilla Real, the Royal Chapel, behind its great iron grille.
