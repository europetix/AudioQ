[amused] When this painting arrived, Seville's own painters were furious. They felt the commission should have been theirs, and the cathedral had given it to Goya.

This is the Sacristía de los Cálices, the chalice sacristy, and the tall canvas is Francisco de Goya's Santas Justa y Rufina, painted in 1817. The commission came through his friend Ceán Bermúdez, a scholar of art who chose the subject and watched every step. He asked for the saints life-size, in decorous postures that would not distract the priest at the altar. Goya was paid 28,000 reales and delivered it early the next year. Rival poems for and against it went round the city.

[curious] Tradition makes Justa and Rufina sisters, and potters. That's why they hold clay pots. On the ground lies a broken pagan statue. Their story says they smashed an image of a goddess, and were martyred for it. The lion licking Rufina's feet is from the same story. Sent into the arena, she was spared by the beast.

[pause] Now look behind them. That tower in the background is the Giralda, where your visit began. It stands for their city.

[warmly] Sevillians see another reason for it. Tradition here says these two saints held the tower up during the earthquake of 1504, so that it didn't fall. They have been its protectors ever since, in paint and in glass.

There's a short Extra in the Sala del Pabellón, the exhibition room. The next main stop is the Sacristía Mayor, the great domed sacristy: look up at a dome carved in rings of figures.
