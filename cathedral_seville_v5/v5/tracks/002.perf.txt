[curious] Eight centuries ago, this tower ended in four golden balls. They shone so brightly, it was said, that you could see them a day's march from the city.

The tower began as a minaret. In 1184 the Almohad caliph Abu Ya'qub Yusuf ordered one for his new mosque in Seville. He did not live to see it rise. His son, al-Mansur, carried the work on. The architect who laid it out was Ahmad ibn Baso. A second master, Ali al-Ghumari, finished it in brick.

[pause] In March 1198 the great gilded balls, the yamur, were hoisted to the very top. A chronicler of the time, Ibn Sahib al-Salat, wrote that travellers saw the minaret "as if suspended among the stars." For a century and a half the balls caught the sun. Then an earthquake in 1356 brought them crashing down.

By then Seville was a Christian city, and the minaret had become the cathedral's bell tower. In the sixteenth century a Renaissance belfry was built on top. That's where you're heading. From the ground to the tip of its weathervane, the tower rises close to a hundred metres.

As you climb, notice what's under your feet. These aren't stairs but broad, gently sloping ramps. They wind between the outer wall and an inner core, like one tower built around another.

[lightly] The Giralda has famous sisters, the great Almohad minarets of Marrakesh and Rabat. And it has children. Towers modelled on it were built across Spain and the Americas.

Keep climbing, and keep to the right. The next stop plays on the ramps themselves, a few turns further up the Giralda.
