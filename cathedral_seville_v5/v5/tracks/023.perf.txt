[reverent] Behind this grille lies the king who turned the mosque into a cathedral. Fernando the Third took Seville in 1248. He was later made a saint, and Seville still calls him San Fernando.

This is the Capilla Real, the Royal Chapel. It fills the head of the church, behind the Capilla Mayor. It is kept for worship and prayer only, so visitors don't go in. Look through the grille, quietly, from here.

Martín de Gainza designed it in the middle of the sixteenth century. He died with everything finished but the dome, and Hernán Ruiz the Younger closed it some years later.

Before the altar is the urn of San Fernando, made by the silversmith Juan Laureano de Pina after the king was declared a saint. It holds his remains. Above the altar, under a silver canopy, is the Virgen de los Reyes, Our Lady of the Kings, a Gothic figure of the thirteenth century. The Cathedral calls her of French origin. [pause] Tradition says she was a gift from the French king, Saint Louis. That is a story, not a record.

[warmly] He is not alone. In niches in the side walls lie his son, Alfonso the Wise, and Alfonso's mother, Beatriz of Swabia. Beneath the floor, in a crypt that no visitor sees, rest Pedro the First and María de Padilla. A royal family, gathered round the conqueror.

Now look at the grille itself. It was put up in 1771, paid for by King Charles the Third. On top, the sculptor Jerónimo Roldán shows San Fernando receiving the keys of Seville.

The chapel reopened in February 2026 after several months of restoration.

[quietly] The city he took is still outside these walls. And up on the grille, he is still being handed its keys.

Now to the courtyard. Go to the arm of the transept on the side where you came down from the tower, to the Puerta de la Concepción, the door under a tall stained-glass window of the Ascension.
