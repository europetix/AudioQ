[curious] In July 1927 Miquel Utrillo, the engineer and painter leading the project, wrote to Lluís Plandiura, one of the Exhibition's leaders. He wrote, in Spanish, that the journey was "so indispensable that a good part of the work done this month will, logically, be changed by the living things that the architects and our friend Nogués will see" as they travelled round Spain.

That September four men set off in one car, a Hispano-Suiza: Utrillo, the painter Xavier Nogués, and two architects, Francesc Folguera and Ramon Reventós. Thirty-one days, at a planned two hundred kilometres a day. They passed through almost seven hundred towns, and stopped to study a hundred and thirty-seven. Two more trips followed. Across all three, they passed through something like sixteen hundred places.

They had a method. In small towns Nogués sketched the outline while the two architects waited with the camera for a good photograph. In the important towns they split into two teams, an architect and a painter in each, and then [amused] they voted on every find, marks out of ten. Colours were matched against scales of tone that Nogués had drawn up.

It wasn't all smooth. On the road to Zaragoza the car broke down, and Utrillo recorded the moment in a caricature. A letter from Borja says, in Catalan, "we have the car in Tudela being repaired, because the clutch burned out".

[pause] Did the trip matter? Of the models in the first plan, only nine survived into the final village, and only three of them unchanged.

[quietly] Then the record of the journey went into a drawer. More than a hundred photographs and sketches, in five travel notebooks, stayed in Reventós's desk for about ninety years. The researchers who found them wrote: "These notebooks, which have remained hidden until today, constitute unpublished material." The city's photo archive also holds about a thousand negatives from that year.

Look up the street in front of you: this is what all that voting produced.

Walk on up Calle Caballeros, slowly, and play the next stop as you go. Look at the doorways and the coats of arms above them: Calle Caballeros · Coats of arms.
