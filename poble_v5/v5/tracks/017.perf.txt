[curious] The wall paintings inside this monastery look medieval. The man who painted them lived until 1961.

His name was Rafael Padilla, a painter born in Málaga. The city of Barcelona puts it plainly: "The frescoes on the walls of the monastery are the work of Malaga-born painter Rafael Padilla." So whatever you may read about medieval frescoes up here, they are twentieth-century work.

This is the Monastery of Sant Miquel, in Catalan the Monestir de Sant Miquel. It stands on the highest point of the village. The Catalan encyclopedia calls it "a little apart", a building that "condenses elements of various Catalan Romanesque churches". It's a monastery in pieces.

[pause] Count the pieces, as the city does. "The portal is from Santa Maria de Porqueres, in Pla de l'Estany." The floor is inspired by Sant Sebastià de Montmajor, at Caldes de Montbui. The cloister is modelled on the Benedictine monastery of Sant Benet de Bages. And "the bell tower is a replica of the one in Sant Genís de Taradell." Four places, and a painter.

Now look at the portal in front of you, the arched doorway that stands in for Porqueres. Then find the square bell tower, modelled on Taradell's.

It has had its own drama, too: a partial collapse in 1993, and a reopening four years later. In 2013 its interior opened to day visitors for the first time.

[wry] One more correction, and this one comes from the Poble itself. Its own events page says the monastery was "designed by the modernist architect Puig i Cadafalch". Every source on the village's authorship credits the same four men instead, among them the architects Folguera and Reventós.

[warmly] The oldest-looking building in the village, and one of the most borrowed.

Now take the path down from the monastery toward the big house at number 69. Go on between 69 and the Vidre art gallery, a former glassblowing workshop, into the Andalusian quarter. Stop in the little Plaza de la Hermandad, where the walls turn white: Plaza de la Hermandad · Left out, then added for the King.
