While the Exhibition lasted, this was a village of fiestas, with folk dances, pageants, even riding contests. What came after is told less often. You can keep walking while you listen, across toward the Plaza de la Iglesia.

[quietly] First, a library. The city's art museum records that in 1934 its library opened "in the Casa de la Junta de Museos in the Poble Espanyol". When the war came, it was sent to Olot with the museum's art and documents. When the war was over, it came back to its rooms here.

Then the war itself. The Poble's own history calls it "the darkest episode in its history", and says: "the brutal war saw its conversion into a prisoner internment camp." [pause] That is the Poble's account, in its own words. The sources we have read don't say who held the prisoners, how many, or when. A list of the Francoist camps in Catalonia, published by the newspaper Ara, counts sixteen. The Poble is not among them.

"In the aftermath of the war," the same history goes on, "the Poble was in danger of falling into ruin."

In 1942 the empty houses found a use, as a museum of popular industries and arts, the Museu d'Indústries i Arts Populars. Today's Museu Etnològic says it was "dedicated to Catalan and Iberian Peninsula culture", with collections on "trades, traditions and everyday life", and an ethnological section designed by Ramon Violant i Simorra. The Poble's own history adds another line: "Franco's regime used the museum to disseminate violently anti-Catalanist messages." Two institutions, two descriptions. We give you both.

From the late fifties to the early seventies, the city tried to relaunch the village and the hill of Montjuïc. But, the Poble's history admits, "the venue still failed to function." The folk museum stayed, in one form or another, until the end of the century.

[pause] A village built for a fiesta: a library, then prisoners, then a folk museum.
