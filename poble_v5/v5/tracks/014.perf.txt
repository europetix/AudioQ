[curious] The four men who made this village were Catalans. And in 1927 one of them wrote that Catalonia was still to do.

The painter Xavier Nogués was writing to Lluís Plandiura, one of the Exhibition's leaders. In Spanish, he said: "This trip will have been of great benefit to the poble, and now only Catalonia remains to be done." [pause] Home, it seems, could wait. A second, shorter trip followed that November: twelve days, mainly through Catalonia and Aragon.

This square is part of the result. Its name, Plaça de la Font, means Fountain Square. The Catalan encyclopedia lists a fountain from Prades, in the Baix Camp, among the village's models, without saying where it stands. The same entry lists the Catalan towns imitated here, from Besalú and Rupit to Montblanc and Tàrrega, "and others".

Around this square, the Poble's own list includes Cornudella, a house from La Garriga, the Casa dels Balbs, and the house of the Marquès de la Floresta from Tàrrega.

One surprise. The islands were left out of the first plans. Yet the Poble's list has a house from Palma de Mallorca, the Casa Son Berga. And the Poble describes the big house at number 69, above this square, as a "Balearic Island-style stately home".

The Poble's own description of this part puts it simply, in Spanish: "In the Mediterranean area you'll get to know the architecture of Catalonia and the Balearics." On the official plan it's all purple. And the purple holds Valencian towns too: Morella and Catí elsewhere in the village, and La Jana, which the list places round this square.

[warmly] Now look at the colours of the fronts around you. On the trips, colours were matched against scales of tone kept by Nogués, the painter. So there's a painter's eye in every wall of this square.

Catalonia came late, and it climbs highest. The monastery at the top of the hill is Catalan Romanesque.
