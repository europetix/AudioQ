"Barcelona at your feet," says the Poble's own page, and it names three landmarks: the Sagrada Família, the Mapfre towers and Tibidabo. See how many you can find.

In 1929 this whole hill was a world's fair. The International Exhibition's theme was "Industry, Art and Sport", and almost six million visitors came. It opened on the nineteenth of May. The village opened the next day. A 2010 study by Sirés for the Barcelona polytechnic found that the village took up only about eight per cent of the Exhibition's built area. It cost less than half as much as the works on the Palau Nacional.

It was also born under a dictatorship, General Primo de Rivera's. The Catalan encyclopedia says the Exhibition was "organised by the Dictatorship's city council with the help of the state", and that it left the city seriously in debt.

There are two ways to read it, and both are argued seriously. The historian Jordana Mendelson, as researchers quote her in Spanish, sees Primo de Rivera "building in Barcelona an architectural representation of a unified Spain". Sirés says, in Catalan, that the project was "accepted and ideologically swallowed" by the dictatorship. The idea of "the indissoluble unity of the Spanish nation" found favour with the regime, and there were "strong pressures from the central government".

The same study gives the other side its full weight. The village's four makers, it points out, were Catalanists, and Utrillo's idea, it argues, came before the dictatorship. That, it says, "substantially complicates the simple, reductionist interpretation" that the Poble Espanyol was the dictatorship's work.

Born under one dictatorship, made by Catalanists, and later used by another regime, Franco's. All three are true.

On the Essential and full tours, there's a short Extra in the Sculpture Garden, below the viewpoint, where the giant slides are. The next main stop is the monastery. A step-free path climbs to it round the houses at the top of Plaça de la Font.
