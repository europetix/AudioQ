On a journey in 1928, passing the real tower at Utebo again, one of them wrote a note, in Catalan: "We like the Exhibition's campanile better than Utebo's." A campanile is a bell tower. The researchers read it as meaning their own design, the one in front of you. If they're right, the travellers liked their version more than the original.

The original is the Mudéjar bell tower of the church of Nuestra Señora de la Asunción at Utebo, near Zaragoza. Its own inscription, in glazed ceramic, says it was finished in 1544, by the master builder Alonso de Leznes. It's known as "the bell tower of the mirrors", because of "the reflections and glints that the sun produces on the ceramics that cover it".

Why is an Aragonese tower here at all? Because of the first trip. The famous town on the travellers' list, Santillana del Mar, let them down. Aragon surprised them, above all the tower of Utebo. So they swapped the Baroque models they had planned for Mudéjar ones, in brick and glazed ceramic, like Utebo's tower.

The church beside the tower changed with it. The first design was Baroque, fusing churches from Amorebieta and Caldes de Montbui. What was built instead is three Aragonese buildings made into one church: the front of the Carmelite church of Alcañiz, this tower, and a façade from Torralba de Ribota.

And the tower itself is a cast. It's slightly smaller than the real one. The builders made a whole plaster mould for each of its four faces, and assembled them on site, as if it were a prefabricated piece.

Now look up at it. The real tower's ceramics flash in the sun. If the sun is out, wait a moment: does this one catch the light too? That's the effect that gave the original its nickname.
