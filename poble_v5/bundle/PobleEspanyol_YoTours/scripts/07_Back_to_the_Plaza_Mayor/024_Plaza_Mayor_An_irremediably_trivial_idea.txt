In 1961 an architecture journal quoted a colleague on this village, in Spanish: "Here is an irremediably trivial idea, expressed, miraculously, with such acute sensitivity."

It hadn't always been so grudging. The journal recalled how three Barcelona critics, Just Cabot, Domènec Carles and Carles Soldevila, "admiringly asked" for the village to be kept. They wrote in a fine issue of the Catalan magazine D'Ací i d'Allà. The Poble's own history agrees: it was "such a success" that it was not demolished.

Then fashion turned. In the years when architects were inventing zoning and vertical green cities, the journal wrote, the village could only be seen as "a merry-go-round for tourism". "As nicely decorated as you like by four sensitive artists," it went on, "but only, and simply, a merry-go-round." It "very soon fell into general disregard."

The 1961 verdict cuts both ways. The idea of a Spanish village at a world's fair, it said, is "neither very original nor very good", even "a rather crazy and extraordinarily dangerous idea". And yet it found these streets "perfectly, deliciously habitable", just as it found so many new developments "scandalously uninhabitable". More than half a million people had come the year before. Later critics studied it as a lesson in what one called "psychological habitability".

In 1986, with democracy back, the city set out to make it, in the Poble's words, "a centre for cultural, leisure and commercial activities". Under the mayor, Pasqual Maragall, its running went to private managers on a thirty-year concession. A night bar opened in the gate you came in by. But the losses kept coming, and a decade on, the consortium running it went into suspension of payments. Then "a group of businessmen" took over its management, and the Poble's history says it began to recover.

In 2017, by the city's figures, about 1.3 million came: the fifth most-visited place of interest in Barcelona.

Trivial idea or deliciously habitable? You're sitting in the evidence. You decide.

For the last stop, walk to the corner of the square by the arcade at house 26, with Plaza Castellana and the gate just beyond: Plaza Mayor · The village that stayed.
