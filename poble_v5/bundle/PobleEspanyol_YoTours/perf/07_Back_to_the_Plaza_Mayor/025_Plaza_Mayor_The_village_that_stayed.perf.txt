[warmly] "You could put multimedia attractions anywhere," the Poble's director general, Anton Vidal, said in 2018, "but you cannot build a town like this."

This town was built as a temporary set, meant to come down when the Exhibition was over. It never did. Today it is protected heritage in its own right, listed as a Bé Cultural d'Interès Local, a cultural asset of local interest.

Why did it last? The researchers who found the team's notebooks give an answer, in Spanish. The journeys, they write, stressed the popular character of the project. Then they go further. Without the journey, the Poble Espanyol would not just be very different: "it would probably no longer exist. It would have disappeared long ago."

[pause] Think back over what you've seen. A gate modelled on the walls of Ávila. A brick tower cast in plaster moulds. An outside stair with seven steps, where Rupit's has fourteen. A Romanesque monastery that is several places in one. A quarter the historians credit to a king who asked where Andalusia was. And this square, laid out like Riaza's, its fronts gathered from across the country. Interpretation, not copies.

In May 2029 the village turns a hundred.

The Exhibition's centenary is coming too, and Montjuïc is being remodelled, with work timed toward it.

Before you go, if you have time. The Poble shows photographs of the building site in the town hall. The glassblower works at house 81, on Carrer Mercaders. Fiesta, the ten-minute show, is in the brick tower on Plaza Aragonesa, the Poble says. And the Fran Daurel Museum is on the Plaza de la Iglesia.

[quietly] One last borrowing, as you go. The historians write that, on this side of the square, you come in through the porticoes of Sangüesa's town hall, from Navarre. If this arcade is the one they mean, even the way in was borrowed.

[warmly] The way out is the Ávila gate you came in by: through the arcade into Plaza Castellana, and out between its two towers.

Thank you for walking the Poble Espanyol with Yo Tours. Goodbye.
