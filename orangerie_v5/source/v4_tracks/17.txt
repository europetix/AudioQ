TITLE: Henri Rousseau — The Wedding & Father Junier's Cart
KIND: ANCHOR · 4:00
WHERE: Walk into the Rousseau room. Look for The Wedding first — it's a vertical group portrait, more than five feet tall.
WHAT: Two paintings: a wedding party arranged like a photograph, with a black dog in front. And a horse-drawn cart carrying a family, a small black dog, and a tiny dog in front of the horse.
LISTEN: Why Rousseau didn't think of himself as 'naïve' — and what he was actually trying to do.
---
Now we meet Henri Rousseau. The Walter-Guillaume collection has nine Rousseau paintings — Paul Guillaume acquired around fifty of them across his career, and Domenica kept the most important nine. Apollinaire pointed Paul toward Rousseau when Paul was twenty-three years old. The two of them — the dealer and the poet — built up Rousseau's reputation almost together, in the years just after the painter's death in 1910. Look at The Wedding first. La Noce. Painted around 1905. Oil on canvas, about a hundred and sixty-three centimeters tall — over five feet. The vertical orientation matters. What you see: a wedding party arranged in front of a wall of trees. The bride is in the center, in white, almost ghostly. Around her, the groom and the family members — each face individualized, each person painted with patient attention. At the front, sitting upright and looking directly out at you, is a black dog. The trees behind them recede sharply because each tree gets visibly smaller as it moves up the picture — like a perspective diagram, but more emphatic than any real photograph would render it. Here's what Rousseau is doing, and why he was misunderstood for most of his life. The figures in this painting are flat. They're silhouettes. They don't model with light and shadow the way an academic painter would. They're presented in a single plane, like figures cut out of paper. And critics in the eighteen-nineties and early nineteen-hundreds laughed at this. They called him primitive. They called him naïve. They thought he couldn't actually paint. But Rousseau saw himself as a realist. He used that exact word. He said he was painting reality as he saw it. He worked from photographs and postcards — and what he was doing, in part, was treating the painting like a photograph from a studio, where people stand in a row, against a backdrop, with the same flatness photographic figures have. He chose that flatness on purpose. He wasn't failing at depth — he was rejecting it. Now turn to the second painting. La Carriole du père Junier. Father Junier's Cart. Painted in 1908. Smaller and horizontal this time — ninety-seven by a hundred and twenty-nine centimeters.

What you see: a horse-drawn cart with a family in it. The man holding the reins is Claude Junier, a vegetable seller and Rousseau's neighbor. With him are his wife Anna, their niece Léa, a young woman, and — sitting in the cart — Rousseau himself. There's a large black dog at the side. A tiny dog trotting in front of the horse. The horse herself stands on the very tips of her hooves, with shadows beneath, as if she's almost floating off the ground. There's a story about how this painting came to be. Junier and Rousseau were friends. Rousseau owed Junier money. Junier had just bought a horse he was proud of. They agreed that Rousseau would paint the family in the new cart in lieu of payment. Rousseau worked from a photograph. But the photograph didn't have a giant black dog. The photograph didn't have a miniature dog trotting in front of the horse. The photograph probably didn't show the horse standing on its tiptoes. Rousseau changed all of these things on purpose. When the painter Max Weber visited him and pointed out that the big black dog was way too large in scale, Rousseau replied that the painting itself demanded the dog be that size. That's the line that captures Rousseau. The painting demands its own size. He chose every proportion. Nothing is an accident. He was a working-class self-taught painter from outside the art establishment, and what looks like incompetence is, every time, a decision. We'll look at his jungles next — they're in this same room.
