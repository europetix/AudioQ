TITLE: Soutine — The Pastry Cook, Page Boy, Carcass of Beef
KIND: HEAVY · 4:30
WHERE: Walk into the Soutine room. Twenty-two paintings — the largest single-artist holding in the collection after Renoir and Derain.
WHAT: Crooked faces. Twisted bodies. Service workers in their uniforms — pastry cooks, bellboys. And one large painting of a slaughtered animal.
LISTEN: Why Soutine painted the world this way, and what it cost him.
---
Twenty-two Soutines in this collection. Paul Guillaume bought them when nobody else wanted them. Domenica kept them all. Chaïm Soutine was born in 1893 in a small Jewish village in what is now Belarus. The tenth of eleven children. His family was extremely poor. They were religiously observant, which meant his decision to study drawing as a child was scandalous — painting living things was forbidden under a strict reading of Jewish law. He was beaten for it. He left home as a teenager. He came to Paris in 1913, at age twenty, and lived for years in extreme poverty in the artists' commune called La Ruche — the Beehive — where he painted in an unheated studio that smelled, by every visitor's account, terrible. He hung carcasses in there to paint from. He didn't bathe. He couldn't afford to. Paul Guillaume met him through the dealer Léopold Zborowski around 1919. Guillaume bought Soutine's paintings in bulk. He once acquired more than fifty Soutines in a single visit from a collector named Albert Barnes, who had bought them all at once from Soutine's previous dealer. Look at the paintings now. Find The Little Pastry Cook — Le Petit Pâtissier. A young man in white uniform, with a crooked apron, leaning slightly. His ear is too red. His face is unsettled. The whole figure twists slightly to one side as if he can't quite stand straight. Find the Page Boy — the bellboy. A red uniform. A small skewed body. The face anxious or angry, depending on how you read it. Now find Carcass of Beef — Le Bœuf écorché. This is the most disturbing painting in this collection. A side of beef hangs in a slaughterhouse. The flesh is rendered in violent reds and yellows. The painting throbs. Soutine made several paintings of carcasses. He had a real carcass hauled to his studio so he could paint it. As the days went by it began to rot. The smell drove his neighbors at La Ruche to call the police. They came to evict him. He refused to part with the carcass — he was still working on it. His friend, the sculptor Chana Orloff, talked the police down. Soutine continued painting. He poured fresh blood on the carcass periodically to keep the color right. This is the level he worked at. He was not painting representations of subjects. He was painting the actual presence of decay, of meat, of bodies in distress. The pastry cooks and bellboys and grooms in the other

Soutines you see in this room — they're being painted with the same intensity. Soutine couldn't paint anything as if it were stable. The world, to him, was always quietly tilting. In June 1940, Germany invaded France. Soutine was Jewish. He was hiding in the French countryside, moving from village to village. By 1943, he had developed a perforated ulcer. He was operated on in Paris — an urgent, dangerous operation that required him to leave hiding. He died of the surgery on August ninth, 1943, at age forty-nine. The Gestapo had been searching for him. Look around this room one more time. These twenty-two paintings represent the work of a man who couldn't afford to eat, who painted in conditions that horrified his neighbors, and who was hunted in his last years. The paint itself is built up thick. The figures lean and twist. The colors are unstable. Soutine couldn't make the world hold still — and so he painted it the way it felt to him. Paul Guillaume saw what Soutine was doing before almost anyone else. He bought his work for almost nothing. Today these paintings are among the most important holdings of Soutine's work anywhere in the world. Three more downstairs artists, more briefly, in the next track.
