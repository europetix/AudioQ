TITLE: Derain, Laurencin & Utrillo — The Quieter Voices
KIND: STANDARD · 3:00
WHERE: Walk through the rooms holding the André Derain paintings, the Marie Laurencin works, and the Maurice Utrillo townscapes.
WHAT: Three different artists, three different moods. Derain's classical post-Fauve work. Laurencin's pastel female groups. Utrillo's Montmartre streets.
LISTEN: Three more painters Paul Guillaume believed in, briefly summarized — because every visitor will at least pass through these rooms.
---
We're heading toward the staircase back up. But before we go, three more artists you'll walk past. First, André Derain. The Walter-Guillaume collection has thirty-one Derain paintings — more than any other artist in this museum except for the Soutines and Renoirs. That's because Paul Guillaume was personally close to Derain, who lived nearby and was a regular visitor at the gallery. Derain had been one of the original Fauves — the wild, color-saturated movement that scandalized Paris in 1905. By the time Paul Guillaume started collecting him, Derain had moved on. He had pulled back into something quieter, more classical. The Derains in this collection are mostly from the 1920s and 1930s. Find Le Joueur de cornemuse — The Bagpipe Player — or Arlequin et Pierrot — Harlequin and Pierrot. The colors are muted now. The figures stand still. The mood is melancholy, almost theatrical, like figures from a paused commedia dell'arte. Derain, in his later years, was looking at Caravaggio, at Italian primitives, at the deep European past. The wildness of his Fauve years had cooled. Some critics consider the late Derain a step backward. Others find a sustained dignity in it. Now Marie Laurencin. The collection has seven of her paintings. Laurencin was one of the few women in the Paris avant-garde of the early twentieth century to make a living from her painting. She had been Apollinaire's lover for years. She kept her own studio. She painted, almost exclusively, women — in soft pastel pinks, pale blues, dove greys. Groups of women. Mothers and daughters. Friends. The faces are simplified, the bodies elongated, the palette deliberately gentle. For decades Laurencin was condescended to. Critics called her work decorative, feminine, lightweight. In recent years, that consensus has been revisited. Laurencin made a specific deliberate choice to paint women, in a register of softness, without irony and without male commentary. It's a kind of feminism by aesthetic rather than by argument. Look at her group portraits in this room. They're not sentimental — they're patient. Finally, Maurice Utrillo. Ten paintings. Utrillo painted streets — particularly the streets of Montmartre, the hilltop neighborhood in Paris where he was born and lived. White walls. Black shutters. Cobblestone slopes. The neighborhood as it looked in the 1910s and 1920s, captured by a man who knew every corner of it. Utrillo's life was difficult. He was the son of the painter Suzanne Valadon, who had been Renoir's and Toulouse-Lautrec's model before becoming a painter herself. He started drinking heavily in his teens. He

spent stretches of his adult life institutionalized. His paintings of Montmartre were initially commercial — he made them, in part, to fund his alcohol. They became, over time, the most accurate visual record of Montmartre as a neighborhood. When the streets were later widened, when the cafés closed, when the working-class character of Montmartre disappeared into tourism, Utrillo's paintings became the closest thing to memory. Walk through these three artists' rooms slowly. They're not the headline works of this collection, but they're the texture. They're who Paul Guillaume believed in. They're who Domenica kept. Now — let's go back upstairs to the Water Lilies. The staircase or elevator is just ahead.
