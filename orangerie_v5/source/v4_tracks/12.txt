TITLE: Giverny, the War, the Cataracts
KIND: HEAVY · 4:30
WHERE: Find a bench in the second oval room, or stand quietly near a corner of the corridor.
WHAT: Whichever Nymphéas panel is closest to where you've sat down. Look at it while we talk.
LISTEN: What Monet was carrying when he made these paintings — his wife, his son, the war forty miles away, and his own failing eyes.
---
Sit down if you can. I want to talk about who made these paintings, and how. Because the rooms you're sitting in are beautiful, and the paintings are beautiful, and it's easy to forget that they came out of one of the hardest stretches of an old man's life. Claude Monet was born in 1840. He moved to Giverny — the village in Normandy where he built the pond and the gardens these paintings come from — in 1883. He was forty-three. He spent the next forty-three years there, until he died in December 1926. The Water Lilies — these specific compositions, the ones around you — were made mostly between 1914 and 1926. Twelve years. The last twelve years of his life. Which means I have to tell you about three things that were happening while he painted them. The first thing is his wife. Alice Monet, his second wife, died in May 1911. She was sixty-six. Monet had loved her for thirty years. After her death, he stopped painting almost entirely for nearly two years. The letters he wrote in that period are heartbreaking. He said he couldn't see anything. He said the world had gone flat. When he finally started working again, he started on these lilies. The second thing is the war. The First World War began in August 1914. Giverny is about forty miles from the front line at its closest. Monet could hear artillery from his garden. His older son Jean was already ill — he died in February 1914, six months before the war began, at the age of forty-six. The younger son Michel went to fight. Monet's stepson Jean-Pierre also went. The village around Giverny emptied of young men. For four years, while Monet was painting these enormous serene panels of his pond, France was being torn apart at the other end of the same train line. And here's the thing. The Water Lilies are not about that war. There are no soldiers in them. No trenches. No tanks. They are paintings of a private garden, of a private pond, of one man's attempt to keep painting water lilies while the world was on fire. And that fact — the fact that he kept painting them anyway — is what made them, in his mind, a memorial. The day after the Armistice, in November 1918, Monet wrote to Clemenceau and said he was ready to donate the panels to France. He called them — and this is his phrasing — "a monument to peace."

These paintings exist because of the war. Not in spite of it. The third thing is his eyes. Around 1908, Monet started noticing his vision changing. By 1912, he was diagnosed with cataracts in both eyes. Cataracts shift color toward yellow and red — they pull the blue out of the world. By the early nineteen-twenties, Monet was nearly blind. He could only see by holding his face inches from the canvas. He had trouble distinguishing colors at all. He had his right eye operated on in January 1923, at age eighty-two. The surgery was difficult. Afterward, some of the canvases he'd painted in the previous years looked, to his recovered eye, too red. He destroyed several. He repainted others. So everything you're seeing in these rooms was made by an old man who had lost his wife, was hearing artillery from his garden, and was nearly blind for the last five years of the work. He didn't stop. He kept painting. He died on December fifth, 1926, with the panels finally finished and waiting to be installed. The Orangerie opened with them in May 1927. Five months after his death. He never saw them in this room. Take a breath. We'll go downstairs in a minute.
