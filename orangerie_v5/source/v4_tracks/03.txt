TITLE: From Citrus Trees to Monet
KIND: ANCHOR · 4:00
WHERE: Inside the entrance lobby, near the introductory wall panels.
WHAT: A small, calm vestibule. The Water Lilies rooms are just ahead. The Walter-Guillaume collection is downstairs.
LISTEN: A hundred and twenty years of small decisions — a war, a friendship, a contract signed two days after the Armistice.
---
Let's walk through how this place actually became what it is. There are a few dates worth holding in your head. Eighteen-fifty-two: built as an orangery, as we just talked about. For the next sixty years, it lived a quiet life — winter shelter for trees, summer venue for the occasional concert, dog show, or art exhibition. Nothing remarkable. The Empire fell in 1870. The Tuileries Palace next door burned in 1871. The Orangerie survived. The state took it over in 1921 and gave it to the Under-Secretariat of State for Fine Arts, with the idea of using it as exhibition space for living artists. And then comes Claude Monet. Monet was eighty-one years old in 1921, living at Giverny in the Normandy countryside. For almost twenty years, he had been painting his pond — water lilies, willows, the surface of water, the sky reflected in it — over and over again. After the outbreak of the First World War, those paintings had grown enormous. He was building rooms in his garden specifically to paint in: a third studio with skylights, big enough to hold canvases nearly seven feet tall and, in some cases, more than fifty feet long. On November twelfth, 1918 — the day after the Armistice that ended the war — Monet wrote a letter to Clemenceau. He said: "I'm about to finish two decorative panels which I want to sign on the day of victory, and ask you to offer to the State." He wanted to give them as a monument to peace. That offer eventually grew. Monet ended up committing not two panels but eight compositions — twenty-two canvas sections in all, joined together so the seams disappear. The donation contract was signed in 1922. And he and the architect Camille Lefèvre worked out the design together — two oval rooms, side by side, the panels mounted directly onto the walls. The floor plan, from above, looks like the symbol for infinity. Two ovals, meeting at a single point. Now — Monet didn't actually want to give them up. He kept working on them until the very end of his life, refusing to part with them. He died on December fifth, 1926, at Giverny. The panels were finally installed at the Orangerie a few months later, and the museum opened to the public in May 1927. The Walter-Guillaume collection came much later — purchased by the state in 1959 and 1963, displayed permanently from 1984. That collection lives downstairs. The Water Lilies live up here, on the ground floor.

What this means in practice: you're about to walk into a space that was designed by a painter, for paintings he didn't want to give away, in a building originally meant for orange trees, lit by the same Paris sky that lit his pond at Giverny. Everything you're about to see has a reason it's where it is. The first oval room is just through that door.
