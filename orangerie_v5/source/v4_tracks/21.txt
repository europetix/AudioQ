TITLE: The Embrace & Large Bather
KIND: STANDARD · 3:00
WHERE: In the Picasso room, find the larger figurative works from the 1920s — heavy bodies, classical poses.
WHAT: Two figures embracing. A massive seated bather. Bodies that look like they were carved out of stone.
LISTEN: What 'return to order' meant after the First World War, and why Picasso reached for ancient art when modernism was at its peak.
---
Find The Embrace — L'Étreinte — in this room. Or Large Bather — Grande Baigneuse — if the rehang has it more prominent. These are the two paintings I want you to stand in front of in this room. Look first at The Embrace. Two figures clasped together. The bodies are heavy, monumental, almost geometric. Arms thick as columns. The flesh isn't soft — it's modeled like sculpture, with smooth volumes and almost no surface detail. The faces are simplified into mask-like forms. This is Picasso in 1922 or 1923, after Cubism, after the First World War, deliberately reaching backward — toward Roman frescoes, toward Greek sculpture, toward what painters across Europe were calling rappel à l'ordre. Return to order. What does that mean? The First World War had just ended. Twenty million dead. The certainties of the European nineteenth century had collapsed. And in the years immediately after the war, painters who had been the most radical experimentalists — Picasso, Derain, Matisse — pulled back. They returned to clearly recognizable bodies. They referenced classical antiquity. They painted in ways their grandfathers would have understood. Why? There's no single answer. Some scholars say it was a response to trauma — the world needed steadiness, not fracture, after four years of unimaginable destruction. Some say it was a cultural retrenchment, a defensive move against the radical left. Some say Picasso himself just wanted to prove he could draw a body. He'd been accused, by the Cubist years, of not being able to. He responded by drawing some of the most academically perfect bodies anyone had drawn in fifty years. Now look at Large Bather. This is the painting where the classicism gets most explicit. A massive woman, seated, naked, monumental. She could be a temple statue. The body is twice the scale you'd expect. The skin is rendered like marble. The pose echoes ancient sculptures of Aphrodite or seated goddesses. Picasso was reaching for things that had been made two thousand years before him. But — and this is the Picasso twist — look closely at her face. Look at her eyes. There's something not quite right. The proportions are off in a deliberate way. The classicism is a costume. Picasso never fully left Cubism behind. He was the kind of painter who could play in any style, and play with that style, and never be entirely inside it. The Large Bather looks classical. She is, somewhere underneath, still Picasso.

These paintings sit in this room because Domenica Walter found them comfortable. Big bodies. Recognizable forms. Classical references. She liked Picasso when he was being graceful. She didn't keep him when he was being difficult. Next is Matisse. The room is just through the next opening.
