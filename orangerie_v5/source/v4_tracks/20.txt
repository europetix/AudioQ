TITLE: Picasso — Twelve Works, Two Periods
KIND: ANCHOR · 4:00
WHERE: Walk into the Picasso room. Twelve works.
WHAT: Picassos from two different periods of his life — the early figurative work, and the 1920s 'return to order' classicism. No Cubism.
LISTEN: Why this Picasso room looks different from a typical Picasso room. And what's been removed.
---
Twelve Picassos. That's what's here. But this is going to be a strange Picasso room compared to what you might expect. If you've been to the Musée Picasso across the river, or to MoMA in New York, or to the Tate Modern in London — what you've seen there is Cubism. Fractured forms. Multiple viewpoints on a single face. Brown and grey and tan. That's the Picasso who changed everything between 1907 and 1914. There is no Cubism in this room. Why? Because Domenica Walter sold it. When she inherited Paul Guillaume's collection in 1934, it contained Cubist works by Picasso. She didn't like Cubism. So she sold every Cubist Picasso the collection had owned. What's left in this room is, on one side, Picasso's early figurative work — paintings from before he became famous — and on the other side, his later return to classical figuration in the 1920s. Two Picassos. Not the one in the middle. Let me show you the two sides. The early work first. Look for the small paintings of bathers, harlequins, and circus performers from the Blue and Rose periods around 1905. The figures are slender, elongated, melancholy. A boy with a flute. A woman seated. A blue-toned mother and child. These are paintings from Picasso's early twenties, when he was a poor immigrant in Paris, painting acrobats and prostitutes and street people. The blues are deep. The figures are quiet. There's almost no movement. Now move to the later side of the room. Mid-1920s Picasso. He's in his early forties. He's famous. He's been through Cubism, through Surrealism, through every controversy. And he goes back to figuration. Not the figuration of his Blue Period, but a kind of monumental, sculptural classicism. Big bodies. Heavy limbs. Faces that look like Greek statuary. These are sometimes called his "neo-classical" or "return to order" period. The two sides of this room represent the two parts of Picasso that Paul Guillaume — and Domenica after him — wanted to keep. The young struggling painter, before Cubism. And the established master, after Cubism. The middle part — the Cubist Picasso — is somewhere else. Mostly in private collections in America, mostly bought by Domenica's contemporaries from her in the 1930s and 1940s.

So when you look at the Picasso wall here, you're seeing what a particular kind of collector wanted Picasso to be. Two clean ends of his career. No fracturing. That tells you something about Domenica. She liked figuration. She liked recognizable bodies. She liked the painter Picasso was at twenty-two and the painter he was at forty-five, but not the painter he was at twenty-six. Her taste shaped what's in this room. Without her, the collection would look very different. Next track — one or two specific Picassos worth standing in front of.
