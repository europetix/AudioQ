TITLE: Stepping Into the First Oval Room — How to Look
KIND: ANCHOR · 4:00
WHERE: Just inside the first oval room. Stand near the center if you can.
WHAT: Four enormous curved panels of water lilies, mounted continuously around an oval. Daylight from a skylight overhead.
LISTEN: How to slow your eyes down. What Monet was actually trying to do here.
---
Step in. Take your time at the threshold. Notice, first, the quiet. The oval shape softens sound — there are no flat corners for footsteps and conversation to bounce off. Notice the light. It's coming from a skylight in the ceiling, filtered by a stretched fabric scrim that diffuses it. There's no electric spotlighting on the paintings. The light you're standing in is the light that's outside right now, just gentled. Now look at the walls. There are four large compositions in this first room — Setting Sun, Clouds, Green Reflections, and Morning. They wrap around you in a continuous oval — no breaks at the corners, because there are no corners. Together they're made of ten separate canvas sections, joined so seamlessly you'd never know unless you went looking for the seams. Each composition is two meters tall — about six and a half feet — and the longest two stretch more than twelve and a half meters across. They were mounted directly onto the curved walls when the museum opened in 1927, and they haven't moved since. The four compositions are oriented to the four points of the compass. Setting Sun, as you'd expect from its title, faces west. Morning faces east. The other two — Clouds and Green Reflections — fill the north and south arcs. The whole room is, in that sense, a kind of clock made out of light. Before we look at any one panel — let me tell you what Monet was trying to do here. In 1909, he gave an interview to a critic named Roger Marx. He had just finished a series of forty-eight water-lily paintings, but he was already imagining something larger. He said — and this is the line on the cover of this tour — that he wanted to create "the illusion of an endless whole, of a wave without horizon and without shore." A room with no edges. A surface of water that would surround the viewer entirely. He talked about nerves overstrained by work being able to rest here, "like a flower-filled aquarium." Those were his exact words. A space designed to dissolve the boundary between you and the painting. So here's what I'd like you to do. Don't read the panels like books. Don't look for objects. There are water lilies, yes — but there's no horizon line, no shoreline, no fixed point of view. Monet painted these from a small boat on his pond at Giverny, looking down at the surface of the water. Every panel is a view straight down. What you're seeing is the surface of a pond — with the sky reflected in it, and the trees and willows above, and the lily pads floating on top of all of that, layered into each other. The eye can't decide what's up and what's down. That's the point.

Walk slowly around the room. Don't try to take it all in at once. Stop at one composition. Look. Move on. The paintings will not get faster. We're going to take them in the order Monet designed for them — starting with Morning, on the eastern wall, because the cycle of these rooms moves with the sun.
