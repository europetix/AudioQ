TITLE: Tree Reflections & Clear Morning with Willows
KIND: ANCHOR · 4:00
WHERE: Turn to the remaining two compositions in the second oval room.
WHAT: Tree Reflections, the darkest composition in either room. And Clear Morning with Willows, which lifts the mood by a few degrees of light.
LISTEN: What's submerged in these paintings, and why Monet finished his life painting reflections rather than the things being reflected.
---
Two more compositions to go in this room. Look first at Tree Reflections — Reflets d'arbres. This is the darkest painting in either oval room. Deep greens, near-blacks, slate-blues, the occasional pale punctuation of a lily pad. There's no light source in it. There's no sky visible. What you're looking at is the water in shadow, with the entire mass of the willow trees above visible only as their reflection in the surface. Notice what Monet has done. The trees themselves are not in this painting. They're not visible. They're outside the frame, above the picture, casting their shapes onto the water. And what we're shown — what he chose to paint — is only the reflection. The original is somewhere off to the side. The image, on the water, is the entire subject. This is the most extreme version of what the Nymphéas project is. In every painting Monet had made before this — every haystack, every cathedral facade, every river view from his earlier years — he had painted the thing itself. A haystack was a haystack. A cathedral was a cathedral. Here, at the end of his life, he's painting only the reflection. The thing is gone. Only the image remains. If you stand in front of Tree Reflections long enough, the surface starts to feel like a memory. Like a glance backward at things that aren't there anymore. Now turn to Clear Morning with Willows. Le Matin clair aux saules. This composition is the gentlest in the second room — pale blues, soft pinks, willow leaves entering again from the top. The mood is the opposite of Tree Reflections: bright, opening, full of air. These two paintings, taken together, give you the full emotional range of the second room. Shadowed water on one side. Clear morning light on the other. Both made of reflections, both with the actual subjects somewhere just out of frame. And if you walk slowly around this whole second oval — Tree Reflections, Morning with Willows, The Two Willows, Clear Morning with Willows — you'll see that the four compositions together form a closed cycle. From dark to light, from submerged to airborne, from the willow as trunk to the willow as leaves. The room is its own complete poem.

A few visitors get to this point and feel they've seen everything they came to see. They walk out. They never go downstairs. I want to suggest that this would be a mistake. Because Paul Guillaume — the dealer who built the collection below us — was thirteen years old when Monet started painting these lilies. By the time these panels were installed in this room in 1927, Guillaume had been the most aggressive young dealer of modern art in Paris for nearly a decade. He was buying Picasso, Modigliani, Soutine, Rousseau, Derain — all alive, all working, all changing what painting could be. The Water Lilies are the end of one tradition. What's downstairs is the beginning of another. And we can't understand one without seeing the other. So we'll take one more breath here, in the second oval. And then we'll go down.
