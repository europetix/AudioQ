This is Morning. Matin, in French.

It's the longest composition in the first room — three panels joined together, two meters tall and twelve and three-quarter meters across. About forty-two feet, end to end. Stand back a little if you can. Try to see it as a whole before you walk along its length.

Look at the colors first. Cool blues. Pale lavender. A wash of soft green at the surface where the lily pads gather. There's no sun in this picture. There's no warmth. This is the pond at Giverny in the very early morning — before the day has decided what it's going to be.

Now look at what's actually painted, and what isn't.

At the far left edge, you'll see willow branches — narrow leaves hanging down into the picture. They drop from the top of the canvas like a curtain at the edge of your peripheral vision. At the far right, the same thing happens. Willow leaves enter from outside the frame. Between them, in the middle of the composition, there's almost nothing solid. No shoreline. No bank. No horizon. The eye has nowhere to land except on the lily pads themselves — small ovals of green, drifting in clusters, and the reflections of trees and sky on the water beneath them.

Monet did something radical here. By the time he started painting these large panels, around 1914, he had been painting his pond for nearly twenty years. He had already painted hundreds of versions of it. What he did differently in the Nymphéas was that he removed the bank. He cropped the world. He left only the surface of the water, the floating lilies, and what's reflected in the surface from above. The picture has no up and no down. It's a kind of disorientation by design.

Here's what to do with that. Look at the lily pads. Then look at what's behind them — the reflections. Then ask yourself which is which. The lily pads sit on top of the water. The reflections are below the water. But because there's no horizon, both of those things occupy the same picture plane. The pads and the reflected sky are in the same place. Your brain has to choose which it's looking at, moment by moment.

This is, by the way, one of the reasons abstract painters of the nineteen-fifties — Jackson Pollock, Joan Mitchell, Ellsworth Kelly — kept coming back to these rooms. Monet got to the dissolution of the picture plane fifty years before they did. He just kept calling them water lilies.

One last thing. Look closely at the brushwork. Step right up to the canvas. You'll see that Monet painted in short, broken strokes — flecks of pigment, dragged and overlapped. From three meters back, those flecks become reflections. From thirty centimeters away, they become what they actually are: paint. Pure paint, in dozens of layered colors.
