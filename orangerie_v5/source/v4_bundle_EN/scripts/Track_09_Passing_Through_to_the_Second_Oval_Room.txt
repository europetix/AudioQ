Walk through. Don't rush it.

This connecting space — the narrow passage between the two oval rooms — is doing real work. Monet and his architect Camille Lefèvre designed the two rooms to read like the two halves of the infinity symbol. The passage you're walking through right now is the single point where the two ovals touch. Mathematically, it has no width. Architecturally, it's a few steps of plaster. But what it does to your experience is force a small reset before the next room begins.

It also means something about the order of the paintings. In the first room, you were watching the cycle of a day — Morning on the east wall, Setting Sun on the west, Clouds and Green Reflections filling the cardinal arcs in between. The compositions in that room are about light hours and time of day.

The second room is different. The compositions there are about willows. About reflections of trees. About the deep water under the surface. If the first room is the open pond at the four hours of the day, the second room is the same pond, but looking inward — toward the trees on the bank, toward what's submerged, toward what's in shadow.

A second thing worth knowing. The second room is slightly larger than the first, and it holds twelve panels rather than ten — though the compositions still number four. Some of the canvases in there are the largest single paintings Monet ever made. The longest composition stretches over seventeen meters. Almost fifty-six feet.

Step in when you're ready. Stand near the center of the room for a moment. Let the new space surround you before you walk to any one painting.
