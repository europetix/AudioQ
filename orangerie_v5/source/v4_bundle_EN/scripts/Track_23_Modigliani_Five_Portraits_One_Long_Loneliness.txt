Five Modiglianis. Slow down here.

Amedeo Modigliani was born in Livorno, on the Tuscan coast, in 1884. He came to Paris in 1906, when he was twenty-two. He died there fourteen years later, in January 1920, of tubercular meningitis. He was thirty-five.

In those fourteen years he painted around three hundred works. He spent most of them poor. He drank heavily. He took drugs. He had affairs. He had a partner, Jeanne Hébuterne, who was nine months pregnant with his second child when he died, and who killed herself two days after his death by jumping out of her parents' apartment window. She was twenty-one.

That's the life that lies underneath these five paintings on this wall.

Now look at the paintings themselves. The faces are unmistakable. Long oval head, almost mask-like. The neck stretched, almost impossibly so. The eyes — and this is the key — most of them are empty. No pupils. Just almond-shaped slits filled with one solid color. Pale blue. Black. Sometimes nothing.

People often ask why he painted eyes that way. Modigliani is supposed to have given an answer himself, to one of his sitters who asked him to paint her eyes. He said: "When I know your soul, I'll paint your eyes." Until then, he painted the eyes empty. That's the line he reportedly used. It's been quoted for a hundred years and may have grown in the telling, but the impulse behind it is real. Modigliani treated the eyes not as physical features but as access to interiority. If he hadn't worked out who the sitter was — fully, deeply, by his own standard — he didn't put pupils in. The face stayed closed.

Find Paul Guillaume, Novo Pilota — the portrait of Paul Guillaume painted in 1915, when the young dealer was twenty-four and just two years into running his gallery. Modigliani painted it in oil on cardboard. Guillaume is wearing a dark suit. The face is the typical Modigliani oval, but here the eyes have pupils — Modigliani knew Guillaume well, by then, and painted them in. The title — Novo Pilota, "the new pilot" — is what Modigliani wrote on the canvas. He thought Guillaume was the new pilot of modern art. The new navigator. It's both flattering and slightly mocking, the way Modigliani's titles often were.

Look around at the other Modiglianis. Find The Young Apprentice — Le Petit Apprenti — painted in 1918 or 1919, near the end of his life. A young workman, probably a builder's assistant, in a blue jacket. The face is elongated, the eyes almost blank, the body is patient and tired. He could be sixteen. Modigliani painted dozens of these young anonymous Parisians — apprentices, servants, models. They're not portraits of important people. They're portraits of the people who lived around the artist's studio, who could be paid a few francs to sit for an afternoon.

There's also a portrait of a woman named Antonia. Find her if she's on the wall. She has the same long neck, the same closed face, the same posture of weary stillness.

Standing in this room, look at the five of them. They share a kind of quiet sadness. Some art critics call it elegance. Some call it melancholy. Whatever you call it, it's coming from somewhere true. Modigliani didn't have time. He knew he was sick — he had been tubercular since adolescence, and his lungs got worse every year. He painted as fast as he could. He died penniless. He was buried in a borrowed grave.

A year after his death, a memorial exhibition of his work in Paris sold out. The prices doubled the year after that, and doubled again the year after that. By 1934, when Paul Guillaume himself died at forty-two — almost exactly the same age Modigliani had been — Modigliani's paintings were among the most sought-after modern works in Europe.
