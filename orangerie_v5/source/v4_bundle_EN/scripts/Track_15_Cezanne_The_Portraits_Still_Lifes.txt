Now Cézanne. There are fifteen Cézanne paintings in the Walter-Guillaume collection. Most of them were bought by Paul Guillaume in the nineteen-twenties — by then, Cézanne had been dead for nearly twenty years, but his reputation was still climbing. Picasso reportedly called him "the father of us all." Matisse called him "the master of us all." Every painter we're going to see in the rest of the collection — Picasso, Matisse, Modigliani, Soutine, Derain — was, in some way, working through Cézanne.

Let me show you a few in this room.

First, the portraits of his wife. Cézanne painted his wife Hortense Fiquet at least twenty-six times over their thirty-year marriage. The Walter-Guillaume collection has at least two of those portraits. Find one — she'll be seated, hands folded, very still, looking somewhere past the viewer. The hands and face are painted with the same care, the same patient stacking of color planes, as the chair she's sitting in. Her body and the chair share the same materiality.

This is the Cézanne secret. He treats every part of the painting with the same weight. A face, a piece of fruit, a wall behind a sitter, a hillside — all of them get the same kind of attention, built up out of small, deliberate strokes of color laid next to each other. He doesn't model with light and shadow the way the Renaissance did. He models with planes of color set side by side, each one slightly different, building toward the form.

Now move to the still lifes. The collection includes works like Apples and Biscuits, Fruit, Serviette and Milk Jug, and Vase, Sugar Bowl and Apples. Look at one of them. Find the apples in particular. They're not soft. They don't have the glossy roundness of Dutch still life. They're built out of facets — small flat planes of red and orange and brown and green, each plane a slightly different color, butted up against each other. The apple is a thing made of light and color, more than a thing made of skin and flesh.

And here's why this matters historically. Twelve years after Cézanne's death in 1906, Picasso and Braque invented Cubism. And Cubism — which broke objects into facets, which showed multiple viewpoints at once, which treated form as a thing built up out of geometric planes — Cubism is, in many ways, Cézanne's still lifes turned up to maximum. Picasso said so himself. He said the only painter he had ever followed was Cézanne.

So when you look at these portraits and still lifes — paintings that might look modest, almost amateur in their stillness — you're looking at the source code of twentieth-century painting. Everything that fractures, everything that abstracts, everything that breaks the picture plane apart over the next eighty years is in some way responding to what Cézanne did first, here, with his wife and a bowl of fruit.
