A few practical notes before we say goodbye.

The Orangerie is open every day except Tuesdays, from nine in the morning to six in the evening. The last admission is at five-fifteen, and the rooms start closing at five-thirty. During exhibition periods there are sometimes Friday late nights — open until nine, with reduced admission after six. The museum is closed on three French holidays each year: May the first, July the fourteenth in the morning only, and December the twenty-fifth.

Tickets. You can buy them at the door, but you'll wait in line. Online reservation through billetterie.musee-orangerie.fr is faster and lets you walk straight in within a thirty-minute window of your reserved time. The Paris Museum Pass also gets you in, but you still need a reservation. EU citizens under twenty-six get free admission. The first Sunday of every month is free for everyone, but you still need to reserve a time slot — and it gets crowded.

A current event worth knowing about. There's a major temporary exhibition on right now: Henri Rousseau, The Ambition of Painting. It runs through July twentieth of this year, 2026. If you came to the museum mainly for the Water Lilies and you only have time for one extra thing, the Rousseau show is the one to see — especially after walking through the Rousseaus in the permanent collection just now. The exhibition argues for Rousseau as a serious ambitious painter, not the naïve curiosity history has sometimes made him.

After July, the next major show is Monet and Time, opening in late September and running through March of next year. That one will explore how Monet captured the passage of time in his series paintings, including the Water Lilies. If you can get to Paris during the run, it's worth a return trip.

A small note on what's around the museum. The Tuileries Garden cafés are decent for a coffee. There's a bookshop at the Orangerie itself, on the lower level — good for catalogs and prints, including the giant infinity-symbol floor plan postcard. Around the corner on the rue de Rivoli, you'll find the standard tourist arcade, and a few quieter side streets if you walk a couple of blocks north.

That's the practical part.

A few words to close. Thank you for walking through this museum with me. The Orangerie is small — you can do it in an hour. But it rewards going slowly. I hope we went slowly. I hope the Water Lilies opened up for you a little more on the second visit than the first. I hope you found at least one painting downstairs that wasn't on your list of things you came to see, and that surprised you. That's what a good museum visit does. It introduces you to things you didn't know you were going to meet.

The Musée de l'Orangerie holds two of the deepest collections of modern painting anywhere in the world. Both of them came out of difficult lives. Both of them were built by people who refused to let their work be small.

Walk gently the rest of the day. À bientôt.
