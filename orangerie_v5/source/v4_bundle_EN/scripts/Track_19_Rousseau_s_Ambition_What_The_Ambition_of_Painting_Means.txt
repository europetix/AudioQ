There's a temporary exhibition running upstairs in this museum right now — from the spring through the summer of 2026 — titled Henri Rousseau: The Ambition of Painting. If you have time, see it. But even if you don't, let me explain what the title means. It's a phrase worth understanding, because it tells you who Rousseau actually was.

When critics in Rousseau's lifetime called him naïve, they meant it as an insult. They meant he didn't know what he was doing. They meant he was an amateur who happened to produce strange pictures.

Rousseau heard those critics. He responded — not in essays, because he didn't write essays, but in his paintings. The Wedding Party, The Sleeping Gypsy, The Snake Charmer, the late Self-Portrait with Lamp — these are not modest pictures. They're not the works of someone who has lowered his sights. They're huge ambitious compositions, painted by a man who clearly believed he was making the most important art of his time.

Look around this room. Rousseau painted Yvonne and Christine Lerolle? No — that was Renoir, in the other room. But notice the contrast in scale and confidence. Renoir is painting his own friends in their parlor. Rousseau is painting a wedding party larger than life, an exotic jungle no one has seen, a customs officer's daughter posed like a Florentine icon. The subjects are smaller, in social terms, but the ambitions are absolutely not.

That's what the exhibition title is getting at. Rousseau wasn't a naïve painter — he was an ambitious painter who happened to be self-taught. The flatness, the careful patient leaves, the figures arranged like icons — these aren't failures of technique. They're choices made by someone who looked at Italian Renaissance frescoes in books and decided he was going to paint that way, only in modern Paris, only with the materials of a working-class life.

And here's why he became important. In 1907, Picasso bought one of Rousseau's paintings — a portrait of a woman — at a Parisian junk shop for five francs. He kept it for the rest of his life. It hung in every studio Picasso ever lived in, from Montmartre to Mougins. When Picasso looked at Rousseau, he didn't see incompetence. He saw permission. Permission to flatten the picture plane. Permission to paint figures as silhouettes. Permission to ignore academic perspective and treat the canvas as a flat field where shapes and colors could sit next to each other on their own terms.

Cubism, Fauvism, the early modernism Picasso and Matisse and Braque made between 1905 and 1914 — much of it owes a debt to Rousseau. Not because they copied him, but because he proved you could break the rules of academic painting and produce something that worked.

He never knew how influential he had become. He died in 1910, still poor, while Picasso was selling Cubist paintings for ten times what Rousseau had ever earned. But the artists who came after him knew. And Paul Guillaume, the dealer who built this collection, knew. He bought every Rousseau he could find, when he could find them cheap. Domenica kept the nine you see in this room.

So as you look around — at the wedding, at the cart, at the jungles, at the careful patient leaves — you're looking at the work of a man who refused to be small. That's the ambition of painting. He never lowered it.
