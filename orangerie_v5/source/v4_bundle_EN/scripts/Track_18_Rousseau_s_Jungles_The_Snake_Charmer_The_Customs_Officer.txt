The jungle paintings.

Henri Rousseau is famous for jungle scenes. Walls of dense foliage, exotic animals — tigers, monkeys, snakes — in clearings between palm fronds. The strange flatness of those paintings is partly what made his reputation. The most famous of them, The Snake Charmer, lives at the Musée d'Orsay. The Walter-Guillaume collection has versions of other jungle works, and you'll find a few in this room. Look for the dense green.

Here's the thing you need to know about Rousseau's jungles. He never saw one. He never went to the tropics. He never left France in his life.

His actual day job — the one that gave him his famous nickname, Le Douanier, the customs officer — was as a low-level toll collector at the Paris customs barrier. He inspected wine and produce coming into the city. The job paid badly. He retired in 1893 at the age of forty-nine to paint full-time, but he never made a real living from painting. He was poor for most of his life.

So where did the jungles come from? From three places.

First, the Jardin des Plantes — the botanical gardens in Paris, which had a tropical greenhouse he visited constantly. Second, the natural history museum next to the Jardin des Plantes, where there were stuffed tigers and monkeys. Third, illustrated books and magazines, which he cut up and saved as visual reference. The plants in his jungles are real plants — but they're plants from a glasshouse, painted by a man who walked through the Jardin des Plantes on his lunch breaks.

He took those sources — domestic, accessible, French — and built imaginary landscapes out of them that felt to viewers like dispatches from somewhere else entirely. That's the trick. The jungle paintings are made from material anyone in Paris could have walked over and looked at. Rousseau just looked harder.

Look at how the leaves are painted. Each leaf is drawn carefully, separately, almost as if he traced it from a botany book. There's no atmospheric perspective — no fading into the distance — so every leaf in the foreground has the same crispness as every leaf in the back. The result feels claustrophobic, intricate, dreamlike. The viewer can't see past the leaves.

And remember — this is the year nineteen-oh-five or nineteen-oh-eight. Picasso is in his early twenties, just starting on the work that will become Cubism. Matisse has just shocked the Salon d'Automne in 1905 with the Fauves. Modigliani is still in art school in Italy. While they're all working their way through what painting should do, this fifty-eight-year-old former customs officer is painting careful tigers in fake jungles, and the young modernists keep coming to his studio to look at what he's making.

In November 1908, Picasso and his friends held a famous banquet in Rousseau's honor at the Bateau-Lavoir — the artists' commune in Montmartre. There was a lot of red wine. Apollinaire read a poem. Rousseau played the violin. Some of the guests laughed at him. Others took him seriously. He left thinking he'd been crowned.

He died two years later, in 1910, at sixty-six. Pneumonia. His reputation kept growing after his death. Paul Guillaume bought his work cheaply and held onto it as the price climbed.
