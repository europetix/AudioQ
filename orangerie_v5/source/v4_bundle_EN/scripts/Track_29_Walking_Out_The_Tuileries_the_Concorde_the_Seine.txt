You're outside. Take a breath of actual outdoor air.

You're standing in the Jardin des Tuileries — one of the oldest formal gardens in Paris, originally designed for Catherine de Medici in the sixteenth century and reworked by André Le Nôtre in the seventeenth. The gravel, the rows of trees, the long axis of the central walkway — all of it is essentially Le Nôtre's design, kept in place for three and a half centuries.

If you have time, here are three directions you can walk from where you are.

First option: east, through the Tuileries, toward the Louvre. It's about a fifteen-minute walk straight up the central path. You'll pass statues, fountains, possibly an open-air carousel for children, and end up at the Louvre's pyramid courtyard. The Louvre is the biggest museum in the world. You can't see it in one visit. But just standing in the courtyard of the Cour Napoléon, with the pyramid in front of you and the old palace wrapped around it on three sides — that's worth ten minutes by itself.

Second option: west, to the Place de la Concorde. Just a few minutes' walk. Concorde is the largest public square in Paris. The obelisk in the center is over three thousand years old — taken from the temple of Luxor in Egypt and erected here in 1836. The square is also where the guillotine stood during the Reign of Terror, in 1793 and 1794, when over a thousand people were executed there, including Louis the Sixteenth and Marie Antoinette. There's no marker. The square just continues, beautiful, terrible, holding it all.

Third option: cross the river. You're a few minutes from the Pont de la Concorde, the bridge that crosses the Seine just south of where you're standing. On the other side is the Quai d'Orsay, and a five-minute walk along it brings you to the Musée d'Orsay — the great companion museum to the Orangerie, holding most of the impressionist and post-impressionist paintings of nineteenth-century France. The Orangerie and the Orsay have been administratively linked since 2010. If you have energy left and the Orsay is open, it's a natural next stop.

Or — and this is the option I'd actually recommend most days — just stay in the Tuileries. Find a chair. They're free. Sit by one of the round basins. Watch the children push their toy sailboats with sticks. Watch the pigeons. The Orangerie doesn't ask you to do anything next. The lilies are still in your head. Let them stay there for a while before you go back into normal Paris.
