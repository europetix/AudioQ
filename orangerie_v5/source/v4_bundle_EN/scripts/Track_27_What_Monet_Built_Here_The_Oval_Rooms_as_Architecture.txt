Look around this room. Not at the paintings. At the room itself.

The walls are curved. They form a continuous oval, with no corners. The ceiling is white, with a skylight in the middle — a circle of glass and stretched fabric scrim that lets daylight in but diffuses it so nothing creates a glare on the canvases. There's a bench in the center of the room, low enough that you can sit and still see all four compositions at once. The floor is a quiet polished stone.

Now — here's what makes this a remarkable space, beyond the paintings inside it.

Monet didn't just donate the panels. He designed the rooms. He worked with the architect Camille Lefèvre, but the spatial concept was his. He insisted on the oval shape. He insisted on natural light from above. He insisted that the panels be mounted directly onto the curved walls, with no breaks between panels — so the seams between the canvas sections would disappear. He insisted on the proportions of the rooms relative to the paintings. He insisted that nothing else be displayed in here. No sculpture. No other paintings. No furniture except the central bench. Nothing to compete with the lilies.

In other words, Monet didn't just give France eight compositions of water lilies. He gave France two rooms full of water lilies. The rooms are part of the work.

This is, by some accounts, the first piece of installation art in modern Western painting. The room is not a neutral container. The room is part of what you're looking at. Without these specific walls, this specific light, this specific oval shape, the paintings would mean something different. They were made for this space. They've never left it.

It also explains why the renovation in the early two thousands was so significant. Between roughly the 1960s and 2006, the Orangerie had been altered — a second floor was added inside the building, and the Water Lilies were displayed under electric light, with a suspended false ceiling above them blocking the original skylights. The natural light Monet had insisted on was gone for nearly forty years. People who came to the Orangerie before 2006 saw the lilies, but they didn't see them in the light Monet intended.

In 2000, a full renovation began. The architect Olivier Brochet ran the project. The added inner floor was demolished. The false ceiling came down. The skylights were restored. The natural light came back. The Walter-Guillaume collection — which had been on the ground floor — was moved downstairs to newly excavated basement rooms, where it lives now. The Orangerie reopened in 2006, returned, finally, to the configuration Monet had asked for in the 1920s.

So what you're standing in right now is, more or less, what Monet wanted. The light is right. The space is right. The paintings are mounted as he asked. It took the museum eighty years and four major renovations to get back to where it started.
