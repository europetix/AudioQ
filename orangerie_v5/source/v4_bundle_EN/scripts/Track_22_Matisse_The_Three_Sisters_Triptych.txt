Ten Matisses in this collection. The most important grouping in this room is the triptych of The Three Sisters — Les Trois Soeurs — three paintings hung side by side that show the same young woman three times in slightly different poses and costumes. Matisse painted them between 1916 and 1917, during the First World War, while Monet was painting his water lilies eighty miles away in Giverny.

Stand in front of them. Let your eye move across all three.

The dominant color is a deep saturated black-green-blue. Each figure is set against this dark ground. The clothing — striped fabric, a patterned sash, a flat-colored dress — interrupts the dark background with deliberate pieces of contrasting color. Reds. Yellows. Pinks. Whites. The figures themselves are simplified. The faces are masks. The hands are minimal. The bodies are arranged with the same flatness Matisse had been working toward for fifteen years.

This is what Matisse meant when he said his goal was an art "of balance, of purity and serenity, devoid of troubling subject matter, like a good armchair." Look at how this triptych works. Three calm figures. Three balanced compositions. No drama. No narrative. Just three young women, against a deep dark backdrop, dressed in colors that interact across all three panels.

Matisse was fundamentally a colorist. Not in the way Renoir was a colorist — Renoir uses color to make flesh feel alive. Matisse uses color as architecture. The colors in The Three Sisters do the structural work that line and shading would have done in a Renaissance painting. The figure is built out of color blocks set next to each other. The dress is one block. The wall is another. The sash is a third. The whole composition holds together because of how those blocks of color answer each other across the painting.

Now move to the odalisques. The Walter-Guillaume collection has several. Look for a woman reclining on cushions, in a room filled with patterned fabrics and an Oriental rug. The mood is languid. The colors are warm. The patterns of the room — the carpet, the wallpaper, the textiles — are all painted as if they're as important as the figure.

Matisse made these in the 1920s, in Nice, on the Côte d'Azur. He had moved south for the light. He had a series of models who came to his studio and posed in costumes. The setting is theatrical — these aren't real harems, they're his Nice apartment dressed up with fabric. The paintings have been criticized for that. Some critics see them as Orientalist fantasy, a wealthy Frenchman painting an imagined East. Others see them as careful color studies, where the actual subject is the relationship between a printed fabric and a body, between a pattern and a face.

Both are true. The odalisques exist in a tradition of Orientalist painting — Delacroix did it, Ingres did it, Renoir did it — and Matisse is working in that tradition with all of its complications. But what he's really doing, what makes these paintings still feel alive a century later, is the color work. Look at how a red sash sits next to a blue wall sits next to a yellow lampshade. The painting is a chord. Each part of it sounds against every other part.

When you've spent some time in front of the Three Sisters and at least one odalisque, you've understood the Matisse in this room.
