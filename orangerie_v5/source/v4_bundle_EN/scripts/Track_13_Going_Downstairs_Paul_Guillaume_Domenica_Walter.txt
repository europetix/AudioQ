Now we're going to walk through a collection that almost didn't exist. So before we look at any specific painting, let me tell you who built it.

Paul Guillaume was born in 1891, in a working-class neighborhood in Paris. He left school early. He found work as a stock boy in a tire factory. And in his late teens, somewhere around 1908 or 1909, he started picking up African and Oceanian objects — masks, statues — from the dockworkers who handled goods coming in from the French colonies. He sold them out of a little shop. He was completely self-taught.

In 1911, the poet Guillaume Apollinaire — yes, Apollinaire was his first name; Paul Guillaume was his surname — Apollinaire spotted this twenty-year-old in the African-art trade and introduced him to the avant-garde art world. To Picasso, Modigliani, Soutine, Derain. To the painters Apollinaire was writing about in the magazines.

Guillaume opened his first gallery in 1914. He was twenty-three. Over the next twenty years, he became one of the most important dealers in modern art in Europe. He bought Modigliani when nobody wanted Modigliani. He bought Soutine when Soutine was painting in a basement, alone, starving. He bought Henri Rousseau — the customs officer who painted on weekends — at a moment when most critics thought Rousseau was a joke. He held the famous 1918 "face-to-face" exhibition that paired Matisse and Picasso in the same gallery. He acquired around fifty Rousseaus over his career. He owned hundreds of paintings by the time he died.

And then, in 1934, at the age of forty-two, Paul Guillaume died. Suddenly. He had been planning, in his last years, to open his collection as the first French museum of modern art. He didn't get there.

His widow, Domenica, was twenty years younger than him. She was thirty-six when he died. Four years later, in 1938, she married a man named Jean Walter — an architect and industrialist, twenty years older than her, who had made a fortune in mining. She kept her late husband's collection but transformed it heavily. She sold over two hundred paintings — including all the de Chiricos, many of the Modiglianis, all of Picasso's Cubist works, and every single piece of African art Paul had collected. In their place, she bought more Renoirs and Cézannes, which she preferred. She also kept the things she liked most about her first husband's taste: the Rousseaus, the Soutines, some of the Matisses, the late Picassos.

Jean Walter died in 1957. Two years later, in 1959, the French state bought the first part of the collection from Domenica. The second part was purchased in 1963. She kept the right to live with the paintings until her own death — which came in 1977. The collection finally went on permanent display here at the Orangerie in 1984. Domenica had asked for it to be named after both of her husbands. So it's the Jean Walter and Paul Guillaume collection.

A hundred and forty-eight paintings. Renoir, Cézanne, Picasso, Matisse, Modigliani, Soutine, Derain, Rousseau, Utrillo, Laurencin, Gauguin, Sisley, Monet, Van Dongen. Built by a self-taught dealer who died at forty-two, kept and reshaped by his widow over the next forty-three years.
