Find this one. The Red Rock. Le Rocher Rouge. It's a vertical canvas — ninety-two centimeters tall, sixty-eight wide, so about three feet by two. Painted around 1895.

What you're looking at: a stand of trees in the lower half, painted in cross-hatched green brushstrokes. Behind and above them, taking up nearly half the canvas, a great wall of orange-red rock — not painted as a solid shape but built out of planes of color, the same patient layering we just talked about. A strip of blue sky at the top. That's it. That's the whole painting.

This is the Bibémus quarry, just outside Aix-en-Provence in Provence. Cézanne grew up in Aix. He had walked these quarries since he was a boy. In the eighteen-nineties and early nineteen-hundreds, he came back to them again and again — Bibémus is the same set of red rock formations he painted from many angles for the last decade of his life. They're abandoned quarries, where stone had been cut out of the hillside for centuries and then left to weather. The rocks have been exposed to air and rain long enough that they've turned this saturated orange-red.

Why does this small painting matter so much? Look at how Cézanne handles the rock face. It's not modeled with shadow. It's not given depth through perspective. It's a flat plane of color — almost abstract — pushed up against the trees in the foreground. The painting refuses depth in the way you'd expect from a landscape. The rock and the trees occupy almost the same picture plane.

When Georges Braque went to L'Estaque in 1908 — two years after Cézanne died — he started painting landscapes that look directly descended from this one. Houses turned into facets of color. Trees and buildings flattened into the same plane. That work of Braque's, along with what Picasso was doing in the same year, became Cubism.

Apollinaire — the same poet who introduced Paul Guillaume to all the modern painters we're seeing in this collection — wrote about Cézanne in 1910, just four years after his death. He said: "It is known that most new painters lay claim to this sincere and selfless artist." Those are Apollinaire's words. By 1910, every young painter in Paris was already saying Cézanne's name.

Stand in front of this painting for a moment. It's not large. It's not dramatic. But it's one of the small turning points in the history of painting. From here, in the Bibémus quarries, you can draw a line that runs through Picasso, Braque, Léger, Mondrian, and on through abstraction. Everything that flattens the picture plane in the next forty years is, in some way, on this canvas first.
