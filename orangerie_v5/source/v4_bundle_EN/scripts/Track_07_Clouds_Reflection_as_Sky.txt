This is Clouds. Les Nuages.

Same length as Morning — three panels joined, twelve and three-quarter meters across. But the mood is different. The colors are paler. There's more white. More lavender. More of that almost-pink that you get in Paris skies in the late afternoon, when the day is still soft but the sun is no longer high.

Now, look at the painting and ask yourself a question. Where is the sky in this picture?

Walk along it slowly. You'll find clouds — clearly clouds, white and grey and shifting — distributed across the canvas. But there's no horizon. There's no upward direction. The clouds aren't above. They're inside the surface of the water. What you're looking at is the reflection of the sky in the pond, and Monet has cropped out every other reference point so completely that the reflection has become the thing itself.

This is the trick of all the Nymphéas, but it's clearest here. Monet didn't paint clouds. He painted the surface of a pond in which clouds happened to be visible. The water is the painting. The sky is what's inside the water.

Look at the lily pads. They sit on top of those clouds — physically on top, since they float on the actual water — but visually, they look like they're hovering in the sky. The painting won't tell you which is right. Both are right.

This is, I think, why people come back to this room over and over. You can never quite settle the image. Every time you stand in front of it, the relationship between the lilies and the reflections shifts. Your brain keeps trying to decide. The painting keeps refusing.

There's a quiet joke embedded in the title, too. Nuages — clouds — is what we'd usually call the subject of a sky painting. But this isn't a sky painting. There's no sky here. There's only water and what water remembers.
