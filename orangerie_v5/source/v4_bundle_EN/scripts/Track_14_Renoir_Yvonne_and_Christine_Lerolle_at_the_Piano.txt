This is the Renoir room. There are twenty-five Renoir paintings in the Walter-Guillaume collection — more than any other artist. Domenica Walter loved Renoir. She bought painting after painting of his in the nineteen-thirties and forties. So you're standing in front of her taste, more than Paul Guillaume's.

Let me take you to one painting in particular. Yvonne and Christine Lerolle at the Piano. Yvonne et Christine Lerolle au piano. Painted in 1897. Oil on canvas, seventy-three by ninety-two centimeters.

Find it. Two young women at a piano. One in white, seated, hands on the keys. The other in red, leaning over her shoulder, looking at the music. The light is warm. The room is full of comfortable bourgeois furniture. On the wall behind them, you can make out two paintings — both by Degas — which were actually in the Lerolle family's home.

The story of this painting matters. The Lerolle family was a well-known household of art patrons in Paris in the eighteen-nineties. Henry Lerolle was a painter himself, and a collector. His daughters Yvonne and Christine were close to Renoir. Edgar Degas was their family friend. The two girls would eventually marry Eugène and Louis Rouart, sons of another major patron family — Degas had introduced the two families to each other.

So this painting comes out of a tight little circle of artists and the families who supported them. Renoir made several preparatory drawings before he started this one. He chose a horizontal format — landscape, not portrait — which gives the composition a settled, almost architectural calm. He was particularly fond of Christine, by his own account. And here's the curious detail. This painting was never part of the Lerolle family collection. Renoir kept it. He kept it in his own home for the rest of his life, and only after he died, in 1919, did it leave the studio.

Why? Nobody knows. It's the kind of small mystery that gets glossed over in museum labels. He just didn't sell it.

Decades later, in 1937, Domenica bought it at a public auction, jointly with another dealer named Paul Durand-Ruel. After 1947, she bought Durand-Ruel out and kept the painting entirely. It was one of her favorite Renoirs in the collection. When you stand in front of it, you're looking at a painting Renoir refused to let go of, that ended up in the home of a woman who was building the largest private gallery of his work in Europe.

Now, the rest of the room. You'll see Gabrielle and Jean — Renoir's son Jean, the future film director, painted as a baby with the family servant. Claude Renoir in Clown Costume — his youngest son in costume. Late nudes — bathers, women drying themselves, fleshy bodies rendered in deep pinks and reds. These are all from Renoir's last twenty years. By this point he had crippling arthritis. He was painting with brushes strapped to his hands because his fingers could no longer hold them. He lived in the south of France, at Cagnes-sur-Mer, surrounded by family and models.

The late Renoir is divisive. Some viewers find it overly sweet, too pink, too domestic. Others find in it a quiet defiance — an old man with ruined hands continuing to make paintings about pleasure, family, music, the human body, while his body itself was failing.

Take a few minutes in this room. The piano picture will be the centerpiece, but look at the late nudes too. There's something honest in them, if you give them time.
