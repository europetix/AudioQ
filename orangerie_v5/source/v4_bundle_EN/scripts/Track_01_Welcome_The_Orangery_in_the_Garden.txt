So — here we are. Take a breath.

You're standing at the western corner of the Jardin des Tuileries, with the Place de la Concorde a few minutes behind you and the Seine just over the parapet to your right. The building in front of you is long and low — almost too modest, given what's inside. It's the Musée de l'Orangerie.

Before we step in, let me tell you what we're walking into. This is one of the smallest major museums in Paris. You can see it all in about an hour and a half. But it has, on the ground floor, two oval rooms — designed by Claude Monet himself — that hold eight large compositions of water lilies. Twenty-two panels of canvas in total, joined together into eight uninterrupted views of his pond at Giverny. And on the lower level, it has a private collection of modern art that an early-twentieth-century dealer named Paul Guillaume put together between 1914 and 1934. After he died young — at forty-two — his widow Domenica and her second husband, the architect Jean Walter, expanded and reshaped it. The French state bought it from her in the late 1950s. Today, it's a hundred and forty-eight paintings: Renoir, Cézanne, Picasso, Matisse, Modigliani, Soutine, Rousseau, Derain. A small, very dense, very personal collection of the early modern moment in Paris.

That's the museum. The Water Lilies — the Nymphéas — and the Walter-Guillaume collection. Two rooms upstairs, a longer route downstairs.

Here's what I want to suggest for the way we walk through it. Most visitors come in, spend ten minutes in the Water Lilies rooms, and move on. I'd like us to do something different. We'll go into the lilies first, slowly, panel by panel. Then we'll go downstairs and meet the Walter-Guillaume collection. And then — and this is the part most people skip — we'll come back up to the Water Lilies and sit with them one more time. Because everything you see downstairs changes what those lilies feel like. Monet painted them as a gift to France after the First World War, a "haven for peaceful meditation," in his own words. And meditation isn't something you do once, in a hurry.

A few practical things before we move. The museum is open every day except Tuesdays, nine to six. The Water Lilies rooms have natural skylights — the light shifts as the sky shifts, which is exactly how Monet wanted it. So if it's a bright day, those rooms will look one way; if it's overcast, they'll look another. There's no wrong day to come.

If you've already got your ticket — good. Let's go in.
