This is Setting Sun. Soleil couchant. It's the smallest of the four compositions in this room — a single panel of canvas, two meters by six. That's about nineteen and a half feet across. After Morning, it'll feel almost intimate.

But look at the colors.

This is the warmest painting in the whole Orangerie. Deep reds. Burnt orange. Gold that almost glows. The reflections in the water are no longer cool blue — they're embers. The lily pads, which a moment ago were soft pale green, are now caught in reflected fire. This is the pond at Giverny at the end of the day, when the western sky is on its way down and the water remembers it.

Notice the orientation. This composition faces west — the actual western direction, by compass — and the architect Camille Lefèvre and Monet planned that on purpose. So Setting Sun is on the west wall. Morning is on the east. The two ends of the day, lined up with the two ends of the world.

Now here's a question that art historians have argued about for over a century. Look at how saturated these reds are. Look at the orange. Some scholars have suggested that the warmth in this composition reflects Monet's failing eyesight. By the early nineteen-twenties, Monet had advanced cataracts in both eyes. Cataracts shift color perception toward yellow and red — they pull the cooler tones out of what you see. After his cataract operation in 1923, on his right eye, Monet looked at some of the canvases he'd painted in the years before and said they looked too red. He repainted some. He destroyed others.

So — is Setting Sun a painting of dusk, or a painting of dusk seen through cataracts, or both? Probably both. We can't pull them apart. Monet was eighty years old when he was working on these. He'd lost his wife Alice in 1911. He'd lost his oldest son Jean in 1914. The First World War was happening, on French soil, only forty miles from his garden. His eyes were failing. And he kept painting. Whatever those reds are, they're the reds of a man working through everything at once.

There's no neat answer to give you about that. Just stand in front of it. The painting is here. The reds are here. You're seeing what he saw — filtered through whatever he saw it with.
