This is The Two Willows — Les Deux Saules. The longest composition in the whole Orangerie. Four canvas panels joined end to end, two meters tall and over seventeen meters across. Almost fifty-six feet.

Stand back. Try to take in the whole length of it before you move. You almost can't, even from the center of the room. The painting is bigger than your field of vision.

Now — here's what's different about this composition. At the far left, you'll see a willow trunk descending straight down into the water. At the far right, another willow trunk does the same thing. Two vertical lines, anchoring the painting at both ends. Everything between them — water, lily pads, reflections, sky-on-water — flows uninterrupted from one trunk to the other.

In every other Nymphéas composition, Monet removed every reference point. Here, he gave you two. Why?

Because Monet's willows weren't decoration. They were the actual willows on the actual banks of his pond at Giverny — trees he had planted himself and tended for thirty years. He once said that if he hadn't become a painter, he would have become a gardener. The pond at Giverny was something he had built — diverted from a branch of the river Epte in 1893, with permission from the local authorities, and stocked with water lilies he ordered from a specialist nursery. The willows around it were grown specifically to drop their leaves into the water. The Japanese bridge that crossed it was inspired by prints he had been collecting since the eighteen-seventies.

So in The Two Willows, those trunks are not symbolic. They're literal. They're the actual willows that stood at the actual ends of the actual pond. And by including them, Monet is grounding the painting — telling you that even in this infinite-feeling surface of water, there are real places on a real bank, with real trees holding up real sky.

Now look just to the right — or left, depending on where you're standing — at the next composition. This one is Morning with Willows. Le Matin aux saules. Same length almost — three panels, twelve and three-quarter meters across. But here the willow trunks aren't on the picture. Instead, the willow leaves themselves enter from above. Long thin leaves, like a curtain, descending into the painting from the top edge.

You're seeing the same trees, but from a different position on the pond. In one composition Monet rowed close to the willow trunks. In the other, he was beneath them — looking up through the leaves at the water.

These two compositions are siblings. They were probably painted around the same time, between roughly 1920 and 1926, in the third studio Monet had built specifically for them at Giverny. That studio had north-facing skylights, just like this room. He could work on canvases this size only because he had designed the building to make it possible.

Walk along The Two Willows from left to right. Stop at the willow trunk on each end. Spend a moment with each. Those are the two ends of his world.
