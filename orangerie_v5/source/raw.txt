Musée de l'Orangerie
A V4 humanized walking audio tour — thirty tracks, about one hour and forty minutes.
The Orangerie is small. You can see it in an hour and a half. But it contains, on the ground floor, two oval
rooms designed by Claude Monet for his eight Water Lilies — paintings the artist worked on for the last
twelve years of his life, while losing his wife, losing a son, and going nearly blind. And on the lower level,
the Walter-Guillaume collection: one hundred and forty-eight paintings of the early modern moment in Paris,
assembled by a working-class dealer who died at forty-two and shaped over forty years by his widow
Domenica.
This tour walks you through both. The Water Lilies first, slowly. Then downstairs through Renoir, Cézanne,
Rousseau, Picasso, Matisse, Modigliani, Soutine, and the quieter voices around them. Then back up to the
lilies, for one more visit, before walking out into the Tuileries and the rest of Paris.
Take your time. The paintings will not get faster. Neither should we.

The Glass Wall & the Stone Wall
Why this building is shaped the way it is
STOP & LOOK
WHERE

Just inside the entrance, or pause again outside if you can see the south facade.
WHAT YOU'LL SEE

Two long walls, deliberately unequal. South: glass, sun, the Seine. North: solid stone, no windows.
LISTEN FOR

How a building made to keep oranges warm in winter turned out to be perfect for holding light.

Look at the building.
It's a long rectangle, and the two long walls are not the same. The one facing the Seine — south — is glass.
The one facing the gardens — north — is almost entirely solid stone. No windows. Nothing.
That asymmetry is the whole story of this place.
The Orangerie was built in 1852, on the orders of Napoleon III, to do exactly what its name says: shelter the
orange trees that grew in pots in the Tuileries Garden. Orange trees can't survive a Paris winter. So every
autumn, the gardeners wheeled them in, and every spring they wheeled them back out. The architect was a
man named Firmin Bourgeois — he died the year after it was completed, so the entrance facades were
finished by Louis Visconti, who was working on the Louvre at the same time. If you look closely at the
carved decoration above the doorways, you'll see ears of corn, cornucopias, plants — the iconography of a
working greenhouse.
The glass wall faces south to catch the winter sun. The stone wall faces north to block the cold winds. That's
just sensible greenhouse design. The trees needed light, and they needed shelter, and the building gave them
both.
Now — here's the part that matters. In 1921, three years after the end of the First World War, the French state
was looking for somewhere to display the Water Lilies that Monet was promising to donate. The painter had
originally imagined them at the Musée Rodin, in another building entirely. But Georges Clemenceau — the
wartime prime minister, who was also Monet's closest friend — pushed for them to come here. To the
Orangerie.
Why? Because Monet wanted the paintings lit by natural daylight. Not electric light. Not gallery spots. The
light of the sky, coming down from above, shifting through the day. And this building — this old greenhouse
for orange trees, with its long glass wall and its tradition of being a place where sunlight could be trusted —
was already built for that. They added skylights to the roof. They built two oval rooms inside. And the same
architecture that had been designed to keep citrus trees alive turned out to be the right architecture for holding
eight panels of pondwater.

TRACK 03

From Citrus Trees to Monet
How the Orangerie became a museum
STOP & LOOK
WHERE

Inside the entrance lobby, near the introductory wall panels.
WHAT YOU'LL SEE

A small, calm vestibule. The Water Lilies rooms are just ahead. The Walter-Guillaume collection is
downstairs.
LISTEN FOR

A hundred and twenty years of small decisions — a war, a friendship, a contract signed two days after the
Armistice.

Let's walk through how this place actually became what it is. There are a few dates worth holding in your
head.
Eighteen-fifty-two: built as an orangery, as we just talked about. For the next sixty years, it lived a quiet life
— winter shelter for trees, summer venue for the occasional concert, dog show, or art exhibition. Nothing
remarkable. The Empire fell in 1870. The Tuileries Palace next door burned in 1871. The Orangerie
survived. The state took it over in 1921 and gave it to the Under-Secretariat of State for Fine Arts, with the
idea of using it as exhibition space for living artists.
And then comes Claude Monet.
Monet was eighty-one years old in 1921, living at Giverny in the Normandy countryside. For almost twenty
years, he had been painting his pond — water lilies, willows, the surface of water, the sky reflected in it —
over and over again. After the outbreak of the First World War, those paintings had grown enormous. He was
building rooms in his garden specifically to paint in: a third studio with skylights, big enough to hold
canvases nearly seven feet tall and, in some cases, more than fifty feet long.
On November twelfth, 1918 — the day after the Armistice that ended the war — Monet wrote a letter to
Clemenceau. He said: "I'm about to finish two decorative panels which I want to sign on the day of victory,
and ask you to offer to the State." He wanted to give them as a monument to peace.
That offer eventually grew. Monet ended up committing not two panels but eight compositions —
twenty-two canvas sections in all, joined together so the seams disappear. The donation contract was signed
in 1922. And he and the architect Camille Lefèvre worked out the design together — two oval rooms, side by
side, the panels mounted directly onto the walls. The floor plan, from above, looks like the symbol for
infinity. Two ovals, meeting at a single point.
Now — Monet didn't actually want to give them up. He kept working on them until the very end of his life,
refusing to part with them. He died on December fifth, 1926, at Giverny. The panels were finally installed at
the Orangerie a few months later, and the museum opened to the public in May 1927.
The Walter-Guillaume collection came much later — purchased by the state in 1959 and 1963, displayed
permanently from 1984. That collection lives downstairs. The Water Lilies live up here, on the ground floor.

TRACK 04

Stepping Into the First Oval Room — How to Look
Before any single painting — how to be in the space
STOP & LOOK
WHERE

Just inside the first oval room. Stand near the center if you can.
WHAT YOU'LL SEE

Four enormous curved panels of water lilies, mounted continuously around an oval. Daylight from a
skylight overhead.
LISTEN FOR

How to slow your eyes down. What Monet was actually trying to do here.

Step in. Take your time at the threshold.
Notice, first, the quiet. The oval shape softens sound — there are no flat corners for footsteps and
conversation to bounce off. Notice the light. It's coming from a skylight in the ceiling, filtered by a stretched
fabric scrim that diffuses it. There's no electric spotlighting on the paintings. The light you're standing in is
the light that's outside right now, just gentled.
Now look at the walls. There are four large compositions in this first room — Setting Sun, Clouds, Green
Reflections, and Morning. They wrap around you in a continuous oval — no breaks at the corners, because
there are no corners. Together they're made of ten separate canvas sections, joined so seamlessly you'd never
know unless you went looking for the seams. Each composition is two meters tall — about six and a half feet
— and the longest two stretch more than twelve and a half meters across. They were mounted directly onto
the curved walls when the museum opened in 1927, and they haven't moved since.
The four compositions are oriented to the four points of the compass. Setting Sun, as you'd expect from its
title, faces west. Morning faces east. The other two — Clouds and Green Reflections — fill the north and
south arcs. The whole room is, in that sense, a kind of clock made out of light.
Before we look at any one panel — let me tell you what Monet was trying to do here.
In 1909, he gave an interview to a critic named Roger Marx. He had just finished a series of forty-eight
water-lily paintings, but he was already imagining something larger. He said — and this is the line on the
cover of this tour — that he wanted to create "the illusion of an endless whole, of a wave without horizon and
without shore." A room with no edges. A surface of water that would surround the viewer entirely. He talked
about nerves overstrained by work being able to rest here, "like a flower-filled aquarium." Those were his
exact words. A space designed to dissolve the boundary between you and the painting.
So here's what I'd like you to do. Don't read the panels like books. Don't look for objects. There are water
lilies, yes — but there's no horizon line, no shoreline, no fixed point of view. Monet painted these from a
small boat on his pond at Giverny, looking down at the surface of the water. Every panel is a view straight
down. What you're seeing is the surface of a pond — with the sky reflected in it, and the trees and willows
above, and the lily pads floating on top of all of that, layered into each other.
The eye can't decide what's up and what's down. That's the point.

TRACK 05

Morning — The Eastern Composition
Matin, c. 1914–1926
STOP & LOOK
WHERE

Stand in front of the long composition on the eastern wall of the first oval room.
WHAT YOU'LL SEE

A composition of three joined panels, almost forty-two feet across. Pale blues, soft greens. Water lilies
floating on a pond at first light.
LISTEN FOR

How Monet uses willow leaves at the edges to frame the surface — and why he removed every reference
point in the middle.

This is Morning. Matin, in French.
It's the longest composition in the first room — three panels joined together, two meters tall and twelve and
three-quarter meters across. About forty-two feet, end to end. Stand back a little if you can. Try to see it as a
whole before you walk along its length.
Look at the colors first. Cool blues. Pale lavender. A wash of soft green at the surface where the lily pads
gather. There's no sun in this picture. There's no warmth. This is the pond at Giverny in the very early
morning — before the day has decided what it's going to be.
Now look at what's actually painted, and what isn't.
At the far left edge, you'll see willow branches — narrow leaves hanging down into the picture. They drop
from the top of the canvas like a curtain at the edge of your peripheral vision. At the far right, the same thing
happens. Willow leaves enter from outside the frame. Between them, in the middle of the composition,
there's almost nothing solid. No shoreline. No bank. No horizon. The eye has nowhere to land except on the
lily pads themselves — small ovals of green, drifting in clusters, and the reflections of trees and sky on the
water beneath them.
Monet did something radical here. By the time he started painting these large panels, around 1914, he had
been painting his pond for nearly twenty years. He had already painted hundreds of versions of it. What he
did differently in the Nymphéas was that he removed the bank. He cropped the world. He left only the
surface of the water, the floating lilies, and what's reflected in the surface from above. The picture has no up
and no down. It's a kind of disorientation by design.
Here's what to do with that. Look at the lily pads. Then look at what's behind them — the reflections. Then
ask yourself which is which. The lily pads sit on top of the water. The reflections are below the water. But
because there's no horizon, both of those things occupy the same picture plane. The pads and the reflected
sky are in the same place. Your brain has to choose which it's looking at, moment by moment.
This is, by the way, one of the reasons abstract painters of the nineteen-fifties — Jackson Pollock, Joan
Mitchell, Ellsworth Kelly — kept coming back to these rooms. Monet got to the dissolution of the picture

Setting Sun — Where Light Becomes Fire
Soleil couchant, c. 1914–1926
STOP & LOOK
WHERE

Cross to the opposite wall of the first oval room — the western arc.
WHAT YOU'LL SEE

The smallest of the four compositions in this room. A single panel, two meters by six. Reds, oranges,
deep golds. The pond at dusk.
LISTEN FOR

Why this panel is the warmest in the room — and what the debate about Monet's eyesight has to do with
it.

Now turn around. Walk to the opposite wall.
This is Setting Sun. Soleil couchant. It's the smallest of the four compositions in this room — a single panel
of canvas, two meters by six. That's about nineteen and a half feet across. After Morning, it'll feel almost
intimate.
But look at the colors.
This is the warmest painting in the whole Orangerie. Deep reds. Burnt orange. Gold that almost glows. The
reflections in the water are no longer cool blue — they're embers. The lily pads, which a moment ago were
soft pale green, are now caught in reflected fire. This is the pond at Giverny at the end of the day, when the
western sky is on its way down and the water remembers it.
Notice the orientation. This composition faces west — the actual western direction, by compass — and the
architect Camille Lefèvre and Monet planned that on purpose. So Setting Sun is on the west wall. Morning is
on the east. The two ends of the day, lined up with the two ends of the world.
Now here's a question that art historians have argued about for over a century. Look at how saturated these
reds are. Look at the orange. Some scholars have suggested that the warmth in this composition reflects
Monet's failing eyesight. By the early nineteen-twenties, Monet had advanced cataracts in both eyes.
Cataracts shift color perception toward yellow and red — they pull the cooler tones out of what you see.
After his cataract operation in 1923, on his right eye, Monet looked at some of the canvases he'd painted in
the years before and said they looked too red. He repainted some. He destroyed others.
So — is Setting Sun a painting of dusk, or a painting of dusk seen through cataracts, or both? Probably both.
We can't pull them apart. Monet was eighty years old when he was working on these. He'd lost his wife Alice
in 1911. He'd lost his oldest son Jean in 1914. The First World War was happening, on French soil, only forty
miles from his garden. His eyes were failing. And he kept painting. Whatever those reds are, they're the reds
of a man working through everything at once.
There's no neat answer to give you about that. Just stand in front of it. The painting is here. The reds are here.
You're seeing what he saw — filtered through whatever he saw it with.

Clouds — Reflection as Sky
Les Nuages, c. 1914–1926
STOP & LOOK
WHERE

Move to the long wall holding Clouds — a three-panel composition almost the length of Morning.
WHAT YOU'LL SEE

Three joined panels, about forty-two feet wide. The pond surface, but with cloud reflections sliding across
it. Pale pinks, lavenders, slate blues.
LISTEN FOR

Where the sky is in this painting — and the trick that makes you forget which way is up.

This is Clouds. Les Nuages.
Same length as Morning — three panels joined, twelve and three-quarter meters across. But the mood is
different. The colors are paler. There's more white. More lavender. More of that almost-pink that you get in
Paris skies in the late afternoon, when the day is still soft but the sun is no longer high.
Now, look at the painting and ask yourself a question. Where is the sky in this picture?
Walk along it slowly. You'll find clouds — clearly clouds, white and grey and shifting — distributed across
the canvas. But there's no horizon. There's no upward direction. The clouds aren't above. They're inside the
surface of the water. What you're looking at is the reflection of the sky in the pond, and Monet has cropped
out every other reference point so completely that the reflection has become the thing itself.
This is the trick of all the Nymphéas, but it's clearest here. Monet didn't paint clouds. He painted the surface
of a pond in which clouds happened to be visible. The water is the painting. The sky is what's inside the
water.
Look at the lily pads. They sit on top of those clouds — physically on top, since they float on the actual water
— but visually, they look like they're hovering in the sky. The painting won't tell you which is right. Both are
right.
This is, I think, why people come back to this room over and over. You can never quite settle the image.
Every time you stand in front of it, the relationship between the lilies and the reflections shifts. Your brain
keeps trying to decide. The painting keeps refusing.
There's a quiet joke embedded in the title, too. Nuages — clouds — is what we'd usually call the subject of a
sky painting. But this isn't a sky painting. There's no sky here. There's only water and what water remembers.
Stand here for as long as you want. We'll move to Green Reflections next.

STOP & LOOK

WHAT YOU'LL SEE

Two panels joined together, about twenty-eight feet wide. Deep greens, blue-greens, dark water. The

LISTEN FOR

Why this is often the panel visitors stop the longest in front of — and what it might be doing to your
nervous system.

Last composition in this room. Green Reflections — Reflets verts.
Two panels joined together, two meters tall and eight and a half meters across. So shorter than Morning and
Clouds — about twenty-eight feet — but still substantial. After the warm reds of Setting Sun and the pale
pinks of Clouds, this one will feel like cool water.
Stand in front of it. Let your eyes adjust.
The dominant color is green. Not bright green — not the green of spring leaves. This is the green of deep
water, of moss seen through shadow, of willow leaves reflected on a still surface. It's the green you get when
light has had to travel through layers of water and leaf before reaching your eye. Where the other
compositions in this room use blue or pink or red as their main note, this one stays in the cool middle of the
spectrum and never leaves.
Look at the water lilies here. They're scattered across the surface in small floating clusters. They're not the
subject. They're not even particularly emphasized. They're more like punctuation — small marks of pink and
white and pale yellow that interrupt the green long enough for your eye to find a place to rest.
Now here's something I want to point out about what happens to you when you look at this painting. Monet
said, in the 1909 interview with Roger Marx — the one the cover quote is from — that he wanted these
rooms to provide "an asylum of peaceful meditation in the middle of a flower-filled aquarium." His exact
words. He was specifically thinking about people with nerves worn out by work. He wanted the room to slow
you down.
And there's evidence it actually does that. The eyes have to keep adjusting between the lily pads and the
reflections beneath them. There's nowhere to focus for long. The painting refuses to resolve. And while your
eyes are doing that — while they're constantly making and unmaking a stable image — something in the rest
of your body slows down. Your breathing changes. You stop scrolling, mentally. You stop reaching for the
next thing.
That's not mystical. It's just what happens when a painting doesn't let your visual attention settle.

Claude Monet was born in 1840. He moved to Giverny — the village in Normandy where he built the pond

years there, until he died in December 1926.

and 1926. Twelve years. The last twelve years of his life. Which means I have to tell you about three things
that were happening while he painted them.
The first thing is his wife. Alice Monet, his second wife, died in May 1911. She was sixty-six. Monet had
loved her for thirty years. After her death, he stopped painting almost entirely for nearly two years. The
letters he wrote in that period are heartbreaking. He said he couldn't see anything. He said the world had gone
flat. When he finally started working again, he started on these lilies.
The second thing is the war. The First World War began in August 1914. Giverny is about forty miles from
the front line at its closest. Monet could hear artillery from his garden. His older son Jean was already ill —
he died in February 1914, six months before the war began, at the age of forty-six. The younger son Michel
went to fight. Monet's stepson Jean-Pierre also went. The village around Giverny emptied of young men. For
four years, while Monet was painting these enormous serene panels of his pond, France was being torn apart
at the other end of the same train line.

tanks. They are paintings of a private garden, of a private pond, of one man's attempt to keep painting water
lilies while the world was on fire. And that fact — the fact that he kept painting them anyway — is what
made them, in his mind, a memorial. The day after the Armistice, in November 1918, Monet wrote to
Clemenceau and said he was ready to donate the panels to France. He called them — and this is his phrasing
— "a monument to peace."

First, André Derain. The Walter-Guillaume collection has thirty-one Derain paintings — more than any other

Derain had been one of the original Fauves — the wild, color-saturated movement that scandalized Paris in
1905. By the time Paul Guillaume started collecting him, Derain had moved on. He had pulled back into
something quieter, more classical. The Derains in this collection are mostly from the 1920s and 1930s. Find
Le Joueur de cornemuse — The Bagpipe Player — or Arlequin et Pierrot — Harlequin and Pierrot. The

backward. Others find a sustained dignity in it.
Now Marie Laurencin. The collection has seven of her paintings. Laurencin was one of the few women in the
Paris avant-garde of the early twentieth century to make a living from her painting. She had been
Apollinaire's lover for years. She kept her own studio. She painted, almost exclusively, women — in soft
pastel pinks, pale blues, dove greys. Groups of women. Mothers and daughters. Friends. The faces are
simplified, the bodies elongated, the palette deliberately gentle.
For decades Laurencin was condescended to. Critics called her work decorative, feminine, lightweight. In
recent years, that consensus has been revisited. Laurencin made a specific deliberate choice to paint women,
in a register of softness, without irony and without male commentary. It's a kind of feminism by aesthetic
rather than by argument. Look at her group portraits in this room. They're not sentimental — they're patient.

hilltop neighborhood in Paris where he was born and lived. White walls. Black shutters. Cobblestone slopes.

Utrillo's life was difficult. He was the son of the painter Suzanne Valadon, who had been Renoir's and

Why these rooms are sometimes called the first installation artwork in modern art.

Look around this room. Not at the paintings. At the room itself.
The walls are curved. They form a continuous oval, with no corners. The ceiling is white, with a skylight in
the middle — a circle of glass and stretched fabric scrim that lets daylight in but diffuses it so nothing creates
a glare on the canvases. There's a bench in the center of the room, low enough that you can sit and still see all
four compositions at once. The floor is a quiet polished stone.
Now — here's what makes this a remarkable space, beyond the paintings inside it.
Monet didn't just donate the panels. He designed the rooms. He worked with the architect Camille Lefèvre,
but the spatial concept was his. He insisted on the oval shape. He insisted on natural light from above. He
insisted that the panels be mounted directly onto the curved walls, with no breaks between panels — so the
seams between the canvas sections would disappear. He insisted on the proportions of the rooms relative to
the paintings. He insisted that nothing else be displayed in here. No sculpture. No other paintings. No
furniture except the central bench. Nothing to compete with the lilies.
In other words, Monet didn't just give France eight compositions of water lilies. He gave France two rooms
full of water lilies. The rooms are part of the work.
This is, by some accounts, the first piece of installation art in modern Western painting. The room is not a
neutral container. The room is part of what you're looking at. Without these specific walls, this specific light,
this specific oval shape, the paintings would mean something different. They were made for this space.
They've never left it.
It also explains why the renovation in the early two thousands was so significant. Between roughly the 1960s
and 2006, the Orangerie had been altered — a second floor was added inside the building, and the Water
Lilies were displayed under electric light, with a suspended false ceiling above them blocking the original
skylights. The natural light Monet had insisted on was gone for nearly forty years. People who came to the
Orangerie before 2006 saw the lilies, but they didn't see them in the light Monet intended.
In 2000, a full renovation began. The architect Olivier Brochet ran the project. The added inner floor was
demolished. The false ceiling came down. The skylights were restored. The natural light came back. The
Walter-Guillaume collection — which had been on the ground floor — was moved downstairs to newly
