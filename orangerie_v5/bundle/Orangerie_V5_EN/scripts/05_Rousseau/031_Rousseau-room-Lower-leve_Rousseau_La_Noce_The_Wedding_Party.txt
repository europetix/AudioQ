Henri Rousseau's day job gave him a nickname that has outlived almost everything else about him. He was a clerk at the octroi, the toll barrier at the gates of Paris. So people called him le Douanier, the customs officer, and the name has stuck ever since.

If you know one thing about him, it's probably his jungle pictures. Don't look for those here. The Rousseaus in this collection stay much closer to home: a wedding, a neighbour's cart, a child, anglers, a factory.

And this is the most ambitious of them. La Noce, The Wedding Party, is one of the most ambitious compositions Rousseau ever undertook. He painted it around 1905, when he was already in his early sixties.

Look at the guests first. Every face is a particular person, and nobody is just a generic relative filling a gap. Then look behind them. The trees are painted kind by kind, each species told apart with real care, and they shrink fast as they go back. A yellow-ochre background glows through, and together with the trees it forms a kind of frame around the whole party. It's a mandorla, the almond-shaped glory that surrounds holy figures in religious art.

That's no accident. The critic Adolphe Basler compared this painting to the idealised figures of medieval frescoes, and to the ex-votos of the old "Primitive" masters. So here is an everyday wedding party, painted with the stillness of an altarpiece.

And down in front, a dog, taking up its place as solemnly as anyone.

Rousseau did not have long left after this. He died in 1910, of gangrene from an infected leg. The wedding party passed later into Paul Guillaume's collection, and from Domenica's to the state.
