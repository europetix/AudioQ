By the early eighteen-nineties, Renoir was around fifty years old. And his friends were annoyed. The French state had never bought a single one of his pictures.

Two of those friends decided to fix it. One was the poet Stéphane Mallarmé. The other was Roger Marx, a young official in the fine arts administration. Together they pushed for Impressionist painting to enter the national museums. After an informal commission from the state, Renoir took up this subject, two girls at a piano. And in eighteen ninety-two, one version was bought for the Musée du Luxembourg. Today that one hangs at the Musée d'Orsay.

But Renoir didn't paint it just once. He painted it several times. There are finished versions in the Metropolitan Museum in New York and in private hands. And there's this one.

This is the version Renoir kept. It spent a few years with his dealer during the First World War, but when he died, it was still in his studio. In nineteen twenty-eight, Paul Guillaume bought it.

So take a moment with the two girls and the piano, knowing that this is the version the painter held on to. The state got its official picture. Renoir kept one for himself.

Guillaume, it turns out, had an eye for exactly that kind of picture. Many of the collection's Renoirs stayed in the artist's studio until his death, and the dealer bought them from the family and the trade through the nineteen-twenties. The collection now holds twenty-five Renoirs, more than of any painter but Derain.
