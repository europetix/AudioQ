The music has stopped, or it hasn't started yet. A young woman sits with a violin resting on her knees, the bow still in her right hand. Nobody is playing, and for a moment the whole room holds its breath.

Look at the open violin case, lying on the table right by her elbow. Has she just taken the instrument out, or is she about to put it away? Matisse doesn't tell us. The bow simply waits.

Behind her stands a Moorish screen covered in a pattern of blue circles. It's a small picture, just over half a metre tall, from nineteen twenty-one or twenty-two, and a typical interior of his Nice years. Matisse painted the scene more than once. A very similar version, with the same title and only a little smaller, hangs today in Baltimore.

Why so many instruments in these rooms? In Nice, music played an important role in the painter's daily life, and instruments keep turning up in the pictures he made there.

Paul Guillaume clearly liked them, since he owned several paintings on the theme of music. The Woman with a Mandolin, Femme à la mandoline, is another. The museum's notes on that picture talk about windows, which Matisse used again and again, to open up a space, or to set a painting inside a painting.

Two women, two instruments, and in neither picture can you hear a note. Matisse makes you listen with your eyes.

Now find his odalisques, the women reclining among patterned fabrics and screens, and listen to them there.
