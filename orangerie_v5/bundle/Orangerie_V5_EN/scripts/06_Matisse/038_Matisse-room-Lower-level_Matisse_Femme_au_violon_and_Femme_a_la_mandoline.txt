Somewhere in Baltimore hangs a painting very like this one. Same title, same idea, only a little smaller. Which came first, and why paint it twice? The museum leaves that question open, and so will I.

It's a small picture, just over half a metre tall, from nineteen twenty-one or twenty-two. And it's a typical interior of his Nice years. A young woman sits with a violin resting on her knees, the bow in her right hand. Behind her stands a Moorish screen covered in a pattern of blue circles.

Look for the violin case, lying open on the table, right where her elbow rests. Has she just taken the instrument out, or is she about to put it away? Matisse doesn't tell us, and the bow is still. The music has stopped, or not yet begun.

Why so many instruments in these rooms? The museum's answer is simple. In Nice, music played an important role in the painter's daily life. Instruments keep turning up in the pictures he made there.

Paul Guillaume clearly liked them, since he owned several paintings on the theme of music. The Woman with a Mandolin, Femme à la mandoline, is another, and its paper trail is unusually clear. Matisse sold it to the dealers Bernheim-Jeune in January nineteen twenty-three, and from them it made its way to Guillaume.

The museum's notes on that picture are all about windows. Matisse used them again and again, to open up a space, or to set a painting inside a painting. So when you find it, look past the woman and her instrument. Notice how he handles the space around her.

Two women, two instruments, and in neither picture can you hear a note. Matisse makes you listen with your eyes.

Now find his odalisques, the women reclining among patterned fabrics and screens. Play their track there.
