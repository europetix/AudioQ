In nineteen eighteen, Paul Guillaume became the first dealer to give an exhibition to Matisse and Picasso together. He hung them in his gallery face to face, one against the other, and let the visitors compare.

Seven years later, Picasso painted this woman. And she carries on that conversation all by herself.

She lies back on cushions, propped up on one elbow, a tambourine in her hand. She's languid, at ease, and very much on display. If you've seen Matisse's odalisques, you'll recognise her. The museum makes the link itself. She recalls the odalisques Matisse was painting at exactly that time.

But Picasso being Picasso, he couldn't simply agree. After his classical giants, he went back for a while to a decorative kind of Cubism. Look at how she's built. Different viewpoints are laid over one another, so you see parts of her from more than one side at once. And then there are the colours, mauve, yellow, garnet red and turquoise, which the museum calls almost jarring. Matisse makes pattern feel like comfort, and Picasso makes it crackle.

Now find the Large Still Life, painted eight years earlier. A compote dish, a bottle and a glass, each seen from several angles. Look at the table, where the beige top tilts up until it's floating almost upright, and everything on it seems to balance by a miracle. After Guillaume's death, Domenica sold many of the bolder Picassos. The museum singles out this still life as the Cubist work she chose to keep.

Guillaume bought this woman with the tambourine in nineteen twenty-seven. In fact he bought most of the Picassos in this collection himself, in the last years before his early death. He was never Picasso's official dealer. He simply wanted them.
