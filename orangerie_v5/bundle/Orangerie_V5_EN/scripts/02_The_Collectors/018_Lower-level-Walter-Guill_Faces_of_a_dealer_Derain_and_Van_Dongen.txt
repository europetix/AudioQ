Paul Guillaume spent his life selling pictures. He also seems to have enjoyed being in them.

Start with Derain's portrait, painted in nineteen nineteen. Guillaume sits in three-quarter view, very elegant, and looks away from us. The museum calls him impassive and detached. Everything is cool and blue, down to the background, laid in with horizontal strokes of blue. The suit is blue, the bow tie is blue, and so are his eyes. Blue, we're told, was his favourite colour. And the likeness isn't even strong, because Derain has pulled the features slightly out of shape.

One thing breaks the chill. In his left hand, a cigarette has burned almost down to nothing. The museum calls it the only relaxed thing in the painting. His other arm leans on an open book.

Van Dongen painted him again, around nineteen thirty, in a soft harmony of grey and blue. It may have been commissioned soon after Guillaume received the Legion of Honour. The young man of the garage window had become an establishment figure. If it isn't on the wall, it may be travelling again.

Now his wife. Derain painted Domenica twice, and this is the second, from about ten years after his portrait of Paul. She's a woman of the world, in an elegant hat and a draped stole. Her face is carefully made up and her expression severe, while small touches of white make her eyes seem to look straight at you. Behind her hangs a red curtain.

Look to the right of her, though, where you can faintly make out the sketch of another painting. It's Derain's Harlequin and Pierrot, Arlequin et Pierrot. And the museum recognises the white-clad Pierrot as a portrait of Paul Guillaume himself. So even here, the husband is in the picture. This portrait had pride of place in his own room, on the avenue du Bois.
