The collection around you carries two men's names. But the woman who shaped it was neither of them.

She was born Juliette Lacaze, and the world knew her as Domenica. She was seven years younger than Paul Guillaume, and thirty-six when he died. Later she married again, to the architect Jean Walter, and outlived him too.

What she inherited was a dealer's collection, full of bold and difficult work. She did not keep it that way. In the museum's own words, she transformed and reduced it. She parted with many of the most daring works of the period. And she bought what she loved. She liked Cézanne, for one, and added his landscapes.

Then came the deal with France. In nineteen fifty-nine and again in nineteen sixty-three, the state bought the collection in two parts, a hundred and forty-six paintings in all. A public appeal by the Friends of the Louvre helped pay for the most important works. And Domenica kept the right to live with her pictures for the rest of her life.

She made one request. The collection would carry the names of both her husbands, Jean Walter and Paul Guillaume. So the dealer's dream of a museum came true, in his widow's hands, and under a double name. It has been on permanent display here since nineteen eighty-four.

You'll meet Domenica herself shortly, painted by Derain under a very large hat. First, her first husband at twenty-three. Look for Modigliani's portrait of a young man in a dark suit and hat, against a red background, and play its track there.
