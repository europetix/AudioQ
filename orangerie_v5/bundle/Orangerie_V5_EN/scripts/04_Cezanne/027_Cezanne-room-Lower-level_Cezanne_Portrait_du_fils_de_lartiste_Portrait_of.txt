Here's a small coincidence to start with: the boy in this portrait is called Paul. So was his father, Paul Cézanne. And so was the man who bought the painting in the nineteen-twenties, the dealer Paul Guillaume. Three Pauls, one canvas.

The boy is Cézanne's son, born to Hortense in 1872. The picture dates from around 1880, so he is still a child here. Cézanne painted him more than once in those years. The museum calls this one the most ambitious and the most accomplished of them all.

At first it hardly looks ambitious, just a small head and shoulders, seen close up. Then the decisions start to show. Young Paul seems to be perched on the arm of an armchair. But Cézanne paints only the back of the chair, off to the right, against a plain blue-green ground. The framing is so tight that it crops the boy, and it crops the chair, the way a camera might. The museum says it's reminiscent of photography.

Now follow the curves: the rounded back of the chair, the slope of the shoulders, then the neck, the oval of the face and the cap of hair. Each one answers the next, like lines in a rhyme. Cézanne simplifies every form and gives it a firm outline. The museum notes that this recalls the work of Gauguin.

What's missing matters as much as what's there. There are no toys, no room, no little story about childhood, just a boy, part of a chair, and a colour behind them.

You might expect a father to soften a portrait of his son. Cézanne does something rarer, and looks at the boy the way he looked at everything, as a problem worth solving properly. And somehow that seriousness feels like a form of love.

Now look for a very different subject: a landscape where an orange rock rises behind a patch of trees, under blue sky. That's Le Rocher rouge. Play its track there.
