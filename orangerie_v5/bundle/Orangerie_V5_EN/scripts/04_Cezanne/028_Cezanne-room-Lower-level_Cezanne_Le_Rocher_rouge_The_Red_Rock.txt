Near the end of his life, Cézanne kept going back to the same unpromising place. It wasn't a cathedral or a famous view, but a deserted quarry called Bibémus, near Aix, where his family's house stood. For about ten years from 1895, he painted its rocks again and again.

This canvas is one of those visits, from around 1895. It's about ninety centimetres tall and sixty-six wide. Domenica bought it after Paul Guillaume's death, and the museum calls it one of the masterpieces of Cézanne's maturity.

Look at how little he needs. A patch of trees, painted in short strokes laid across each other, stands out against the orange rock, and above it all there's a band of blue sky. Green, orange and blue are the whole subject.

And yet it doesn't feel empty. Get close, and the trees turn into a weave of separate strokes. Step back, and they knit together again into a solid mass against the rock.

Young painters noticed. Cézanne died in 1906, and within two years, Picasso and Braque had begun the experiments that became Cubism. And four years after his death, the poet Guillaume Apollinaire put it in a single sentence. "It is known that most new painters lay claim to this sincere and selfless artist."

Sincere and selfless are not the words you usually hear about a revolutionary. But stand here long enough, and they fit. There's no showing off in this picture, just a man in a quarry, trying, very patiently, to get it right.
