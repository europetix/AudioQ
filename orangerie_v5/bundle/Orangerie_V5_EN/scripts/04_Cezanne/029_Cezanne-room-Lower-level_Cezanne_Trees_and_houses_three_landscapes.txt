If you want to watch a painter change his mind about the world, a wall of landscapes is a good place to do it. These three span roughly a quarter of a century of Cézanne's life. And they don't look like the work of the same eye.

Start with the earliest, the upright one. Paysage au toit rouge, also called Le Pin à l'Estaque, The Pine at L'Estaque. He painted it during a stay at L'Estaque in 1876, and the museum sees the influence of Camille Pissarro in it. You can find that influence in the tall format, in the tree trunk right up front, and in the low wall. Follow that leaning trunk and its branches. They lead your eye, quite deliberately, into the trees at the centre. This is a painter still using the old tools of a landscape to guide you in.

Now the second, Arbres et maisons, Trees and Houses, from around 1885. A little hamlet sits among two patches of leafless trees, near Le Jas de Bouffan, the Cézanne family house outside Aix. Dark trunks stand against very pale, almost schematic houses. And here something gives way. The planes blur into one another, and traditional perspective starts to dissolve. Where exactly is that house? In front of the tree, or behind it? Cézanne painted this view three times. The other two are in New York, at the Metropolitan Museum, and in Oslo.

Then the latest, Dans le parc de Château Noir, In the Park of Château Noir, painted around the turn of the century, a few years before his death. I won't tell you what to see in this one. Just notice how differently the trees are made from the pine at L'Estaque, and decide for yourself how far he has travelled.

The Pine at L'Estaque came into the collection through Domenica, after Paul Guillaume's death. She went on buying Cézannes after he died, the museum says, notably landscapes like this one.
