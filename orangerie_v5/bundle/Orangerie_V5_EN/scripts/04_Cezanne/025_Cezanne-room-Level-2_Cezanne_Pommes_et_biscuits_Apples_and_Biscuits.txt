In May 1952, a painting of apples caused a sensation in a Paris saleroom. The sale was at the Galerie Charpentier. The buyer was Domenica Walter, Paul Guillaume's widow, and she paid an unprecedented sum. The effect was instant. The purchase turned the public's attention to her, and to the magnificent collection she had been building.

That collection holds fifteen Cézannes. In the nineteen-twenties, Paul bought several major ones, among them portraits of the painter's wife and son. After his death, Domenica kept buying, landscapes above all. And then this still life, the most public purchase of them all.

She also bought one painting twice, in a way. Paul had acquired a canvas of flowers and fruit from the dealer Ambroise Vollard. Years after his death, Domenica bought a picture of flowers in a blue vase. Neither of them knew they were two halves of the same unfinished canvas, probably cut apart by a dealer long before. A curator, Michel Hoog, pieced the story together in 1992. Husband and wife had reunited a painting without ever meaning to.

Now the apples. The museum counts this among the symbols of Cézanne's great mastery of still life, and part of the reason is how little there is. A plate and a few apples, all set on a chest. The roundness of each apple is built out of colour. His experiments in expressing volume through colour are fully developed here. Give it a moment, and the whole arrangement settles, like a scale that has just stopped moving.

So this small group of fruit carries two stories at once. A painter building an apple out of colour, and a widow whose purchase made the public look at her whole collection.

Now find Madame Cézanne, the painter's wife, painted full length in a garden, and listen to her story there.
