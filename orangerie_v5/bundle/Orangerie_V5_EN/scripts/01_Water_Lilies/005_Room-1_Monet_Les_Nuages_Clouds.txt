The clouds in this painting aren't in the sky. They're lying on the surface of a pond.

That's the whole trick of this room. Monet points you downward, at the water, and the sky arrives as a reflection. In the museum's own description, this room moves from clouds on the water's surface to reflections where the water lilies emerge. Les Nuages, Clouds, sits at the airy end of that journey.

It's also one of the two longest paintings in this room. Two metres high and twelve and three-quarter metres long, exactly the same length as Morning, its partner on the Seine side.

Monet had been looking at this pond for a long time before he started. In 1909 the Durand-Ruel gallery showed forty-eight of his water-lily paintings. They were easel paintings, made to hang on an ordinary wall. So when you stand here, the subject isn't the new thing. What's new is the size, and the fact that it doesn't stop at a frame. It wraps around the room, and you stand in the middle of it.

He designed this cycle from around 1915 until he died, which means a painting like this was the work of years, not an afternoon at the water's edge.

Here's a way to look at it. Start at one end, and walk slowly along it to the other, without stopping, the way you'd stroll along the edge of a pond. Notice how the reflected sky changes as you go. Then turn round, and walk back.
