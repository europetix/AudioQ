The clouds in this painting aren't in the sky. They're lying on the surface of a pond.

That's the whole trick of this room. Monet points you downward, at the water, and the sky arrives as a reflection. The room moves from clouds on the water's surface to reflections where the water lilies emerge. Les Nuages, Clouds, sits at the airy end of that journey.

It's one of the two longest paintings in this room, twelve and three-quarter metres from end to end. Its partner in length is Morning, on the Seine side.

Monet had been looking at this pond for a long time before he started. In 1909 the Durand-Ruel gallery showed forty-eight of his water-lily paintings. They were easel paintings, made to hang on an ordinary wall, each one a window onto the pond. So when you stand here, the subject isn't the new thing. What's new is the size, and the fact that it doesn't stop at a frame. It wraps around the room, and you stand inside it.

A painting like this was the work of years, not an afternoon at the water's edge.

Here's a way to look at it. Start at one end, and walk slowly along it to the other, without stopping, the way you'd stroll along the edge of a pond. Watch how the reflected sky changes as you go. Then turn round, and walk back. The second time, you'll see things the first walk missed.
