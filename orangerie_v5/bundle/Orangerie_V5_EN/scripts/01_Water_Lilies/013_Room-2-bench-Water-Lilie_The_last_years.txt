In January 1923, a man of eighty-two went into surgery on his right eye. By then that eye could make out little more than light. The left one saw about a tenth of what it should. The patient was Claude Monet. The man who had urged him to go through with it was Georges Clemenceau.

His cataracts had been found more than ten years before. Now the surgeon, Dr Charles Coutela, operated on the right eye. It did not bring the world straight back. Monet complained that colours looked wrong, too yellow, or too blue. He is said to have destroyed some of his own canvases in these years. Only with new corrective lenses, over the next two years or so, did his sight partly return.

Think about what that means for the walls around you. Some of this colour was laid down by a painter who could not fully trust his own eyes. And he kept on painting anyway.

He also kept the panels. The deed of gift was signed. In Paris, the architect was turning the old orangery into two oval rooms. And at Giverny, Monet went on retouching his canvases, and could not bring himself to let them go.

In the end, he didn't have to. He died at Giverny in December 1926, at eighty-six. Within two months, the canvases were being fixed to these walls.

So he never sat where you're sitting now. He never saw the light come down on the eight compositions, joined into the shapes and spaces he had worked out so carefully. He planned every part of this experience, and then left it for other people to have.
