[warmly] On the other side of this wall, out of sight, is the Tuileries garden. This north side of the old orangery is the garden side, built almost without windows. So these painted willows have real trees for neighbours, just beyond the stone.

Monet's own trees were somewhere else entirely. His pond was at Giverny, about seventy-five kilometres from Paris. And he didn't paint this canvas on its bank. He painted it indoors, in his big glass-roofed studio. What you're seeing is a morning he knew by heart, made enormous.

Le Matin aux saules, Morning with Willows, is twelve and three-quarter metres long, three canvases joined edge to edge. [pause] It's the second of the three mornings in this cycle. You met the first in the other room. The third is right behind you.

[curious] Try a different way of looking this time. Go to one end of the wall and look along it, at an angle, the way you might look down a riverbank. The painting curves away from you with the wall, and the far end seems to drift off. Then walk to the middle of the room and face it square on. From the end, it's a long stretch of water. From the middle, it surrounds you.

[amused] It's a rare painting that asks you to look at it sideways. This one rewards it.

Look for the willows of the title as you go, and for the morning in the water around them. Don't hurry it. A morning this size takes a while to arrive.

Now turn around. The long wall facing this one holds the third morning, Clear Morning with Willows. Cross the room, and play its track there.
