[curious] Why would a painter hang two mornings in the same room, face to face?

Behind you, on the north wall, is Morning with Willows. In front of you, on the Seine side, is Le Matin clair aux saules, Clear Morning with Willows. They are exactly the same size, twelve and three-quarter metres wide. This one is made of three equal canvases, each about four and a quarter metres across. Same hour, same length, same willows. [pause] Only one word is different. Clair. Clear.

So here's a game this room is almost asking you to play. Stand in the middle and turn from one wall to the other. Give each a proper look before you decide anything. Which feels brighter to you? Which feels cooler, or nearer? Which would you rather wake up to?

[warmly] There's no right answer printed on a card somewhere. Set face to face like this, the two walls make every small difference count. Whatever you notice, you noticed it yourself.

Then try one more thing. Pick a single patch of this painting, about the size of a window, and stay with it for half a minute. Don't look away. Colours you didn't see at first may arrive late, the way your eyes adjust in a dim room. Now do the same with the morning behind you.

[lightly] If you're here with someone, play the game without comparing notes first. Then compare. You probably won't agree, and that's rather the point.

Two titles, one word apart. It sounds like a small thing. This whole room is built on small differences, and they're the best reason to slow down in here.

Now walk to the far end of the room, to the longest painting in the museum. That's The Two Willows. Play its track there.
