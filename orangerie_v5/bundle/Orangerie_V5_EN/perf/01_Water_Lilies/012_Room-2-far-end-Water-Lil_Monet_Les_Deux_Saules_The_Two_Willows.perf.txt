[quietly] You are now about as far from the traffic of the Place de la Concorde as this building lets you get. The entrance is back at the western end. Between you and the street lie an entrance hall, a whole oval room and a passage. The museum calls that entrance hall a transition from the outside world. By now, the outside world is a long way off.

And at the end of the walk, Monet put this. Les Deux Saules, The Two Willows. It's the longest composition in the museum, seventeen metres of painting on four joined canvases, each two metres high. [pause] Laid out flat, it would be longer than a city bus. Wrapped around the end of the room, it hardly feels long at all. It feels as if the room simply carries on.

[curious] Keep going in your mind, straight through this wall, and you'd be travelling east along the great line of Paris, toward the Louvre. These rooms sit on that axis. So the longest painting in the building closes its eastern end, and looks back down the whole length toward the Arc de Triomphe.

[warmly] Now find the trees. The title promises two willows, and nothing more. Walk the whole length, slowly, and look for them. When you think you have them, go back to the middle of the room and let the four canvases join up again into one picture.

[amused] Seventeen metres of painting, and the title mentions just two trees. Finding them is your job. It may take longer than you expect.

It's tempting to read weeping willows, painted in years of grief and war, as a kind of mourning. I won't claim Monet meant it that way. But standing here, it's hard not to think of it.

[lightly] You've done a lot of walking. There's one more story in this room, and it's better heard sitting down. Find a bench in the middle of the room and play its track there.
