[warmly] One December day in 1922, an American collector walked into Paul Guillaume's gallery in Paris, and a painting stopped him in his tracks. It showed a pastry cook. The collector was Albert Barnes. The painter was a young man from near Minsk named Chaïm Soutine, and few people outside Paris had heard of him.

Guillaume had. He'd found Soutine through an article by the critic Michel Georges-Michel, and he was one of the first to take his painting seriously. Now he took Barnes to Soutine's dealer, Léopold Zborowski. Barnes bought dozens of canvases. [pause] Through that one introduction, Soutine became internationally known.

[curious] And the pastry cook that started it all? It seems it went to America with Barnes. Guillaume never quite forgot it.

Soutine liked to work in series, and he painted this subject six times. In the early nineteen thirties, long after Barnes had made Soutine famous, Guillaume bought this version. The museum says he bought it "in memory of the first Pastry Cook that he had liked so much." [long pause] So what you're looking at is a kind of consolation prize. A dealer's keepsake of the one that got away.

Now look at the boy himself. The face is stretched long, the neck is fragile, and the features and limbs are pulled out of shape. That distortion is where the power comes from. His hat and jacket are white, touched with grey and green. And in his hands is a cloth of flat, vivid red, the hottest thing in the picture.

[amused] One more thing. The pose may come from a portrait in the Louvre, Jean Fouquet's Charles the Seventh, King of France. A kitchen boy, sitting like a king.

Close by, look for a hotel waiter in a red waistcoat with his hands on his hips. His story continues in front of him.
