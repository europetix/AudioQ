[curious] In a smart spa hotel, the staff are meant to be invisible. They carry the trays, open the doors, and nobody really looks at them. [pause] Soutine looked.

Between 1926 and 1928 he spent time at Châtel-Guyon, a spa town, where he met the collectors Madeleine and Marcellin Castaing. He seems to have watched the hotel staff closely there. And then he painted them, one after another, as a series of portraits. This is one of them, Le Garçon d'étage, the room-service waiter.

[wry] He is not exactly at your service. Look at the pose. Hands on hips, a little aggressive. The face is coarse, with a huge forehead and a narrow, pointed chin. His chest is squeezed into a tight uniform, his shoulders don't match, and his hands are misshapen.

Then follow the red. It's in his waistcoat, in the skin of his face, and in his ears, which glow. Around the time he painted this man, Soutine's obsession with red reached its peak, in his pictures of grooms and huntsmen. Here the uniform and the man seem made of the same hot colour.

[quietly] Now find the bride, La Fiancée. She's a few years earlier, probably painted at Cagnes in the south. The canvas is tall and narrow, and it closes tightly around her thin body and long face. Her white dress isn't white for long. It's spattered with long strokes of green, blue and yellow, against a background of green and brown.

A waiter in his red uniform, and a bride in hers. Soutine squeezes them both, and somehow makes them more alive for it.

Nearby hangs La Jeune Anglaise, the Young Englishwoman, painted in 1934, about a decade after the bride. See how she compares.

When you're ready, look for the still lifes of hanging meat, a side of beef and a rabbit, and listen to their track there.
