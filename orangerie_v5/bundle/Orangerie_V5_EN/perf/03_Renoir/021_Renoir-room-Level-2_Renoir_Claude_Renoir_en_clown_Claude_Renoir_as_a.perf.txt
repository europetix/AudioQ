[amused] Every portrait has a price. This one cost a box of paints and a train set.

The boy is Claude Renoir, the painter's son. The family called him Coco. In nineteen oh nine, when this was painted, he was about eight years old. And he did not want to be here.

[pause] The trouble was the costume. Coco hated the red clown suit. Above all, he hated the stockings, which prickled and tickled his legs. The portrait needed several sittings, and every sitting meant the stockings again. In the end, they struck a deal. Coco would put the stockings on, for a short time only. In return, he would get a box of oil paints and a train set.

[lightly] So when you look at this little clown, remember that he had been well paid to pose.

But Renoir wasn't painting a joke. He wanted a ceremonial portrait of his son, a picture in the grand manner. With it, he meant to measure himself against the old masters, and above all against Velázquez. That's quite an ambition for a picture of a boy in a costume he can't stand. Here, the ceremonial costume is a clown's red suit. And the young sitter has been promised a train.

[warmly] So look at it both ways at once. See the red costume as Renoir wanted it seen, a painter's challenge to the old masters. Then spare a thought for the stockings, the cause of all the trouble. A great painter's ambition and a small boy's bargain, in a single picture.

Renoir's older son, Jean, appears in this room too, as a toddler on his nanny's knee. Look for the small painting of a woman and a little boy playing with toy animals, and start its track there.
