[lightly] For most of its life, the side of the building you've just come out of was very nearly a blank wall.

The Orangerie was built in 1852, on the orders of Napoleon the Third, as a winter home for the orange trees of the Tuileries. All the glass went on the south side, facing the Seine. This north side, facing the garden, was left almost blind. Only recently was it given a way out, the exit you've just used and the footbridge under your feet.

[warmly] It took the end of a war, and an old painter's promise, to turn a greenhouse for orange trees into what you've just seen.

On the twelfth of November 1918, the day after the Armistice, Monet wrote to Georges Clemenceau. He was about to finish two decorative panels, he said, and he wanted to sign them on Victory Day and offer them to the State. [pause] Two panels. Clemenceau went to see him at Giverny and talked him into something far bigger. In the end it was eight compositions on twenty-two panels, and a building to hold them.

[quietly] Monet never saw them in place. He died in December 1926, and the rooms opened about five months later. The museum calls the gift a monument to peace. After an afternoon inside it, that seems about right.

Around you is the Tuileries garden. It goes back to Catherine de' Medici in the sixteenth century, and the great garden designer Le Nôtre reshaped it in the seventeenth. Wander it slowly if you can. After the Water Lilies, the real water and the real sky can look a little different.

[warmly] Thank you for spending this time with me, and with Paul Guillaume's painters, and with Monet. Whatever you remember of the faces downstairs, I hope you keep a little of that light. Enjoy the garden.
