[lightly] Before it held a single painting, this building was a winter coat for trees. In 1852, Napoleon the Third had it built here in the Tuileries garden, to shelter the garden's orange trees and other citrus through the cold months. That's what an orangery is. And the name stuck.

It was designed for the job. The long side facing the Seine is the south side, and it's glass. The garden side, to the north, is almost blind. Plants need light, and they need shelter, and the building gave them both.

[pause] Hold on to that idea of light, because it runs through this whole visit. About seventy years after the orange trees, the building was given a very different tenant. Claude Monet's Water Lilies, eight huge compositions wrapped around two oval rooms and lit from above. Monet died before they were hung. But the rooms were shaped to his wishes.

Here's the plan. First the Water Lilies, on this floor. Then the lower level, where a collection built by a Paris art dealer, and reshaped by his widow, is waiting, with Renoir, Cézanne, Matisse, Picasso, Modigliani and more. And if you have time at the end, a second look at the lilies.

[warmly] When you leave, it will be by that plain garden side, straight out into the Tuileries. For now, go in through the entrance at this end. Stop in the hall, just before the first oval room, and play its track there.
