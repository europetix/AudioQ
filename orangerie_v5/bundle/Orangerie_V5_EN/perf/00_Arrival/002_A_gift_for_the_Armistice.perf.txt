[warmly] The guns had been silent for just one day. On the twelfth of November, 1918, the day after the Armistice, an old painter sat down and wrote to Georges Clemenceau.

"I am on the verge of finishing two decorative panels," Claude Monet wrote, "which I want to sign on Victory Day, and I am writing to ask you to offer them to the State on my behalf."

Two panels. That was the whole gift. [pause] It didn't stay that size for long. Clemenceau came out to Giverny, and he persuaded Monet to widen it, from two panels to the whole set of large panels he'd been working on for years.

Finding them a home took time. A first plan, for a pavilion in the garden of what is now the Musée Rodin, was dropped. The choice fell instead on this old orangery. And in April 1922 the deed of gift was signed. Eight compositions, on twenty-two panels.

The architect Camille Lefèvre, who looked after the Louvre and the Tuileries, rebuilt the inside to Monet's wishes, as two oval rooms lit from above. Monet, meanwhile, couldn't let go. Back at Giverny, he kept reworking the panels, right to the end.

[quietly] He died there in December, at eighty-six. In the first months of the new year, the canvases were fixed to these walls, and on the seventeenth of May, 1927, the rooms opened. About five months too late for him. [pause] He never saw them in place.

Long before any of this, the critic Roger Marx had published Monet's dream of a room like these. What he wanted, Marx reported, was "the illusion of an endless whole, of a wave without horizon and without shore." The museum calls the finished gift a monument to peace.

[long pause] Two panels to sign on Victory Day. Step through and see what they became. Stand near the middle of the first oval room, and play its track there.
