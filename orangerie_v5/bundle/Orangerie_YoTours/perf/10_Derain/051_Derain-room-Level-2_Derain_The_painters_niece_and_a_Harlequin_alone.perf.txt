[warmly] In 1919, a baby came to live in André Derain's house, and she never left.

Her name was Geneviève. Her mother was Derain's sister-in-law, and that year the two of them moved in with him for good. Derain adored the child. He started painting her when she was nine months old. And he didn't stop. [pause] Over the years, Geneviève posed for more than a hundred of his paintings.

Think about that for a moment. Most children grow up with a few photographs. This one grew up in front of an easel, and her uncle painted her again and again.

There are two portraits of her in this collection, La Nièce du peintre and La Nièce du peintre assise, the Painter's Niece, and the Painter's Niece Seated. In one of them, which the museum calls one of his most exquisite portraits, she's shown life-size, at about twelve. She stands on her left leg, with her right knee bent up on a chair. The canvas is built from browns and yellows. Against them, her white dress and her face light up the whole picture.

[amused] Now turn to the Harlequin with a Guitar, Arlequin à la guitare, painted in 1924. He's life-size too, on a canvas almost two metres tall. But this sitter was a professional. The model was Salvado, the Spanish painter you may have met in Harlequin and Pierrot, this time playing one part instead of two.

[quietly] So here are two kinds of life-size. A hired man in a borrowed costume, playing a part. And a girl who simply grew up in her uncle's pictures, one sitting at a time.

When you're ready, look for Derain's big, dark still life of a kitchen table, and start its track there.
