Paul Guillaume helped send a great many paintings to America. This one, he went out and bought for himself.

Guillaume did a great deal to build the collection of Doctor Albert Barnes, in Philadelphia. And Matisse painted these three sisters more than once. Three other pictures of them, each time in different clothes and different poses, hang at the Barnes Foundation today. They very likely passed through Guillaume's gallery on their way across the Atlantic.

Then, in nineteen twenty-six, the collection of Auguste Pellerin came up for sale, and this canvas was in it. Guillaume hardly ever bought at public auctions. He bought this one, and the museum's reading is a gentle one. He did it, almost certainly, in memory of the sisters he had let go.

So what made this one worth keeping for himself? Look at how little the three of them agree. The three of them sit against a plain brown background. Two of them look straight out at you. The third has no interest in us at all, lost in her reading.

Matisse puts things together that shouldn't fit. Three different moods sit side by side, with colours that ought to clash and several levels of perspective at once. And somehow it all holds, like a family that argues and still sits down to dinner.

As for where the idea came from, look to Manet and to Japanese prints. And to an older group portrait in the Louvre, Les Dames de Gand, the Ladies of Ghent, then believed to be by Jacques-Louis David.

It's dated nineteen seventeen, the threshold of Matisse's Nice years. And those are the years Guillaume's widow, Domenica, chose to keep. Almost every other Matisse in this collection comes from them.

Find the hotel room with a big red curtain, a window full of sky, and two women on a divan. Its story is waiting there.
