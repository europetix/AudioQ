Before it held a single painting, this building was a winter coat for trees. In 1852, Napoleon the Third had it built here in the Tuileries garden, to shelter the garden's orange trees and other citrus through the cold months. That's what an orangery is. And the name stuck.

It was designed for the job. The long side facing the Seine is the south side, and it's glass. The garden side, to the north, is almost blind. Plants need light, and they need shelter, and the building gave them both.

Hold on to that idea of light, because it runs through this whole visit. About seventy years after the orange trees, the building was given a very different tenant. Claude Monet's Water Lilies, eight huge compositions wrapped around two oval rooms and lit from above. The rooms were shaped to his wishes, for paintings of water and sky.

Here's the plan. First the Water Lilies, on this floor. Take your time there, because that's the heart of the visit. Then the lower level, where a collection built by a Paris art dealer, and reshaped by his widow, is waiting, with Renoir, Cézanne, Matisse, Picasso, Modigliani and more. And if you have time at the end, a second look at the lilies.

For now, go in through the entrance at this end. Stop in the entrance hall, just before the first oval room. The story begins there.
