The light in this room comes from the sky. It falls through a glass roof and a pale stretched-fabric ceiling, and there's a little artificial light up there too. But mostly this is daylight, the way Monet wanted it. So what you see depends a little on the weather outside. A cloud passes over Paris, and the pond in front of you changes.

Around you are four of the eight compositions. Laid end to end, all eight would run to about ninety-one metres of painting. They're made of twenty-two panels, and every one is two metres high. The height never changes. Only the width does, cut to fit each stretch of curved wall.

The rooms themselves have a shape worth knowing. Two ovals, joined by a short passage. From above, the museum compares them to the sign for infinity. An endless loop, for a painter who dreamed of an endless whole.

This first room is about the sky and the plants, reflected in water, from morning to evening. So you're not looking across a pond. You're looking into its surface. Remember Monet's dream, a wave without horizon and without shore. Here it goes all the way around you.

Take a slow turn on the spot before you go up to any one painting. Let your eyes adjust to the light. Then begin with the smallest composition in the room, a single panel six metres wide. It's called Setting Sun, and its story continues in front of it.
