There's another painting hiding in this one. Look down, below the legs of these two young figures. You'll see faint wavy lines that don't belong to anything. They're the trace of a different composition, laid out sideways, that Picasso painted over and abandoned.

Now step back and take in what he kept. Two nude adolescents, modelled in pink and red on a ground of nearly the same colour. On one side, a young man crosses his arms above his head. On the other, a girl, or perhaps an androgynous youth, balances a pitcher on her head. They feel less like people than like sculpture, calm and solid and almost ancient.

Where did that colour come from? In nineteen oh six Picasso spent time in Gósol, a village in the Catalan Pyrenees, with his companion Fernande Olivier. The earth there had an unusual ochre tint, and it seeps into everything here. This is his pink classicism. It looks forward, oddly, to the classical giants he would paint fifteen years later, which you'll meet in this collection too.

But Picasso didn't stay calm for long. Find the smaller nude against a flat red background, painted later that same year. She has long hair, and her head is tilted as she holds a lock of it. Yet everything is hardening. The body turns into simplified, geometric blocks. And look at the eyes, two black slits without expression. The face has become a mask.

Within a few months, Picasso would paint Les Demoiselles d'Avignon, now in New York. The museum calls this red nude the first of a series that led up to it, a picture that already holds the seeds of that revolution. So between these two canvases, in a single year, you can watch him change direction.

Now find a standing nude combing her long black hair. The story continues with her.
