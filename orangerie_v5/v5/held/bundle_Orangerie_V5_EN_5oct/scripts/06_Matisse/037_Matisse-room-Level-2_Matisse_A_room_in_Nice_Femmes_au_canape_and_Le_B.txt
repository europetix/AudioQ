Here's a great painter who kept checking into the same hotel. From nineteen sixteen, Matisse often stayed at the Hôtel de la Méditerranée in Nice. He'd come south captivated by the light of the Midi. And the hotel itself became his subject.

This is one of those pictures. Femmes au canapé, Women on a Sofa, from nineteen twenty-one, shows his own hotel room.

But Matisse saw a room as more than furniture. In Nice he kept coming back to one idea. An interior that feels close and heavy, and a door or window that opens onto the free world outside. Inside and outside, shut in and let out.

Now look at how he builds that into a single picture. There are really two ways of looking here. One looks down at the floor, where the tiles give you pattern and surface, but almost no depth. The other looks right out through the window, far into the sky.

Follow the lines, and you'll find the composition makes a triangle that steers your eye toward one point out in the sky beyond the glass. The big red curtain on the right softens it, so the push isn't too hard. And there's no reflection on the window, nothing to stop your gaze on the way out.

The two women are almost an afterthought. They're over on the right, more or less sketched. The divan got more attention. Matisse repainted it to get the angle exactly right.

Then there's the colour, reds and greens, some warm, some cool, pulling against each other. The museum reads them as the painting's mixed feelings, the weight of the room and the wish to be out in that light.

If Le Boudoir is hanging near it, compare the two. Another room seen from the inside, another small world Matisse made out of an ordinary interior.

Look now for a young woman with a violin across her knees. Her story continues in front of her.
