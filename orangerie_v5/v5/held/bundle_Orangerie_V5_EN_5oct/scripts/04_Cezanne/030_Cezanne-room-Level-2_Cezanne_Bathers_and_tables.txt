One of the paintings in this room has been in three pieces. It's the long, narrow one, La Barque et les baigneurs, The Boat and the Bathers, about thirty centimetres high and well over a metre wide.

Cézanne painted it around 1890 as a decoration for the Paris apartment of the collector Victor Chocquet. It was meant to hang above a door, paired with another panel. According to the writer Georges Rivière, both were still unfinished when Chocquet died in 1891. At some point afterwards, the frieze was cut into three. Domenica gave two of the pieces to the state. The middle was still missing. In 1973, the national museums finally bought it, and the boat and its bathers were put back together.

Now the smallest picture here, Luncheon on the Grass, barely bigger than a book. Young people picnic near a village, and a church bell tower shows. In the centre a woman stands, and the church spire seems to carry her silhouette up into the sky. Cézanne may have been thinking of Poussin's bacchanals, which he copied at the Louvre, or of happy childhood memories around Aix. As for the title, it may have been added later. If it is his, it may be a sly nod to Manet's famous picnic.

Then the tables. In the still life with the straw-covered vase, look hard at the sugar bowl and the apples. You're seeing them from angles that can't all be true at once. Cézanne picks a coherent picture over a realistic one. There's even a slanting line nobody can quite name. A knife handle, perhaps.

In the still life with fruit, a napkin and a milk can, his usual props come back: the chest, the apples, the knife, the wallpaper. The greens are fainter and the lines more geometric, echoed by the pattern on the wall. That unusual viewing angle, the museum says, was a true innovation, even shocking in its day.

That's where we leave Cézanne. Now look for Henri Rousseau's paintings, starting with his wedding party, which may hang back in the large first hall of the collection.
