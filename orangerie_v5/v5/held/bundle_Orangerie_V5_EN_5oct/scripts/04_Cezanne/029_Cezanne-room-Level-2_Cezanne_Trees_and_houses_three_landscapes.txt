If you want to watch a painter change his mind about the world, a wall of landscapes is a good place to do it. These three span roughly a quarter of a century of Cézanne's life. And they don't look like the work of the same eye.

Start with the earliest, the upright one, Le Pin à l'Estaque, The Pine at L'Estaque. He painted it during a stay at L'Estaque in 1876, and it shows the influence of Camille Pissarro. You can find that influence in the tall format, in the tree trunk right up front, and in the low wall. Follow that leaning trunk and its branches. They lead your eye, quite deliberately, into the trees at the centre. This is a painter still using the old tools of a landscape to guide you in.

Now the second, Trees and Houses, from around 1885. A little hamlet sits among two patches of leafless trees, near Le Jas de Bouffan, the Cézanne family house outside Aix. Dark trunks stand against very pale, almost schematic houses. And here something gives way. The planes blur into one another, and traditional perspective starts to dissolve. Where exactly is that house? In front of the tree, or behind it? Cézanne painted this view three times. The other two are far away, in New York and in Oslo.

Then the latest, In the Park of Château Noir, painted around the turn of the century, a few years before his death. Just notice how differently it is made from the pine at L'Estaque, and decide for yourself how far he has travelled.

Three walks in the country, then. In the first, the painter takes your hand and leads you in. By the last, he trusts you to find your own way.

Next, look for a long, low frieze of a boat and bathers, and a tiny picnic scene, and the story continues in front of them.
