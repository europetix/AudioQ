Cézanne painted Hortense Fiquet some twenty-five times. That's a great many hours of sitting still while someone stares at you. And yet, standing in front of her, you may find it hard to say what she was like.

She was a young model when they met, and she lived with Cézanne, and in 1872 she gave birth to their son, Paul. They married only in 1886, fourteen years later. Paul Guillaume bought both of these portraits of her in the nineteen-twenties.

Start with the garden, Madame Cézanne au jardin, Madame Cézanne in the Garden, from around 1880. A full-length figure out of doors is unusual for him. He preferred to frame people close, in bare rooms, and here her face gives nothing away. Then look down to the bottom of the canvas, which is barely painted. A wide patch of white priming simply shows through, as if he stopped mid-sentence.

The other portrait is later. She sits in a plain armchair, hands in her lap, in a simple black dress. The pose is stiff and upright, almost ceremonial, as if she were posing for a statue of herself. Behind her, a blue-green background is brushed on so loosely that you can't tell what kind of room she's in. Her face is completely expressionless, and rather unflattering. This one is unfinished too.

It would be easy to read all this as a marriage gone cold. Maybe it was, but the museum offers another reading. With the people closest to him, Cézanne put his research first. He wasn't chasing a mood or a flattering likeness. He wanted his sitter to have monumentality and permanence, to sit on the canvas as solidly as the apples on his table.

So give her a minute, because the longer you look, the less she seems bored, and the more she seems simply there, unmovable, the way a hillside is there.

Their son is close by in this room too: a boy's head and shoulders, cropped close against a blue-green ground.
