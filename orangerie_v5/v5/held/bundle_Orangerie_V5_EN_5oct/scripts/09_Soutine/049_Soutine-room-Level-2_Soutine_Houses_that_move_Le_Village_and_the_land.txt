Most landscape painters hope the view will hold still. In Soutine's landscapes, nothing holds still at all.

It started with a deal. His dealer, Léopold Zborowski, sent him to Céret, in the south, with his room and board paid. In return, Soutine handed over his paintings. He stayed about three years. Then came Cagnes, and the village of La Basse Gaude, about eight kilometres away.

Look for Paysage avec personnage, Landscape with Figure. A road climbs straight up the picture. The houses in the middle distance lean to the left, as if a wind had caught them. That lean is a habit he picked up in Céret, and he kept it.

Now Le Village. It was painted in 1923, one of nine pictures he made at La Basse Gaude in barely two years. The museum calls it the most radical and the most unusual of all his landscapes. You can see why. Every house, roof and tree is twisted and wound into its neighbour. There's no proper perspective and no balance. You can't quite tell where one building ends and the next begins. And yet it still reads as a village, and it feels more alive than most villages that keep their walls straight.

Les Maisons, The Houses, is another of his landscapes here. See whether its houses manage to stand any straighter.

Soutine's later years were hard. During the German Occupation he lived in hiding. He died in 1943, at about fifty. Thanks to Paul Guillaume's early eye, this museum now holds the largest collection of his paintings in Europe.

From here, find André Derain's paintings, and look for two musicians in costume, Harlequin and Pierrot. The story continues in front of them.
