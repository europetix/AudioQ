Paul Guillaume ordered this painting from André Derain. And he ended up inside it.

Around 1924 Guillaume commissioned a picture from Derain of the two old clowns of the Italian comedy. Harlequin wears his chequered costume and two-cornered hat. Pierrot wears a loose white smock, a ruff at the neck and a small black skullcap. Each of them lifts one knee and plays a guitar. They seem caught in a dance that never ends, like marionettes or puppets.

Now look at Pierrot's face. The figure of Pierrot, the museum says, "was finally recognised as a portrait of Paul Guillaume." The man who paid for the painting is standing in it, dressed in white.

Here's the twist. The model who actually posed for both figures was a Spanish painter called Salvado, who worked for Derain and for Picasso too. So the body may be Salvado's. The face, it seems, became Guillaume's. Two men in one white costume.

And it's not a happy party. Look at their eyes. Harlequin and Pierrot play side by side, yet their gazes never meet. Their faces are serious. Against that plain, empty background, the whole picture is touched with a quiet melancholy.

If you saw Derain's portrait of Guillaume's wife Domenica in her large hat, there's a postscript. On the right of that portrait, behind her, you can just make out a faint sketch of this very painting. Her husband, as Pierrot, hovering in the background of her picture.

Guillaume died in 1934, at forty-two. He never got to grow old in that white suit.
