Paul Guillaume spent his life selling pictures. He also seems to have enjoyed being in them.

Start with Derain's portrait, painted in nineteen nineteen. Guillaume sits in three-quarter view, very elegant, and looks away from us. He seems impassive, detached. Everything is cool and blue, down to the background, laid in with horizontal strokes of blue. The suit is blue, the bow tie is blue, and so are his eyes. Blue was his favourite colour. And the likeness isn't even strong, because Derain has pulled the features slightly out of shape.

One thing breaks the chill. In his left hand, a cigarette has burned almost down to nothing. It's the only relaxed thing in the painting. And he leans on an open book.

Van Dongen painted him again, around nineteen thirty, in a soft harmony of grey and blue. It may have been commissioned soon after Guillaume received the Legion of Honour. The young man of the garage window had become an establishment figure.

Now his wife. Derain painted Domenica twice, and this is the second, from about ten years after his portrait of Paul. She's a woman of the world, in an elegant hat and a draped stole. Her face is carefully made up and her expression severe, while small touches of white make her eyes seem to look straight at you. Behind her hangs a red curtain. This portrait had pride of place in her husband's own room, on the avenue du Bois.

Look to the right of her, though, where you can faintly make out the sketch of another painting. It's Derain's Harlequin and Pierrot, Arlequin et Pierrot, a picture Guillaume himself commissioned. Keep that faint sketch in mind. When you reach Derain's paintings, you'll find out who is hiding in it.

That's the dealer and his wife. Now find Renoir's paintings, by a painter Guillaume greatly admired and traded in all through the twenties and thirties. Look for the upright canvas of two girls at a piano, and play its track there.
