[lightly] There's an aeroplane in this fishing scene. Rousseau painted Les Pêcheurs à la ligne, The Anglers, in 1908 or 1909, at the very dawn of flying. And there it is, sailing over a few people out with their rods.

Rousseau liked to paint ordinary people at leisure. Not heroes, not saints, just people with time on their hands. But he doesn't let them live in a timeless postcard. Behind them rises a factory chimney, and above them flies that machine. [pause] They're two small signs that this is the twentieth century, slipped into a scene that could otherwise belong to almost any decade.

[curious] Then there's the factory he chose to paint for its own sake. La Fabrique de chaises à Alfortville, The Chair Factory at Alfortville, from 1897. The museum's explanation for the subject is wonderfully simple: he chose it because it was so unusual. Who else would pick a chair factory as a subject?

Look at how big the building is. It's oversized compared with the landscape around it, and especially compared with the figures. They look like visitors in a world built for the factory.

[amused] And here's the bonus: the collection owns this factory twice. There's a small version too, only thirty-eight by forty-six centimetres, painted around the same time. If both are hanging, play spot the difference. In the small one, the landscape gets more room, and above all the sky. It's the same building, from around the same year, but in one, the factory is the hero. In the other, it shares the stage with the sky.

It's a quiet idea, and a modern one. The ordinary edge of the city, with its chimneys and workshops, is worth a painting.

Last in Rousseau's company, look for walkers in a park, and a cliff. Listen to them there.
