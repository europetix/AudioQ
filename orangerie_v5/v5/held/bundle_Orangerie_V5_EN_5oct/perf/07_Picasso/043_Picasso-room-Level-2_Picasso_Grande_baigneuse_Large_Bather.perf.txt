[amused] A few years earlier, this painter was breaking tables and bottles into Cubist fragments. And then, around nineteen twenty-one, he gives us this. A woman so massive she seems carved rather than painted.

She sits enormous on the canvas, with drapery about her, her limbs huge, her face heavy and solid. The proportions come from ancient sculpture, pushed until they're almost too much. It can feel monumental and slightly unsettling at the same time.

[curious] Why the turn? It's known as the return to order. Picasso looked back to the art of Greece and Rome, to Ingres, and to Renoir. And he wasn't alone. Derain, one of the best-represented painters in this collection, was part of the same movement. So if you've seen Renoir's late nudes in this collection, you've already met one of Picasso's sources, on this same floor.

Paul Guillaume bought this bather after nineteen thirty. [lightly] And Domenica kept it with great care, because, the museum says, it suited her taste perfectly.

Now find the Large Nude with Drapery. A seated woman pulls a cloth over her right shoulder with a hand that is far too big. Her head is bowed, her eyes closed, all in greys and pinks. Behind her stand the same ancestors, and one more, Raphael's Fornarina.

[pause] And then look for the two small studies of women at a fountain. That same year, from spring into autumn, Picasso stayed at Fontainebleau with his family, and worked toward a large painting of three women drawing water, now in New York. Compare the figure on the left. In the larger study, she carries her jug on her head. In the smaller one, she holds it by the handle, with a single finger.

[quietly] That second pose is the one Picasso kept. You're watching him make up his mind.

Now find the reclining woman with a tambourine, in mauve, yellow and turquoise. She closes the Picasso story.
