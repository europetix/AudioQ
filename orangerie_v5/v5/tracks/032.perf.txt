[amused] Every neighbourhood has a grocer who knows everyone. Rousseau's was Claude Junier. He and his wife ran a grocery near the painter's home, and every morning Junier went out to buy vegetables from the market gardeners. He also trained horses, and he was very proud of his white mare, Rosa.

In 1908, the Juniers most likely commissioned this picture. Junier holds the reins on the front seat. Beside him, the museum tells us, sits Rousseau himself. Behind them ride Madame Junier and two young relatives.

Now look at Rosa. [pause] She stands, as the museum puts it, curiously on the tips of her hooves. It lets Rousseau show off the shadows underneath her, and it makes her look suspended, like a dancer on points, a carthorse doing ballet.

Look at the passengers too. Apart from Junier, every one of them faces you square on, stiff and upright. The museum compares them to Byzantine icons. Count the dogs while you're at it: there are three, which is rare in Rousseau's work.

[conspiratorial] Here's the part I love. Rousseau worked from a photograph of the family, but he didn't simply copy it. He changed the size and position of the dogs, and he left out a tree. The painter Max Weber pointed out that one dog was far too big. Rousseau's answer was that the painting demanded it.

[pause] That reply tells you a great deal. It's tempting to call Rousseau naive, as if he couldn't help the way his pictures came out. But a man who edits a photograph, moves the dogs and drops a tree is making choices. He knew what the picture needed better than the camera did.

It's a big canvas, almost a metre high and well over a metre wide, for a portrait of the local grocer. [warmly] And the grocer's horse gets to be a dancer.

Next, look for Rousseau's portrait of a child holding a doll. That's L'Enfant à la poupée. Play its track there.
