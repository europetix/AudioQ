[quietly] In eighteen eighty-one, Renoir went to Italy. He was forty. After that journey, the way he drew began to change.

You can see the change in the seated bather by the water. Renoir painted her in eighteen eighty-three. She's a traditional figure in a timeless waterside setting, her brown hair pinned up. She sits on a white cloth and uses it to dry her leg. Look at her outline. The contours are firm, and the line is sure. The style is more confident, and it points straight ahead to his Large Bathers, Les Grandes Baigneuses, now in Philadelphia. Some historians think the model looks like Suzanne Valadon, whom Renoir often painted at the time. A maybe, nothing more.

[pause] About a dozen years later comes the bather with long hair. She steps out of the water and draws a white drape across her chest. Her loose golden hair echoes the movement of the plants behind her. This is Renoir's pearly period, named for the luminous skin tones. An almost identical bather hangs at the Barnes Foundation in Philadelphia.

[warmly] And then the long picture. The woman reclining here is Gabrielle, the nanny with the toy animals, by now one of his favourite models. In nineteen oh six, Renoir painted her as a nude indoors, reclining on a canvas more than a metre and a half wide.

That puts her in very old company. The reclining nude indoors runs through Titian, Rubens, Goya, Ingres and Manet. In Renoir's last decade, Titian and Rubens mattered most to him. Indoor nudes are rare in his work. In just a few years around this one, he painted three large horizontal figures reclining on cushions.

[quietly] Three nudes, then. A sharper line, a pearly glow, and the household's own nanny, laid out like a Venus.

To finish with Renoir, look for his smaller, quieter pictures: fruit, a bouquet of tulips, and a landscape under snow.
