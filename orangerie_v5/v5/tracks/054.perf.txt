[quietly] Nothing on these walls has changed since you first walked in. But you have.

You've met a pastry cook and a hotel waiter, a dealer dressed as Pierrot, a girl who grew up in her uncle's paintings. And here's something worth knowing as you look at the water again. Monet painted these panels between 1914 and 1926, and he kept reworking them until he died. So while Soutine was painting his pastry cook, and Derain his Harlequin and Pierrot, an old man at Giverny was still at work on these. [pause] The same years. A completely different idea of what a painting is for.

[long pause] Now the strangest part. For about forty years, the collection you've just seen hung directly above your head. In the nineteen sixties, an upper floor was built over these rooms to show the Walter-Guillaume paintings, and it cut the Water Lilies off from the daylight. It took the great renovation at the start of this century to take that floor away, give Monet back his sky, and move the collection down below ground.

So the light you're sitting in is a kind of homecoming. It comes in through the ceiling, and these rooms run east to west, with the course of the sun. Depending on the sky outside, the water can look quite different from an hour ago.

[reverent] Take a few minutes. There's nothing more to find here, only more to see.

When you're ready, follow the exit signs out to the garden side of the building. Once you're out on the footbridge, play the last stop there.
