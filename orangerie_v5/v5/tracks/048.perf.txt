[lightly] For a painter whose pictures can look this violent, Soutine had surprisingly old-fashioned heroes. Not the rebels of his own day. Rembrandt. Chardin. Two old masters who were not afraid to paint a dead animal.

Start with the beef, Bœuf et tête de veau. A side of beef hangs beside a calf's head, against a plain dark ground. Soutine painted it in his studio on the rue du Saint-Gothard in Paris. Behind it stands one picture above all. Rembrandt's Slaughtered Ox, painted in 1655, which hangs in the Louvre, a short walk along the river from here.

[pause] You may have heard stories about how Soutine painted his carcasses. They're told in many biographies, and they get more dramatic with every telling. We can't check them, so let's stay with what's on the canvas.

And what's on the canvas is colour. Broad strokes of red and yellow, so the bloody flesh almost glows against the dark. It's brutal, yes. [quietly] But look at it the way Soutine looked at his Rembrandt, and it's also strangely beautiful.

Now the rabbit, Le Lapin, from 1923 or 1924. It hangs by one leg and runs the whole height of a tall, narrow canvas. Unlike the plucked chickens he also painted, this one still has its fur, in every shade from brown to gold. Beside it, a red jug hangs by its handle, slightly lopsided.

This is where Chardin comes in. Soutine admired him so much that he copied his still life of a ray, La Raie. And this rabbit belongs to a whole series Soutine made of hares and rabbits, each one hung by the leg.

Nearby you may also find a turkey with tomatoes, Dindon et tomates, another of his still lifes of killed animals.

Then look for his landscapes, where whole villages seem to lean and sway. Play their track there.
