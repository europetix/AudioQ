[curious] Why would a painter hang two mornings in the same room, face to face?

Behind you, on the north wall, is Morning with Willows. In front of you is Le Matin clair aux saules, Clear Morning with Willows. They are exactly the same size, twelve and three-quarter metres wide. This one is made of three equal canvases, each about four and a quarter metres across. Same height, same length, same willows. [pause] Only one word is different. Clair. Clear.

That's the game this room invites you to play. The museum describes the second room as a set of contrasts, willow branches against the water. Facing each other across the floor, these two walls add one more contrast, morning against morning.

[warmly] So stand in the middle and turn from one wall to the other. Give each a proper look before you decide anything. Which feels brighter to you? Which feels cooler, or nearer? There's no right answer on a card somewhere. It's your eye against Monet's.

Your answer may even depend on the sky outside. The light in these rooms comes mostly from above, through the ceiling, with a little help from lamps. Monet wanted daylight to fall on these paintings. And daylight never holds still. When a cloud crosses over Paris, both mornings shift a little, together.

[lightly] If the colours look fresh, there's a reason for that too. During building works that ran until October 2025, the museum had the Water Lilies cleaned.

Two titles, one word apart. It sounds like a small difference. This whole room is made of small differences, and they're the reason to slow down in here.

Now walk to the far end of the room, to the longest painting in the museum. That's The Two Willows. Play its track there.
