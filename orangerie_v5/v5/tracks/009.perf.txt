[lightly] Here's something easy to miss. These paintings don't hang on the wall. They're part of it.

In the first months of 1927, the canvases were glued straight onto the curved walls of these rooms. The French word for it is marouflage. [pause] It's a choice with no easy way back. A painting on a hook can be lifted down and carried off. These would have to come off the wall itself. So Monet's gift was never just eight paintings and a building to keep them in. In the end, the two became one thing.

This composition shows how it was put together. Two canvases, each about two metres high and a little over four metres wide, joined into one picture eight and a half metres long. See if you can find the place where one canvas meets the other. Once you've found it, notice how little it matters. The picture simply carries on.

[curious] Now the title. Reflets d'arbres, Reflections of Trees. Take it as a fair warning. Monet isn't showing you trees standing on a bank. He's showing you what the pond does with them. And a pond is an odd sort of mirror. It turns a tree upside down, and the slightest breeze breaks it up. So if you find trunks and branches here, think of them as hanging down into the water, not growing up out of the ground.

[quietly] That's the shift in this second room. In the first room, the water held the sky. Here, the trees at the water's edge come into the picture, branches and all.

This is the shortest of the four compositions in here, and a good place to tune your eye before the long ones.

Walk to the long wall on your left as you face the far end of the room. That's Morning with Willows. Listen to it there.
