[lightly] Here's something easy to miss. These paintings don't hang on the wall. They're part of it.

In the first months of 1927, the canvases were glued straight onto the curved walls of these rooms. The French word for it is marouflage. This composition is two canvases, each about two metres high and a little over four metres wide. Together they make one picture, eight and a half metres long, cut to fit its own stretch of wall.

[pause] That choice had a consequence nobody could have planned for. In August 1944, during the Liberation of Paris, a shell struck the building. Some of the panels were damaged, and a series of photographs recorded the harm. A picture on an easel can be carried down to a cellar. A picture that is part of a wall goes through whatever the wall goes through.

The masonry was repaired, and so was the paint. From the late 1940s, people began to worry about the climate and the light in these rooms. Condition reports and careful treatments go on to this day. [quietly] So the calm you feel in here is looked after, quite deliberately.

Now the painting itself. Its title is Reflets d'arbres, Reflections of Trees, and it's a fair warning. Don't look for a riverbank, or for trunks standing on solid ground. The title tells you where the trees are. They're in the water, as reflections.

[warmly] Give it a minute. Let your eyes find the difference between the trees and the light around them. That contrast, branches against the brightness of the pond, is what the museum says this whole second room is about. Here it's on a modest scale. Along the long walls it opens right out.

Walk to the long wall on your left as you face the far end of the room. That's Morning with Willows. Play its track there.
