[warmly] The guns had been silent for just one day. On the twelfth of November, 1918, the day after the Armistice, an old painter sat down and wrote to Georges Clemenceau.

"I am on the verge of finishing two decorative panels," Claude Monet wrote, "which I want to sign on Victory Day, and I am writing to ask you to offer them to the State on my behalf."

Two panels. That was the whole gift. [pause] It didn't stay that size for long. Clemenceau came out to Giverny, and he persuaded Monet to widen it, from two panels to the whole set of large panels he'd been working on for years.

Then the gift needed a home. A first plan, for a pavilion in the garden of what is now the Musée Rodin, was dropped. The choice fell instead on this old orangery, the winter house for trees. In April 1922 the deed of gift was signed. Eight compositions, on twenty-two panels.

The architect Camille Lefèvre rebuilt the inside to Monet's wishes, as two oval rooms lit from above. And Monet had known for years what he wanted from a room like that. Back in 1909, the critic Roger Marx had published his dream of it. What Monet wanted, Marx reported, was "the illusion of an endless whole, of a wave without horizon and without shore."

[quietly] The rooms opened in May 1927. Between the deed and that day, something happened at Giverny that you should hear sitting down, in the second room. For now, just notice how far a promise can travel. The museum calls the finished gift a monument to peace.

[long pause] Two panels to sign on Victory Day. Step through and see what they became. Stand near the middle of the first oval room, and start its track there.
