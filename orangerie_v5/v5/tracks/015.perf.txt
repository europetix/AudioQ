[warmly] Paul Guillaume did not start out in a gallery. Around nineteen ten, he was a young man working in a car garage in Paris. In the window, he put a few African sculptures. And one day a poet stopped to look.

The poet was Guillaume Apollinaire. He noticed the sculptures. Then he noticed the young man who had chosen them. With Apollinaire opening doors, Guillaume moved fast. In February nineteen fourteen he opened his own gallery, on the rue de Miromesnil. He was twenty-two.

What followed reads like a roll call of modern art. Through the poet Max Jacob, he met Modigliani, and rented him a studio in Montmartre. Through Max Jacob again, on Apollinaire's advice, he met Derain. In his gallery he staged the first exhibition to set Matisse and Picasso face to face. He published his own magazine, Les Arts à Paris. And he helped the American collector Albert Barnes build his famous collection, now in Philadelphia.

[pause] He had a bigger plan, too. His dream, as the museum puts it, was to create the first French museum of modern art.

[quietly] He never got the chance. Paul Guillaume died in nineteen thirty-four, at only forty-two.

So look around this hall with him in mind. The collection opens with big pictures by what the museum calls the modern primitives, among them Picasso, Rousseau, Derain, Modigliani and Matisse. It's hung that way on purpose, to follow Apollinaire's vision of his own time. Somewhere on this level you may also find African and Oceanian sculptures from Guillaume's own collection, on deposit from the Quai Branly museum. The objects in that garage window, in a sense, have caught up with him.

The rooms down here are each given to one artist, and every stop in this guide stands on its own, so take them in whatever order the crowds allow.

[warmly] As for the museum he dreamed of, it does exist, in a way. Just not quite as he planned it. That part of the story belongs to his widow. Stay in this hall, and play her track right here.
