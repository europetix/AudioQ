[amused] Monet planned a great deal about these rooms. The shape of the walls. The spaces between the paintings. The rhythm as you move from one to the next. And, according to the museum, one thing more. He wanted your visit to be unguided, with several ways in and no set path. [pause] So please treat what follows as a suggestion.

He'd been working toward a room like this for years. In 1909 he showed forty-eight paintings of his water-lily pond at the Durand-Ruel gallery in Paris. Those were easel pictures, each made to hang on its own. Here the idea turned into architecture. All eight compositions in these two rooms share the same height, two metres. But each has its own width, so it fits its own stretch of curved wall.

Le Matin aux saules, Morning with Willows, fills this long north wall. It's twelve and three-quarter metres across, made of three panels. That's too long to take in from one spot. I suspect that was the point.

[warmly] So walk it. Start at one end and go slowly to the other, a few steps back from the surface. Look for the willows of the title, and for the morning in the water around them. Then go back to the middle of the room and take it in whole. Close up, you're at the water's edge. From the middle, you're inside the morning.

The museum points out that these rooms run east to west, in step with the course of the sun. Morning in this room is not a moment you glance at. It's a place you walk through.

Now turn around. The long wall facing this one holds a second morning, Clear Morning with Willows. Cross the room, and play its track there.
