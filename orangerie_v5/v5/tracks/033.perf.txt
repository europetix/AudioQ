[curious] In 1908, Pablo Picasso was rummaging through a junk shop in Paris, run by a dealer known as Père Soulié. Among the odds and ends he found a portrait of a woman by Henri Rousseau. He paid five francs for it, and he kept it for the rest of his life. Today it belongs to the Musée Picasso.

That little purchase set off something much bigger. In November of that year, Picasso and his friends threw a banquet in Rousseau's honour at the Bateau-Lavoir, a studio building in Montmartre. [pause] Picture it: Picasso's young circle, raising their glasses to a toll clerk turned painter, a man in his sixties.

So what did they see in him? This museum gives you a clue in the way it hangs the collection. True to the vision of the poet Apollinaire, it opens with large works by what it calls the modern "primitives": Picasso, Derain, Modigliani, Matisse, and Rousseau, right there among them. [lightly] In this building, primitive is a compliment.

Which brings us to this child.

[quietly] I'll be honest with you. The title tells us what we're looking at: a child, and a doll. It doesn't name the child, and I'm not going to invent a name. So for once, let's do the looking together, with questions rather than answers.

Where are the child's eyes, on the doll or on you? Think back to the passengers in Junier's cart, turned to face you like icons. Does this child do the same? Look at the hands, and at how much space the doll takes up between them. And look at the ground, and what lies behind. Is it a real place, or a backdrop?

There are no right answers here, only yours. But if a plain picture of a child with a toy makes you look twice, you've felt a little of what drew those young painters to that banquet.
