[quietly] You are now about as far from the traffic of the Place de la Concorde as this building lets you get. The entrance is back at the western end. Between you and the street lie an entrance hall, a whole oval room and a passage. The museum describes that first hall as a transition from the outside world.

And at the end of the walk, Monet put this. Les Deux Saules, The Two Willows. It's the longest composition in the museum, seventeen metres of painting on four joined canvases, each two metres high. [pause] Laid out flat, it would be longer than a city bus. Wrapped around the end of the room, it hardly feels long at all. It feels as if the room simply carries on.

Monet worked on this cycle from around 1915 until he died in 1926. Those were years of grief, war and failing eyes. Yet the subject he chose for the far end of his rooms was not a monument, and not a battlefield. It was two trees beside a pond.

[warmly] So find them. The title promises two willows, and it's worth walking the whole length to see how Monet sets their branches against the brightness of the water. That play of dark and light is what the museum says this room is about. Then come back to the middle and let the four canvases join up again into one.

It's tempting to read weeping willows, painted in those years, as a kind of mourning. I won't claim Monet meant it that way. But standing here, it's hard not to think of it.

[lightly] You've done a lot of walking. There's one more story in this room, and it's better heard sitting down. Find a bench in the middle of the room and play its track there.
