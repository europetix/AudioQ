[curious] For about forty years, the Water Lilies lost their sky.

In the late fifties and early sixties, the State acquired a great private collection of modern paintings. You're about to see it downstairs. It needed a home, and the Orangerie had the space. So in the early 1960s, the architect Olivier Lahalle built a new floor right on top of these oval rooms. The collection was hung up there, at the top of a monumental staircase. [pause] And the daylight Monet had planned to fall on his paintings stopped at the new floor.

For decades, the lilies lived under a false ceiling. A fine collection above, and a dimmer room below.

[warmly] Putting it right took six years. The museum closed in January 2000, and the architect Olivier Brochet took the upper floor away. Light came down through the roof again. The collection went the other way, into about a thousand square metres of new galleries dug out below ground. The rooms reopened in May 2006.

Digging down held a surprise. The builders found a stretch of old city wall from the sixteenth century, from the reign of King Charles the Ninth. Part of it can still be seen on the lower level, so keep an eye out as you go.

Before you leave, look up once more at that pale ceiling. That light is Monet's idea, given back.

Now head for the stairs or the lift down to the lower level. Play its track in the first hall there.
