[wry] In nineteen twenty-seven, some critics looked at paintings like these and saw a wallpaper dealer's display. It was meant as an insult. By accident, it comes close to describing what Matisse was after.

These are his odalisques, painted in Nice in the nineteen twenties. An odalisque is a woman of the harem, and Matisse drew on two earlier French painters of the subject, Ingres and Chassériau. But the museum puts it plainly. For him, the theme was a pretext. A reason to paint the nude.

And to do something more. He wanted the body, and even its shadow, to become part of the decoration around it. The pattern, the screen, the fabric and the woman, all lying flat together on the surface of the canvas. So the wallpaper was rather the point.

[curious] Start with the Blue Odalisque. The décor is cut down to a Moorish screen, and she's closed into a tight little space. One arm is raised, which lifts the curve of her chest. The whole thing is painted in blues, so thinly and fluidly that it brings watercolour to mind.

Now the Reclining Draped Nude. Here he strips the setting right back. A red curtain, a plain white sheet over the sofa, a cushion or two. Heavy black lines draw her outline. She lies in a pose Matisse loved, legs drawn up, arms raised above her head. [pause] Look at her right side. The pink-tinted folds of her body echo the folds of the cloth at her hips.

Then find the two odalisques named for their trousers, one red, one grey. Same theme, same game of body against pattern. See how each one plays it.

[quietly] Several of these came to Paul Guillaume in nineteen twenty-nine. And at the very moment Matisse was painting them, Picasso painted a languid reclining woman of his own. The museum itself makes the comparison. Keep these in mind when you meet her.

Find Picasso's paintings, and start with the pastel of a couple embracing in blue. Play its track there.
