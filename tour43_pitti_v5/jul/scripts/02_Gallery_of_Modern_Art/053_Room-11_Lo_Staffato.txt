Lo staffato is an Italian cavalry term, it means a rider whose foot has caught in the stirrup as he falls, so that he is dragged behind his horse instead of being thrown clear. It is one of the worst ways to die in warfare, and a soldier dragged by a panicked horse can be killed or mutilated within minutes. Fattori painted this in eighteen-eighty. He was fifty-five years old, the established master of the Macchiaioli school, and he had been painting the Italian Wars of Independence for twenty years. The war he is thinking about here is none of them specifically and all of them together, the long catastrophic series of nineteenth-century conflicts that produced the unified Italian state. Fattori had not himself fought. But several of his closest friends had. He spent his career painting their experience. Look at the composition. Fattori has put the horse in the centre-right, in full gallop, moving leftward across the canvas. The soldier's body is dragged behind, head thrown back, one foot still caught in the stirrup. The landscape is empty. No witnesses. No officers. No enemy. The horizon line is low. The sky is enormous. The cavalry soldier, a man with a name, a family, an Italian city he came from, is being killed in the middle of nowhere, by no one in particular, in absolute silence. Look at the trail of blood. A long dark line across the dry ground, the way a brush leaves a mark on a sheet of paper. Fattori has painted the trace with deliberate economy, almost a single gesture. The painting is not heroic. It is not patriotic. It is an argument against the rhetoric of war painted in the same decade the unified Italian state was building its first monumental war memorials. Fattori's version is what war actually looks like when you strip out the speeches and the flags. When you've looked enough, find the small panel nearby, twelve by thirty-five centimetres only, called the Rotonda di Palmieri. It looks like a sliver of seaside tent and a row of seated women.
