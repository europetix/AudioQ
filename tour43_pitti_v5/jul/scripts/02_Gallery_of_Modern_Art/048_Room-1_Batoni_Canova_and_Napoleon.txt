Walk slowly through this cluster. The atmosphere is restrained, calm light, smaller paintings, smaller sculptures, appropriate to the moment in European art it represents. Neoclassicism dominated late eighteenth-century European painting and sculpture as a deliberate reaction against the swirling exuberance of the Baroque and Rococo that preceded it. Artists looked back to Greek and Roman antiquity for models of clarity, restraint, and moral seriousness. Lines were clean. Compositions were balanced. Colours were cooled. On the walls, look for Pompeo Batoni's portraits. Batoni was the most fashionable portraitist in Rome in the seventeen sixties and seventies, and his studio was a near-mandatory stop for English aristocrats on the Grand Tour. He painted them in front of Roman ruins, holding fragments of antique sculpture, looking thoughtful and educated. His portraits are documents of how eighteenth-century European elites wanted to be seen, as inheritors of classical civilisation rather than as the regional nobles they actually were. In Room 2 you'll find the sculpture that gets its own track here: Antonio Canova's marble bust of Napoleon. You met Canova earlier in the Sala di Venere, where his Venus Italica stands at the centre of the room. Here he appears as a portraitist of the most powerful man in Europe.
