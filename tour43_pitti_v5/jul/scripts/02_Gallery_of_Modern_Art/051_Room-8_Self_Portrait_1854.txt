Lean in. It's a small canvas, and it gives back what you bring to it. Giovanni Fattori was twenty-eight when he painted this. He'd been born in Livorno on the Tuscan coast, the son of a working-class family, and had moved to Florence in his teens to study at the Accademia di Belle Arti. By eighteen fifty-four he was still a student painter, still working in the academic style, historical scenes, careful compositions, conventional finish. The self-portrait in front of you was made in that conventional manner. Tight brushwork. Restrained colour. A young man being serious about himself. But look at his eyes. There's something watchful in them, almost suspicious. Fattori would become, within a decade, the central figure of the most important Italian painting movement of the nineteenth century, the Macchiaioli, and he would do it by rejecting almost everything the Accademia di Belle Arti had taught him. He would start painting outdoors, directly from observation. He would use patches of pure colour, macchie, instead of careful tonal blending. He would paint Tuscan peasants, soldiers, landscapes, and small ordinary moments instead of grand historical themes. By the eighteen sixties, Fattori would be painting the Battle of Magenta, a major work elsewhere in these Macchiaioli rooms, in a style that anticipated Impressionism by ten years. By the eighteen eighties he would paint Lo Staffato and the Rotonda di Palmieri, two of the small masterpieces of the entire nineteenth century, both of which are also ahead of you. Look one more time at the face of the young man in this self-portrait. He doesn't yet know any of this. He's a student painter with a serious face, painting himself the way Italian painters had painted themselves for centuries.
