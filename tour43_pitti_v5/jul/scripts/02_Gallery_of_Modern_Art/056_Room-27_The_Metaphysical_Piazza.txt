Giorgio de Chirico was born in Greece in eighteen eighty-eight to an Italian engineering family, studied painting in Athens and Munich, and arrived in Italy in his twenties already in possession of one of the strangest visual imaginations in modern European art. Between nineteen-ten and nineteen-eighteen he painted a series of small canvases, Italian piazzas with no people in them, classical statues stranded in modern squares, long shadows pointing at nothing, light too hot and stillness too still, which he and his fellow painters Carrà and Morandi called pittura metafisica, metaphysical painting. The argument was that ordinary objects, when isolated and arranged in unfamiliar spatial relationships, could become unsettling, could open up a sense of the world as a fundamentally mysterious place. Not religious mystery. Not romantic mystery. A harder, blanker mystery. The mystery of a world that doesn't quite cohere. Look at this piazza. The arcades recede with the careful perspective of a Renaissance fresco, but the perspective doesn't quite match up. The vanishing points are slightly off. The shadows fall at different angles to different objects, as if there were two suns. The figures, if there are any, are tiny and without faces. The piazza is the kind of Italian square you've walked through a thousand times, but something is wrong with it. De Chirico's metaphysical paintings would directly influence the Surrealists in Paris in the nineteen twenties. André Breton called him a brother. Salvador Dalí said the metaphysical piazzas were the most important single influence on his own painting. By the time Dalí was famous, De Chirico had moved back to classicism and was repudiating his own metaphysical period, but the early paintings, like this one, kept their hold on twentieth-century imagination.
