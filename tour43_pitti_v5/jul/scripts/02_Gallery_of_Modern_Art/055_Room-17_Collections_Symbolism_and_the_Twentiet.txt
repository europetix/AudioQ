The Macchiaioli were the high point of Italian nineteenth-century painting. What follows fills the rest of the floor in three bands, and you can walk it briskly. First, rooms seventeen through twenty-one: the donated collections. These are the works that came to the gallery by gift and bequest rather than by ducal inheritance, society portraits by Boldini and De Nittis, the city's own municipal acquisitions of the nineteen tens and twenties, and several private collections that carry names like Ambron. It's a different kind of museum-making: the taste of individual Florentine collectors rather than the state. Then rooms twenty-two through twenty-six: the Italian schools and Symbolism of the end of the century. In the late eighteen eighties and nineties Italian painters split. Some pursued Symbolism, moody, half-mystical, literary paintings full of personifications and dreams. Others took up Divisionism, the Italian counterpart of French Pointillism, small dots and strokes of pure colour laid side by side to vibrate optically. Walk slowly and you'll see the two running in parallel, alongside study collections of works on paper. Finally, rooms twenty-seven through thirty: the early twentieth century, where Italian painting breaks into the modern moment. You'll find Giorgio Morandi, the Bolognese painter who spent his life painting the same bottles and jars on the same table and made out of that extreme restriction some of the most quietly moving paintings of the century, and, in the avant-garde rooms, a small Giorgio de Chirico, the founder of Metaphysical Painting, with his haunted empty piazzas and impossible shadows. The very last room gathers the works the gallery acquired from the Primaverile exhibition of nineteen twenty-two. If you have time, the Andito degli Angiolini, a mezzanine off this floor, holds the gallery's temporary exhibitions, which change through the year. One work in these final rooms gets its own track.
