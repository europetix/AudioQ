Fattori painted this in eighteen sixty-six, at the Bagni Palmieri, a seaside establishment on the Livornese coast where respectable middle-class women went to take the sea air. The 'rotunda' of the title is the circular wooden platform jutting out over the water. The yellow striped awning above it is for shade. Underneath the awning, in a row, sit seven women. The conventions of nineteenth-century seaside bathing meant that respectable women could not wear bathing costumes, they were entirely covered in long dresses, jackets, hats, and shawls, and what they actually did at the seaside was sit in the shade and breathe in the salt air. That's what's happening in this painting. Seven women on holiday, doing what their society permitted them to do. Fattori painted from memory and from preliminary studies in a sketchbook he carried with him. The figures are tiny, none of them larger than a fingernail, and yet each one is an individual. Some lean forward. Some lean back. One is in profile, head turned toward the sea. Another is in three-quarter view, talking to a neighbour. You can't see their faces in detail. But you can read each posture, each angle of head, each configuration of body in a chair. Look at the colour. The yellow of the awning, the pale Mediterranean blue, the soft greys of the women's clothing, the strip of beige coast on the left. The whole panel is built from broad strokes of pure colour, sitting side by side without blending. This is the macchia at its purest, and it's eight years before Monet's Impression, Sunrise. Fattori was thirty-eight years old. Italian critics have argued for a hundred and fifty years about which of Fattori's small panels is the greatest. This one is usually near the top of the list.
