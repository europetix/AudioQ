Look at the face first. Then we'll talk about the politics, because the politics are the thing. Canova carved this bust around eighteen-oh-five. Napoleon had just crowned himself Emperor of the French and was at the apex of his power across Europe. He wanted a portrait by the most famous sculptor on the continent, and Canova was that sculptor. The brief was clear: Napoleon was to be shown as a Roman emperor, bare-chested, idealised, as if a Greek god had been born in Corsica. Canova hated the assignment. He had spent thirty years arguing for the moral seriousness of neoclassical art, and the idea of carving a flattering portrait of the dictator who had stripped Italy of its art treasures was, personally, repugnant. The Pitti's Venus Italica which you saw earlier was Canova's replacement for the antique Medici Venus that Napoleon had taken to Paris. The two men had history. Canova made the bust anyway. He worked from life sittings in Paris, and what he saw wasn't a Roman god, it was a short, tight-faced thirty-six-year-old man with a pronounced cleft in his chin and the early signs of the weight Napoleon would put on by the time of his exile. Canova carved an idealised version. But look closely, the idealisation is restrained. The face has structure. The brows are heavy. The mouth is set. Whatever Canova's feelings, he made the face of a man, not a fantasy. Napoleon and Canova met in Paris during these sittings. By all accounts they argued. Napoleon at one point told Canova he should move to Paris and become his court sculptor. Canova refused. He returned to Rome. He spent his last years working diplomatically, and successfully, to get the looted Italian art returned after Waterloo. The bust came to the Pitti through the Habsburg-Lorraine collection after eighteen fifteen.
