Before you reach the famous grotto, here's its older, smaller predecessor, and it helps to see them in order. This is the Grotta di Madama, the oldest grotto in the gardens, designed by Niccolò Tribolo, the same architect who laid out the first plan of Boboli, and built in the fifteen fifties. It's small. A single low chamber set into the hillside, decorated with sponge stone, artificial stalactites, and sculpted goats and animals emerging from the rock. It may well be gated when you arrive, in which case you can look in through the grille. The question worth asking is why a sixteenth-century garden needed artificial caves at all. The answer goes back to ancient Rome. Roman villas had nymphaea, cool, shaded, water-filled grottoes where the family escaped the summer heat and where, in myth, the nymphs and minor gods of springs and woods were thought to live. Renaissance Florentines, rediscovering antiquity, wanted their own. A grotto was a cool retreat on a hot day, a piece of theatrical garden architecture, and a philosophical statement about the boundary between nature and human art, all at once. Tribolo's Grotta di Madama is the modest first attempt. Thirty years later, Buontalenti would take the same idea and turn it into the extraordinary three-chamber Grotta Grande just above you, bigger, stranger, far more ambitious. Seeing the small one first lets you watch the idea grow up. Climb the path to the Grotta Grande, the Buontalenti Grotto, marked by a deeply carved stone façade with statues in two niches.
