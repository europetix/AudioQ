Walk up to the rim of the basin and take it in from a few angles. Stoldo Lorenzi cast this Neptune in fifteen sixty-five for Cosimo the First, who had just become Grand Duke of Tuscany and wanted the gardens of his new palace to announce his maritime ambitions. Cosimo was building a navy. He wanted Florence to compete with Genoa and Venice as a Mediterranean power, and a bronze Neptune on the highest visible point of his gardens was political iconography as much as decoration. Florentines, though, have a habit of renaming great art with affectionate irreverence. The big marble Neptune in the Piazza della Signoria was nicknamed il Biancone, the big white one, because the city didn't think much of its proportions and said so. The Boboli Neptune got off more lightly: they called it the Fontana del Forcone, the Fountain of the Big Fork, after the prominent trident. By the late fifteen hundreds, il forcone was simply what everyone said. Look at Neptune's pose. He stands in contrapposto, weight on one leg, the other bent, with the trident raised high, the twist of his torso lifting the bronze against the line of the hill behind him. The muscle, the beard, the wave of the hair are all high Mannerist: elongated, slightly nervous, deeply polished. He isn't a calm classical god. He's restless, energetic, almost theatrical.
