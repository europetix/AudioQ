You're at the threshold of one of the most important formal gardens in Europe. Take it slowly. The gardens cover forty-five thousand square metres, and the central axis alone runs six hundred and fifty metres and rises sixty metres in height. Boboli was begun in fifteen forty-nine by Eleonora di Toledo, the Spanish-born wife of Cosimo the First, whose funeral dress you saw in the Costume Museum. The original design was by Niccolò Tribolo, who died before finishing it; the work passed to Ammannati and Buontalenti. The Boboli that emerged became the prototype for every Italian Baroque garden that followed, and through them, for Versailles itself. One honest note before you set off. The whole garden is mid-way through a long restoration, a fifty-million-euro plan called Boboli Twenty-Thirty, meant to return it to its Medici-era condition over several years. You'll see scaffolding, fenced-off fountains, and works-in-progress as you walk. It's a garden being actively healed, worth knowing rather than being surprised by. The architectural logic is the axis. Stand at the back of the courtyard and look out: a single sight line runs through the Artichoke Fountain in front of you, up through the amphitheatre, past the Neptune Fountain, all the way to the Statue of Abundance at the top of the hill. And one access restriction. The Buontalenti Grotto opens its interior only at eleven, one, three, and four in the afternoon. Time your visit if you want inside. Thirteen tracks for Boboli. First, walk to your right toward the Bacchus Gate, there's a fat little figure astride a turtle you shouldn't miss. Play the next track.
