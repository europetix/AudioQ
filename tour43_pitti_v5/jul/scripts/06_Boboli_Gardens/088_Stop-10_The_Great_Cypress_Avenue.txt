Walk slowly. The cypresses on either side are mostly the original trees from the sixteen thirties, four hundred years old, and together they make a green corridor that is one of the most photographed views in Florence. The Viottolone was laid out in sixteen thirty-seven, in the second great expansion of Boboli under Grand Duke Ferdinando the Second, the same patron of Pietro da Cortona's Planet Rooms. The plan was to extend the gardens dramatically southward, and this long cypress avenue was the spine of the new design. The trees were planted young, in matched pairs, in a perfectly straight line, with the intention that they would grow into the dark towering colonnade you walk through today. Along the avenue, statues. Most are ancient Roman pieces from the Medici collections, brought from the family's villas in Rome. Some are high-quality ancient marbles; some are seventeenth and eighteenth-century Florentine carvings. The mixing was deliberate, the Medici wanted the garden to feel like a continuous civilisation reaching back to antiquity. Don't worry about identifying them; many are unlabelled. What to notice is the light. The cypresses cast long shadows across the gravel, and in the late afternoon they make a dark grid you walk through. And look back over your shoulder now and then, the long view back up the hill toward the palace is one of the great Florentine vistas, and most visitors miss it entirely. Keep walking. At the bottom you'll reach the Isolotto, a circular pool with an island at its centre, ringed by lemon trees in pots and crowned by a colossal fountain. Play the next track when you arrive.
