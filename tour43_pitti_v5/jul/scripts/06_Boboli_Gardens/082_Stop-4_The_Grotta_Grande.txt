Look at the façade first. Two niches flank the entrance, holding statues of Apollo and Ceres, gods of light and harvest, of civilisation, framing the descent into something wilder. The carving, with its stone swags imitating flowing water and dripping rock, is the work of Bernardo Buontalenti, the most inventive theatrical and garden designer in Italy in the fifteen eighties. If you're inside, take the chambers in order. The first is the famous one. Walls and ceiling are worked to look like the inside of a wet cave, stalactites, sponge stone, embedded shells, and from the rough rock, half-emerging, are figures of shepherds and animals. Look into the four corners. Until nineteen twenty-four, those corners held Michelangelo's unfinished Prisoners, the great slave figures struggling to free themselves from raw marble, which he never completed for the tomb of Pope Julius the Second. Buontalenti installed the originals here, where they seemed to climb out of the artificial rock the same way they climb out of real stone. The originals are now in the Accademia, protected; what you see are fine casts. That placement was the whole argument of the grotto. The shepherds emerge from the rock the way the slaves do. The cave looks natural but is entirely made. The made rock holds real shells. Where does nature end and human art begin? Sixteenth-century Florentines thought hard about that question, and the grotto is their answer built in stone and water. The second and third chambers are smaller and darker. The third holds Giambologna's Bathing Venus from fifteen seventy-three, a small marble of a young woman stepping from her bath, surprised by satyrs climbing the basin to spy on her.
