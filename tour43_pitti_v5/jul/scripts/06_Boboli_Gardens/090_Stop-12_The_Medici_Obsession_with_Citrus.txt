Those lemon trees in pots you've seen all over the garden, around the Isolotto, on the terraces, live here for half the year. This is the Limonaia, the Lemon House, and the story behind it is one of the most characteristically Medici things in the whole garden. The Medici were obsessed with citrus. Not casually fond, obsessed. From the sixteenth century on, the family assembled one of the largest collections of citrus varieties in Europe: hundreds of kinds of lemons, oranges, citrons, and stranger things. Citrus was a status symbol, hard and expensive to keep alive through a Tuscan winter, exotic, and producing fruit, flowers, and scent all at once. A grand duke who could show you fruiting lemon trees in January was showing you his wealth and his mastery over nature. The strangest part of the collection were the bizzarrie, oddities. Grafted, deliberately deformed citrus, bred to produce knobbled, fingered, or fused fruit in bizarre shapes. The Medici prized them precisely because they were strange. The most famous, the Bizzarria, is a single tree producing lemon, orange, and citron all at once. Some of these ancient varieties survive here and almost nowhere else. The building went up in seventeen seventy-seven, by Zanobi del Rosso, the same architect as the Kaffeehaus, painted the same Lorraine green. It's long, low, and heated, with tall windows for the winter sun. Every autumn the potted trees are wheeled in out of the cold; every spring back out.
