Cosimo the First de' Medici was the first Medici grand duke of Tuscany, who consolidated the family's hold on Florence in the fifteen thirties and forties. Eleonora di Toledo was his Spanish-born wife, daughter of the Viceroy of Naples, mother of eleven children, and one of the most powerful women in sixteenth-century Italy. Don Garzia was their fifth son, born in fifteen forty-seven. Their deaths form one of the darkest chapters of Medici family history. In November of fifteen sixty-two, Eleonora, Don Garzia, and his older brother Cardinal Giovanni were travelling together in the Tuscan Maremma, the marshy coastal region, when all three caught malaria. Giovanni died on the twentieth of November. Don Garzia died on the twelfth of December. Eleonora died on the seventeenth of December, less than a month after her son. A persistent rumour at the time, and for centuries after, held that Cosimo had murdered Don Garzia in a rage for some unspecified crime, and that the grief had killed Eleonora. The rumour is almost certainly false. Modern medical analysis of the remains has confirmed malaria. But the rumour stuck. Their garments survived because they were buried in them. The Medici family vault in the Cappelle Medicee in San Lorenzo, where the three were interred, kept the silk and gold thread of their funeral clothes in tolerable condition for four hundred years. In the late twentieth century the garments were exhumed for study and restoration, and have been on permanent display here since the museum opened. Look at Eleonora's dress. The silk is heavy damask, the embroidery is in real gold thread, the cut is the high Renaissance Spanish style she had brought with her from Naples. Bronzino had painted her in this same kind of dress, different colour, different season, in his famous portrait now in the Uffizi. The dress you are looking at is the same dress as the painted dress.
