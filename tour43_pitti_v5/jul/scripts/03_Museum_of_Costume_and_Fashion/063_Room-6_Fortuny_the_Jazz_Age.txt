Room six is one of the highlights of the museum, and it turns on a single revolutionary dress. Around nineteen-oh-seven, a Spanish artist living in Venice, Mariano Fortuny, invented the Delphos: a column of finely pleated silk that hung straight from the shoulders to the floor, clinging to the body with no corset, no structure, nothing underneath. He named it for an ancient Greek bronze, the Charioteer of Delphi, and he guarded the secret of how he set the pleats so closely that it has never been fully reproduced. Worn by dancers and free spirits, the actress Eleonora Duse among them, the Delphos was scandalous and liberating at once, a dress you could actually move and breathe in. It points straight to the nineteen-twenties, also gathered here, when the female silhouette finally dropped the corset for the loose, straight, knee-skimming line of the flapper, and Coco Chanel made simplicity itself the height of luxury.
