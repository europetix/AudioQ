Two things to know before you go in. First, this building is itself a Florentine monument. Second, the museum reopened at the end of twenty twenty-three with a new arrangement after four years of renovation, so any older guidebook you have is out of date. The Palazzina della Meridiana, the small Palace of the Meridian, was begun in seventeen seventy-six for Grand Duke Pietro Leopoldo and finished in eighteen-forty. The building served, across its history, as a residence for the Lorraine, Savoy, and assorted regents. The meridian in the building's name is literal. Stop in the entrance vestibule and look down at the floor. A long thin bronze line runs across the marble, marked with metal points at intervals, the days of the year. Above your head the ceiling has a pinhole. At solar noon on any given day, a spot of sunlight falls through the pinhole and lands on the bronze line at exactly the right point for that day. The installation was the work of Vincenzo Viviani in sixteen ninety-nine, Galileo's last pupil, carrying his teacher's scientific legacy forward. Look up. The vestibule ceiling shows Gabbiani's Allegory of Time and the Arts from sixteen ninety-three, an appropriate fresco for a room with a meridian line. Time in the floor, time on the ceiling. Inside the museum, the collection of over fifteen thousand pieces is shown in rotation, with displays changing every two to three years. The permanent core is the sixteenth-century funeral garments of three Medici. One W-track on those. Play the next track when you find them in their dedicated case.
