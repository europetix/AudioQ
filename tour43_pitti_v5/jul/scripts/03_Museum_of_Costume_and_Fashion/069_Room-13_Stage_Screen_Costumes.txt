The last room turns from fashion to costume, the clothes made not to be worn in life but to be seen on stage and screen. The heart of it is the donation associated with Piero Tosi, the great Italian costume designer who dressed the films of Luchino Visconti and Federico Fellini, together with the holdings of the Tirelli house, which built costumes for opera, theatre, and cinema across the twentieth century. These are garments designed to carry under stage light and the camera's eye, to make a character real at fifty feet. Look closely and you'll see the difference from couture: the construction is bolder, the detail calibrated for distance, the whole thing a kind of wearable acting. It's a fitting place to end a fashion museum, with a reminder that all clothing is, in a sense, costume, a way of telling the world who we are. That's the Museum of Costume and Fashion. From here, head back into the main palace for the Imperial and Royal Apartments, the fourteen state rooms along the south wing.
