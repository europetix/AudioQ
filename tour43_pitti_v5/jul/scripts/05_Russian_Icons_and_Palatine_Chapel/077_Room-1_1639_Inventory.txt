These are the two oldest pieces in the museum. Both were already in the Pitti by sixteen thirty-nine, eighty-three years before there was a museum here to put them in. They're listed in the household inventory of Grand Duke Ferdinando the Second and his wife Vittoria della Rovere, kept in their Chapel of Relics. How they got to Florence in the first place is less certain than we'd like, most likely as diplomatic gifts, possibly through the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, possibly through Habsburg connections. The Beheading of Saint John the Baptist is straightforward in subject, the moment described in the Gospels, when Herod's executioner takes the saint's head, but look at how it's painted. The body is calm. The executioner is calm. There's no Western theatrical horror here. Orthodox icons deliberately strip out the drama because the saint, in the Orthodox understanding, is already in heaven; the event we're looking at is past, finished, sanctified. The other one, the Mother of God titled 'In You Let Every Creature Rejoice,' takes its name from a hymn, a few lines of liturgical poetry praising Mary as the meeting point of all creation. So the iconographer has painted exactly that: Mary in the centre, the Christ Child on her lap, and all around her every order of being, angels in the sky, saints, ordinary people, kneeling, looking up. The icon is the hymn given a form you can stand in front of. That's the important thing to know about looking at icons generally. They're not paintings of religious subjects. They're objects made for a specific kind of attention, closer to prayer than to viewing. The gold ground isn't a decorative choice; it's the light of the divine, and the figures inside that light are meant to be present in the room with you, not pictured at a distance. You don't have to share the theology to feel the difference. Stand in front of one for a full minute and you'll feel it.
