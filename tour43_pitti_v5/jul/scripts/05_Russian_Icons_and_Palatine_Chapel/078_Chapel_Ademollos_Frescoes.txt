Take a slow look around. The room is rectangular, with the altar at the far end, and every surface that isn't furniture is painted. The frescoes are by Luigi Ademollo, a Milan-born painter who spent most of his working life in Florence and is almost forgotten outside it. He painted this room in the eighteen twenties. Why this room is a chapel at all is part of the story. When Cosimo the Third and Marguerite Louise married in sixteen sixty-one, this was their summer apartment's grand reception hall, a secular room for entertaining. A century later, in seventeen sixty-six, the new Habsburg-Lorraine ruler Pietro Leopoldo, the Enlightenment reformer who later became Holy Roman Emperor, decided the palace needed a court chapel on the ground floor, accessible without climbing stairs. He consecrated this hall. Ademollo's frescoes came later, after Napoleon. What Ademollo does is combine neoclassical figure painting with the older Italian tradition of illusionistic architecture. Walls open into painted colonnades. Ceilings open into painted sky. Stand near the centre and tilt your head back, and the room becomes taller than it is. The proximity here is the museum's deliberate point. Orthodox icons next door, Catholic chapel here, two traditions of Christian devotional art under one roof, kept separate by history and connected by the same building. When you've taken it in, you've finished inside the palace. The last part of the tour is outdoors, the Boboli Gardens behind the palace, on a separate ticket.
