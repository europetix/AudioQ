This is a museum that didn't exist when most guidebooks of Pitti were written. The four rooms you're now walking into opened to the public in January of twenty twenty-two, never previously part of a Pitti visit, used as offices for decades. The restoration uncovered the seventeenth-century frescoes you see on the walls and ceilings, and the museum now hangs the icons in front of those frescoes, letting both speak at once. How did Russian icons end up in Florence? In sixteen sixty-one the future Grand Duke Cosimo the Third married Marguerite Louise d'Orléans of France, and these very rooms were decorated as their summer apartment. Two icons were already in the palace by then, kept in the household chapel by the previous Grand Duchess Vittoria della Rovere, you'll see those in a moment. After the Medici line died out in seventeen thirty-seven, the new Habsburg-Lorraine grand dukes who succeeded them kept adding to the collection. By the time it was catalogued, it ran to seventy-eight pieces, the oldest collection of Russian icons anywhere outside the lands where they were made. The icons themselves are small or medium-sized, devotional panels for private rooms or for travel, not church-sized altarpieces. And the gold backgrounds aren't decoration. In the Orthodox tradition they're the light of heaven, and the figures inside that light are meant to be present, not pictured. One pair of works gets its own track. Find the small Beheading of Saint John the Baptist, or, just nearby, the Mother of God of the long title. When you're in front of one of them, play the next track.
