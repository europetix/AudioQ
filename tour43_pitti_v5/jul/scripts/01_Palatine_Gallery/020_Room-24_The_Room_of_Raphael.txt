You've just stepped into one of the great rooms of European art. Take a breath before you start looking. First, the ceiling. It's by Pietro da Cortona, begun in sixteen forty-six and completed by his pupil Ciro Ferri in the sixteen sixties. Saturn, the old god, time, the slowest planet, stands at the centre, with allegories of Fame and Eternity around him. The frescoes in all five Planet Rooms together form a single programme written by Francesco Rondinelli, the Grand Duke's librarian. The programme follows a young prince through the stages of his education: tempted by the pleasures of Venus, instructed by Apollo, hardened by Mars, crowned by Jupiter, and finally in old age in this room, Saturn, joining the realm of eternity. Under the Medici, the visitor entered at Venus and worked through to Saturn as a kind of moral ascent. The modern museum reverses that route, so we end at Venus. The walls of this room are dominated by Raphael, four major works, the largest concentration of his Florentine and Roman portraits anywhere. They're here because Vittoria della Rovere, the seventeenth-century Medici grand duchess, brought them from her family's collection in Urbino. Without her inheritance, this room would not exist as it is. Four Raphaels get their own track. Start with the Madonna della Seggiola, a small round painting, a Virgin embracing the Christ Child, the most reproduced Madonna in Western art. Find it and play the next track.
