Andrea del Sarto, who painted this around fifteen twenty-six, was for many Florentines the most accomplished painter working in the city between Raphael's departure for Rome and the rise of the next generation. Vasari, who knew him, called him pittore senza errori, the painter without errors. Which sounds like high praise until you realise Vasari also half-meant it as a criticism: too smooth, too correct, without the divine roughness of Michelangelo or the inventive fire of Raphael. Look at the painting. The composition is split into two zones: heaven above, earth below. In the upper half, the Virgin rises on a cloud carried by angels, in the assumption, the traditional Catholic belief that Mary was taken bodily into heaven at the end of her life, without dying. In the lower half, the apostles gather around her empty tomb. Some look up. Some look at one another. Some look at her empty grave-clothes laid on the ground. Now look more carefully at the apostles. They aren't all rapturous. The figure in the lower right is leaning down and reaching into the tomb, almost suspicious, checking whether she's really gone. Another, on the left, is gripped by a much older apostle, as if being told to calm down. This is what Andrea del Sarto was particularly good at: giving every figure in a crowd a different psychological reaction to the same event. The miracle is happening above. The human response to the miracle is happening below. The painting was commissioned by Margherita Passerini for the high altar of a church in Cortona, which is why it's sometimes called the Passerini Assumption. It came into the Medici collection in the eighteenth century and has been at the Pitti since.
