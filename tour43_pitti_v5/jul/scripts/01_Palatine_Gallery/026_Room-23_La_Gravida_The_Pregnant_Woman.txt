The painting has been called La Gravida, the pregnant woman, for centuries, and we have no record of who commissioned it or who the sitter was. Best scholarship places it around fifteen-oh-six, when Raphael was twenty-three years old and spending time in Florence between his early career in Umbria and his summons to Rome by Pope Julius the Second. She would have been a Florentine woman, probably from a prosperous family, the cloth of her gown, the white chemise, the simple gold ring on her index finger all suggest moderate wealth without courtly ostentation. What makes the painting unusual for its moment is exactly what makes it ordinary now. Renaissance portraits of women were almost always about something other than the woman herself. A bride at her wedding. A widow in mourning. A donor on the side of an altarpiece. Raphael has stripped all of that away. No symbols, no inscription, no allegory. He's painted a specific person at a specific moment in her body, late second or early third trimester, and let her look at us. Look at her hand on her abdomen. It's not demonstrative. She's not pointing. The hand is just resting there the way a pregnant woman's hand often does, half-conscious of the weight. Her expression is calm and faintly amused. She knows we're looking at her and she's looking back, and the painting trusts us to handle that. Raphael painted three or four small portraits of Florentine women in these years, all of them carrying this same direct quality. La Gravida is the one that survives best because she's the least adorned and the most herself.
