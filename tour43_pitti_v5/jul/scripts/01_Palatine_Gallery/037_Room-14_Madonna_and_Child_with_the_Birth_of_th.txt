Step in close. There's more going on in this round panel than first meets the eye. Filippo Lippi painted this around fourteen fifty-two for the Bartolini family of Florence. He was a Carmelite friar by training, though by this point in his life he had abandoned the monastic discipline almost entirely and was living with a former nun named Lucrezia Buti, with whom he eventually had a son, Filippino, who would become one of the most important Florentine painters of the next generation. Lippi the elder was one of the great storytellers of the early Renaissance. Look at the structure of the painting. The Virgin and Child in the foreground are the ostensible subject, but step into the round frame and you realise the painting contains at least three separate stories. In the deep background on the left, behind a series of open archways, you can see the birth of the Virgin herself, Saint Anne reclining on a bed, women bringing her food, a servant washing the baby. On the right, in a separate architectural space, Joachim and Anne meet at the Golden Gate of Jerusalem, the moment tradition held the Virgin was conceived. So what you're looking at is, in time, three generations layered into one frame: the Virgin's grandparents meeting, the Virgin being born, and the Virgin herself now seated with her son. Now look at the architecture. Lippi painted this in the decades immediately after Brunelleschi had revolutionised Florentine thinking about perspective. The arches receding into the deep background of the painting are a perspectival exercise, straight lines converging on a single vanishing point, but Lippi has bent the exercise toward storytelling. Each archway is a stage. Each stage holds an episode. The deeper you look into the painting, the further back in time you go. Look at the Christ Child reaching for the pomegranate. The pomegranate, with its many seeds inside a single fruit, was a traditional symbol of the Church and of resurrection, but the gesture here is the gesture of any baby reaching for something bright.
