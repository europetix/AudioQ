The Sala di Ercole is unlike any other Palatine room you've walked through. Instead of paintings on the walls, you're surrounded by a set of monumental porcelain vases, each one almost as tall as a person, white and blue, with scenes painted around the body. These are Doccia porcelain. The Doccia factory was founded by the Florentine Marquis Carlo Ginori in seventeen thirty-five in a small town just outside Florence, and it became the most important Italian porcelain manufactory of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. Italy lagged behind Meissen and Sèvres in the development of hard-paste porcelain, the technical knowledge was guarded almost as a state secret in Germany and France, but by the early eighteen hundreds, Doccia had caught up. The set in this room shows the twelve Labours of Hercules, the cycle of twelve impossible tasks the hero of Greek mythology had to perform in penance for killing his own children in a fit of madness sent by the goddess Hera. The Nemean lion. The Hydra. The Erymanthian boar. The stables of Augeas. Each vase carries one Labour, painted as a miniature classical scene on the white porcelain ground. Walk slowly around. Some of the vases are more elaborately painted than others. The best of them are extraordinary objects of early nineteenth-century craft.
