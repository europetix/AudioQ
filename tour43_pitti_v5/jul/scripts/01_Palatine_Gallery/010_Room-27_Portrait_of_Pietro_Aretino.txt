Stand close, then step back, the way you did for the other Titian in the Mars room. The man is Pietro Aretino, and to understand the portrait you have to understand him. Aretino was the most feared writer in sixteenth-century Italy, a satirist, poet and pamphleteer who made a living partly by praising the powerful and partly by threatening to wreck their reputations if they didn't pay. He more or less invented the celebrity gossip column and the blackmail letter. He was also one of Titian's closest friends; the two of them, with the sculptor Sansovino, formed the inner circle of Venetian artistic life for decades. Titian painted this around fifteen forty-five, and Aretino sent it as a gift to Cosimo the First de' Medici here in Florence, which is how it reached the Pitti. Look at how it's painted. The face is finished, alert, watchful. But the robe, the great russet-and-gold expanse that fills most of the canvas, is painted with astonishing freedom: broad slabs of colour, the gold shimmer suggested rather than described, the whole surface alive with loose, visible brushwork. Aretino himself had mixed feelings. In a letter he grumbled that the portrait was more sketched than finished, and that Titian would have worked it up further if he'd been paid more. But he knew it was extraordinary. What he read as unfinish, we read as the thing that makes Titian Titian: the late, free handling where paint stops describing cloth and becomes cloth, where standing too close shows you only strokes and stepping back conjures a living man. It's the same lesson as the anonymous gentleman in the Mars room, but here you know exactly who's looking back at you: the sharpest, most dangerous pen in Italy, painted by the friend who knew him best.
