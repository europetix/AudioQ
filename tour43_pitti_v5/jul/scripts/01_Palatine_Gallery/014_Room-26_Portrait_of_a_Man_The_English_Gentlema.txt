We don't know who he is. The painting has been called the Portrait of an English Gentleman since at least the seventeenth century, but no documentation supports the identification. He might be English. He might be Venetian. He might be one of the international diplomats or merchants who passed through Titian's Venetian studio in the fifteen forties, when he was painting portraits of half the courts of Europe. What we know is what we can see. Titian painted this around fifteen forty-five, when he was perhaps fifty-five and at the height of his powers. The portrait is, in a way, all about what's missing. There's no architectural setting. No symbolic objects. No coat of arms. No inscription telling you the sitter's name or station. Just a man in front of a flat background, looking at you. Look at his eyes. This is what Titian could do that no other Renaissance painter could quite manage. Look at the way the iris isn't a flat colour but a structure of small touches of different blues and greys. Look at the moisture at the inner corner of each eye. Look at the slightly different angle of each eye, not looking straight forward, but each focused at a slightly different distance, the way real eyes are when they're really looking at something. Now step back two paces. From two metres away, the brushwork dissolves and the face becomes real. Step close again. Up close, you can see every individual stroke of paint, rough, deliberate, almost casual. Titian in his late career painted with what his contemporaries called pittura di macchia, the painting of the patch. Strokes that resolve into faces only at distance. He had figured out, two hundred years before the Impressionists, that the eye completes what the brush only suggests.
