She's worth a slow look. Mary Magdalene, in the Gospels, is the woman who anointed Christ's feet and was the first witness to his Resurrection. By the medieval period a whole legendary tradition had grown up around her, that she had been a prostitute before her conversion, that she had spent her later years as a hermit in a French cave doing penance, that her hair had grown long enough to cover her body. Almost none of that comes from the actual biblical text; most of it is fourteenth-century French legend. But by Titian's time the iconography was fixed: the penitent Magdalene was nude or near-nude, her long hair her only garment, eyes raised in religious ecstasy. Titian painted this around fifteen thirty-one for Federico Gonzaga, the Duke of Mantua, who had asked for a Magdalene 'as tearful as possible.' Titian gave him a Magdalene balanced between two states. Look at her face. Her eyes are raised, her mouth is parted, her expression is uncertain. Is she weeping in penitence? Is she experiencing mystical rapture? Is she just looking up because something has caught her eye? Titian deliberately leaves the question open. The ambiguity is the painting. Look at the hair. Every strand is painted differently. Some are tight curls, some loose waves. The light catches the top of her head and runs down the surface of the hair as a kind of slow gold river. The hair both covers her body, making this religious art, and reveals it. Critics complained at the time. Federico Gonzaga didn't. The Pitti's Magdalene is one of at least seven Magdalenes Titian painted over his career, each slightly different. He returned to the subject again and again. The version here is the earliest of the type and the one most scholars consider the best.
