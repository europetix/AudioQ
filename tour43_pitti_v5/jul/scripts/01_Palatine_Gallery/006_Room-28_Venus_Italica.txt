Antonio Canova was the most famous sculptor in Europe in eighteen-oh-four when he took this commission. The brief was difficult. Napoleon had taken the antique Medici Venus from the Pitti to Paris, and Canova was supposed to make a replacement. He could have made a copy. He chose not to. Look at her pose. The antique Medici Venus stands frontally, weight balanced, hands raised in a symmetrical gesture of modesty. Canova's Venus is doing the opposite. She's caught mid-movement, weight on one leg, turning her body away from us, pulling a cloth around her in a single twisting gesture. Her head is bent. Her gaze is downward. She isn't posing. She's reacting to having been seen. The cloth is the most extraordinary thing in the carving. Canova was famous in his lifetime for his ability to make stone look like cloth, and look at this drapery, the wet weight of it where it clings to her thigh, the soft folds where it falls away from her hand, the slight rumple at the small of her back. She's just stepped out of a bath. The cloth is what she's reached for. There's a story Canova told about the carving. He said that finishing the surface of a Venus was always the hardest part. The marble had to be worked and worked with fine abrasives and wax until it lost the look of stone and started to look like skin. Stand close. The shoulder catches the light differently from the back. The cheekbone differently from the forehead. It isn't a flat polish, it's a modulated surface that responds to where the light is coming from. Her pose has been called the most romantic of the neoclassical age. Looking down, vulnerable, turning away, she isn't a goddess in glory. She's a young woman caught off guard. The shift from the antique to the modern, here, is the shift from cosmic order to private feeling.
