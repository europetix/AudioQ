There's a quiet joke in where this portrait hangs, and it's worth knowing before you look. The man is Galileo Galilei. In sixteen ten, when he turned his new telescope on Jupiter and saw four little points of light circling it, he named them the Medicean Stars, a piece of flattery aimed straight at his patron, Cosimo the Second de' Medici, who promptly made him court philosopher and mathematician for life. Four centuries later, the portrait of the man who discovered the moons of Jupiter hangs in the Medici's Room of Jupiter. The painter is Giusto Sustermans, a Fleming who became the Medici court portraitist around sixteen twenty and held the post for the better part of sixty years. If a grand duke or duchess has looked out at you from a frame anywhere in this palace, the odds are good that Sustermans painted them. Here he gives us Galileo near the end of his life: the great dome of the forehead, the grey beard, the eyes that by now were beginning to fail him, he would lose his sight entirely within a few years. And remember what you're standing in front of. This is the face of a man condemned as a heretic, painted while he lived out his sentence under house arrest after his trial by the Inquisition, kept safe, and kept employed, by the Medici to the very end. More of Sustermans' Medici faces are waiting for you later, in the Royal Apartments. When you've looked enough, walk through into the next room, the Sala di Saturno, the Room of Raphael.
