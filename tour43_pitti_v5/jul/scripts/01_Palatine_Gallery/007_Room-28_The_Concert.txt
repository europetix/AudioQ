Give this one a slow minute, up close. The painting is small enough that the three figures crowd close together, almost too close. The young musician at the keyboard has turned his head back over his shoulder, as if he heard a noise behind him. His hands are still on the keys. Whatever he was playing has stopped, mid-phrase. The older bearded man behind him rests a hand on his shoulder, supportive or restraining, hard to tell. The younger figure on the right looks on with a kind of waiting attention. The painting is one of the most haunting in the Palatine. For about three centuries it was attributed to Giorgione, the mysterious Venetian painter who died young in fifteen ten and whose few surviving works changed Venetian painting forever. The attribution was compelling: the lush warmth, the silent narrative, the figures caught in a moment that doesn't explain itself. Twentieth-century scholarship moved it back to Titian, painted around fifteen ten or fifteen twelve, when Titian was a young man in his early twenties working in Giorgione's studio and absorbing everything he could. It probably is Titian. But the painting still belongs to Giorgione's world, the world of small, intimate Venetian paintings that don't tell you what they mean. We don't know who the three figures are. We don't know what music was being played. We don't know what made the young man turn around. Critics have proposed all kinds of identifications, a music master with two students, a Christian morality scene about temptation, a portrait of three real Venetians. None has won. What we have is the moment. The interruption. The hand on the shoulder. The pause in the music. The eyes of the young player meeting ours from inside the painting.
