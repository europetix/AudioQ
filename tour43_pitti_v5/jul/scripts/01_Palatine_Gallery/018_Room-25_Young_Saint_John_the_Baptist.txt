Get in close, the panel is small, and the brushwork rewards you for it. Andrea del Sarto painted this around fifteen twenty-three, fifteen twenty-four, about a decade before the Passerini Assumption you saw in the Iliad Room. By this point in his career he had a well-established Florentine workshop and was painting major commissions for the Medici and the most important Florentine churches. The subject is the young Saint John the Baptist. The traditional iconography for John had him either as a baby playing with the infant Christ, or as a grown desert prophet, wild-haired, gaunt, dressed in camel hair, pointing his viewer toward Christ. Andrea del Sarto chose neither. His John is an adolescent, painted from a real Florentine model, looking out at us with an expression that's neither prophetic nor playful. It's simply attentive. He's been caught mid-thought. The model was almost certainly a boy named Giuliano Bugiardini who worked in Andrea del Sarto's studio. His face appears in several other paintings of the period. The bare chest, the slightly crooked smile, the touch of shadow under the cheekbone, these are observed, not invented. Look at the painting of the skin. Andrea del Sarto was a master of warm flesh tones, the ability to make painted skin look as if blood is moving underneath it. Look at the soft transition from the lit side of the chest into the shadow at the shoulder. Look at the way the curls of hair catch the light. This is the kind of painting that taught the next generation of Tuscan painters, Pontormo, Bronzino, what flesh could do on a panel. Vasari liked Andrea del Sarto. He thought him the painter without errors. Critics have spent five centuries arguing whether painting without errors is the same thing as painting with greatness. Look at this John and decide for yourself. When you've looked enough, there's one more face in this room you shouldn't miss, Galileo's, on the next track.
