Stand close. The composition is unusually intimate. It's a tondo, a round painting, a Florentine format that goes back to wedding gifts of the fourteen-hundreds. The round frame forced painters to think differently from a rectangular panel. Curves had to follow the edge. Figures had to nest into one another. Raphael, painting this around fifteen-thirteen during his Roman years under Pope Leo the Tenth, used the constraint as an invitation. Look at how Mary is sitting. She's on a low chair, la seggiola, the small seat, and she's turned to embrace the Christ Child without facing him. Her cheek presses against the top of his head. He turns to look at us with the alert seriousness of a small child who's been disturbed mid-task. The pose is the opposite of formal. There is no throne. No framing arch. No floor. Just three figures packed into a circle. Now look at her clothing. The striped cloth on her head, the rough green shawl across her shoulders, the simple dark gown underneath. Tradition says Raphael saw a peasant woman sitting with her child on a low stool in a Roman doorway and painted what he'd seen. The story is almost certainly invented, but it shows how unusual the painting felt even to contemporaries. Mary as a real woman with a real toddler in real clothes. The painting was already in the Tribuna of the Uffizi by fifteen eighty-nine. Napoleon took it to France in seventeen ninety-nine; it came back in eighteen-fifteen and has been in Florence ever since. From the moment it returned, copying this tondo became an almost mandatory exercise for young artists on the Grand Tour. Reproductions of it have hung in more European homes than any other Renaissance Madonna.
