This portrait was painted in fifteen eighteen, two years before Raphael's death, when he was already the most sought-after artist in Italy and Pope Leo the Tenth was the most powerful patron in Europe. The pope is Giovanni de' Medici, second son of Lorenzo the Magnificent, Florentine to the bone, who took the name Leo the Tenth when he became pope in fifteen thirteen. He's the Medici pope who excommunicated Martin Luther, began the rebuilding of Saint Peter's, and drove the papacy nearly to bankruptcy with the scale of his patronage. He famously said, on becoming pope, 'God has given us the papacy. Let us enjoy it.' Or so the chroniclers report. Whether he said it or not, he meant it. The two cardinals are his cousins. Behind him on the right is Giulio de' Medici, the future Pope Clement the Seventh, who would later have Rome sacked under his watch and supervise the construction of the Medici Chapels in San Lorenzo. On the left is Luigi de' Rossi, the pope's first cousin, who would die a year later of plague. Now look at the tiny extraordinary thing in the painting. On the back of the pope's chair, behind his shoulder, there's a small burnished gold ball, a finial. Look closely at it. You can see, reflected in its surface, the window of the room the painting was made in. Vasari, writing about this painting fifty years later, thought this single detail alone proved Raphael could do anything he wanted with paint. One more thing to know. For a long time some scholars believed the two cardinals were added later by Raphael's workshop.
