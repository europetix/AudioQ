The painting's name is its background. An impannata, in Italian, is the oiled-paper windowpane that filled the role glass would later play in Italian windows. Cheaper than glass, more rigid than cloth, it diffused the light through a yellowish, milky surface and made rooms warmer in winter. Raphael painted an impannata across the window in the background of this painting, and the gentle yellow-cream light of that window falls evenly across all five figures. He painted this around fifteen-thirteen, in Rome, during his years as court painter to Pope Leo the Tenth. By this point his workshop was producing paintings on an industrial scale, commissions arriving faster than Raphael could complete them personally, and a team of skilled younger painters working alongside him on backgrounds, drapery, and secondary figures while Raphael handled the faces. This painting shows that division clearly. The faces, especially the Virgin and the Christ Child, are Raphael himself. The hands are mostly Raphael. The drapery of Saint Anne and the architectural backdrop were probably painted by Giulio Romano, his most talented assistant. Look at the composition. Five figures fit into a tight pyramidal space. The Christ Child at the centre is turning in his mother's lap to reach toward the infant John on the right, the cousin who would one day prepare the way for him. The two children making eye contact is the moment the picture turns on. Everything else, the three older women, is framing. The painting came to the Pitti through the della Rovere inheritance, the same path that brought the great Raphaels into the Saturn Room. Cardinal Bibbiena, Raphael's close friend, may have commissioned it for his own household, the painting was certainly in his estate at his death in fifteen twenty.
