Walk all the way around the table. You're looking at one of the last great showpieces produced by the Opificio delle Pietre Dure, the Medici workshop of semi-precious stone inlay, which had been founded by Ferdinando the First in fifteen eighty-eight. By the time this table was completed in eighteen fifty-one, the Opificio had been working continuously for two hundred and sixty-three years. Look at the surface. It isn't painted. Every leaf, every petal, every shading of colour is a separate piece of stone, lapis lazuli, malachite, agate, jasper, mother-of-pearl, porphyry, cut to shape and fitted together so tightly that you can run a finger over the seams and barely feel them. The technique is called commesso fiorentino, Florentine mosaic. Each petal might have taken a craftsman a full day to shape, polish, and set. The whole tabletop took something like twenty years of intermittent work. The central medallion shows the nine Muses, the Greek goddesses of inspiration, who give the table its name. Around them, a riot of flowers, fruits, birds, and small landscapes. It's the kind of object Habsburg-Lorraine Florence wanted to be seen producing: at once intricate, learned, and impossibly expensive. The bronze base underneath was made by Giovanni Dupré, a young Florentine sculptor who would later become one of the leading nineteenth century Italian sculptors. He cast it in eighteen fifty-one to match the tabletop. The figures around the base, small allegories, drapery, scrollwork, are worth crouching for if you have the knees. When you've looked enough, walk through the Hall of Music toward the Grand Duke's apartment. The first of the great Planet Rooms you'll enter is the Sala di Venere, the Room of Venus.
