Stand close. This one doesn't ask to be liked. Most painters who took on this subject painted the killing itself, the sword going in. Artemisia Gentileschi paints the minute after. Judith and her maidservant Abra are still inside Holofernes' tent, in the dark, his severed head half-bundled into a basket between them. And they've both stopped, turned toward something off to one side, a sound we can't hear. You're watching two women a heartbeat from being caught. Look at the sword. Holofernes' own oversized blade rests back against Judith's shoulder, its edge close to her bare neck, a reminder of exactly what she's just done, and of what happens to her if the camp wakes. But look at her grip and her face. This isn't panic. It's control. The light is pure Caravaggio, one hard source, deep shadow, the two faces lifted out of the black, but the steadiness is Artemisia's own. So is the partnership. Judith's hand rests on Abra's shoulder, and the maid isn't a servant doing a job; she's an accomplice, about the same age, in it together. Painters before Artemisia tended to make the maid old, or frightened, or barely there. Here the two women hold the danger between them. Worth knowing who painted it. Artemisia was the first woman admitted to Florence's academy of drawing, working for Grand Duke Cosimo the Second, in a profession that barely let women through the door. She returned to Judith again and again across her life. The Uffizi has her version of the beheading itself; this is the quieter, tenser sequel. When you've looked enough, leave the Sala dell'Iliade through the doorway on the far wall. From here, continue to the Sala dell'Educazione di Giove, the Room of the Education of Jupiter.
