She's traditionally identified as Margarita Luti, the woman known as la Fornarina, the baker's daughter, who was Raphael's mistress and longtime model during his Roman years. The identification isn't certain, but it's been argued for centuries. The same face appears in at least one other Raphael portrait, the more famous Fornarina at the Galleria Barberini in Rome, where she's painted with bare shoulders, a turban, and a band on her arm bearing Raphael's name. The two paintings are almost certainly the same model. If she is la Fornarina, the painting is a private declaration, Raphael painting his lover in the most expensive clothes he could imagine and giving her the dignity of a noble portrait. If she isn't, she's an unidentified Roman gentlewoman, painted by Raphael at the height of his powers, in a manner that established the standard for female portraiture for the next two hundred years. Now look at the dress. It's the dress that makes the painting. The white silk catches the studio light and gives it back in folds, ridges, ripples. The sleeves are slashed in the Roman court fashion of the fifteen-tens, showing puffs of fine white linen underneath. The gold-thread embroidery on the bodice shimmers but doesn't distract. The veil over her hair is the same white silk, almost the same colour as the dress, and it tells you this isn't a wedding portrait. In Italian tradition a married woman covered her hair. Whoever she is, she's been deliberately framed as both modest and very visible. Look at her right hand on her chest. The pose is calm, slightly defensive, slightly wary, a gesture of self-containment. The single pearl pendant rests above her hand, on her heart.
