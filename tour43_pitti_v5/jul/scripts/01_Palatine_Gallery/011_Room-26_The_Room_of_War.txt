You've moved one room deeper into the Grand Duke's reception suite. The ceiling here is by Pietro da Cortona again, painted in the late sixteen forties. Mars at the centre, sword raised, surrounded by the chaos of war, figures tumbling, weapons clashing, the smoke of battle. In the Education of the Prince programme that runs through all five Planet Rooms, Mars is the stage of hardening. The young prince has been tempted by Venus, instructed by Apollo, and now must learn the brutal arts of rule. Cortona doesn't make war glorious here. He makes it violent and confused. The lesson the prince is supposed to take is not that war is beautiful but that he will sometimes have to fight, and that fighting costs. The walls of this room carry some of the largest and most ambitious paintings in the Palatine. Two of them are by Peter Paul Rubens, the Flemish master who worked as court painter and diplomat across half of seventeenth-century Europe. Rubens spent eight years in Italy in his twenties and never stopped responding to Italian painting after he went home. The two Rubens canvases here belong to different decades of his life and show different sides of him. Three works in this room get their own track. Start with the Four Philosophers, a small intimate group portrait of four men seated around a table, far quieter than the rest of the room. It hangs at eye level. Find it and play the next track.
