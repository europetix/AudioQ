Get close, then step back. This is the painting the room is known for. At first it reads as almost serene. A beautiful young woman in a gold-and-blue silk dress, calm, upright, looking out past you. Then your eye drops to her lowered hand, and the serenity curdles: she's holding a man's severed head by the hair. That contrast, the exquisite surface, the horror inside it, is the whole painting. Cristofano Allori finished it around sixteen eleven, and he loaded it with his own life. The head of Holofernes is a self-portrait. The radiant Judith is a Florentine woman known as la Mazzafirra, who had recently left him. The story told at the time was that Allori painted his own face onto the dead general as a kind of confession, this is what loving her cost me. True or not, he made the most personal version of this subject anyone in Florence had attempted. Look at the dress. That shimmering brocade isn't only Allori showing off; it's a tribute to the thing Florence was richest at, cloth. The city's whole fortune was built on textiles, and fabric painted this convincingly was a kind of civic boast. And one thread ties this room back to the Iliad Room you walked through earlier. Allori learned this trick of lifting figures out of pure darkness partly from Caravaggio, and partly through Artemisia Gentileschi, who was working for the Medici at the very same moment. Her Judith and his Judith are cousins, painted streets apart.
