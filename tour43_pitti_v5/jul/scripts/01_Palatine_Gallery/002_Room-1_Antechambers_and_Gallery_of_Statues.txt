You're now inside the Palatine Gallery, the main reason most people come to Pitti, and the densest concentration of Italian Renaissance and Baroque painting anywhere outside the Uffizi. The Palatine isn't really a museum in the modern sense. It's a Habsburg-Lorraine picture gallery from the late seventeen-hundreds and early eighteen-hundreds, hung the way the grand dukes wanted to be seen. About five hundred paintings on damask walls, stacked two and three deep, floor to ceiling, in heavy carved frames. No labels next to the works, only small numbered tags. It was hung to impress, not to study; Henry James said the masterpieces here jostle each other in their splendour. The rooms are the show as much as the paintings are. What you walk through first: long antechambers, where court visitors once waited to be received. Then a long gallery of classical marble statues, most from the Villa Medici in Rome. The gallery used to be an open loggia overlooking Ammannati's courtyard, you can still feel its proportions, even though it was glassed in long ago. The route here has been reversed from the original. The grand dukes wanted you to begin with the Room of Venus and work through the Planet Rooms in Ptolemaic order, ending at Saturn. The modern museum sends you in from the other end, for crowd flow. So we'll start with the Castagnoli Room and the Room of the Iliad, then enter the Planet Rooms in reverse, ending at Venus.
