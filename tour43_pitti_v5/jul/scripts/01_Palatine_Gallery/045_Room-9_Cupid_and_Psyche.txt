Look up first. The ceiling shows the climax of the myth of Cupid and Psyche, one of the great love stories of classical mythology, told by the Roman writer Apuleius in The Golden Ass. Psyche, a mortal princess of extraordinary beauty, falls in love with Cupid and, after a long series of trials imposed by Cupid's jealous mother Venus, is finally received as an immortal among the gods on Mount Olympus. The fresco shows that final moment. Jupiter has consented to the marriage. Psyche, draped in white, is being welcomed into divinity. Giuseppe Collignon painted this around eighteen twenty for the Habsburg-Lorraine grand dukes. He was a French-born painter who had settled in Florence and become one of the more successful court frescoists of the period. On the walls, and worth crossing the room for, are usually several paintings by Salvator Rosa. Rosa was a seventeenth-century Neapolitan painter, poet, satirist, and general troublemaker, famous in his time for wild stormy landscapes populated by tiny figures of bandits, hermits, soldiers, and philosophers. His landscapes anticipate the Romantic taste for the sublime by a hundred years. The Medici grand dukes collected him enthusiastically in the seventeenth century, and the Pitti holds one of the best Rosa collections anywhere.
