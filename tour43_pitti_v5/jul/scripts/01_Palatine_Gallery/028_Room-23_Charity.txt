Lorenzo Bartolini was born in seventeen seventy-seven in a Tuscan village, trained partly in Paris, and became the most influential Italian sculptor of his generation. By the eighteen twenties, when this Charity was commissioned, the dominant style across Europe was the high neoclassicism of Antonio Canova, figures of ideal proportion, smooth as porcelain, modelled on Greek and Roman antiquity. Bartolini thought that whole tradition had grown cold. His campaign through the eighteen thirties was for what he called naturalismo, a return to warmer, less idealised, more domestic figures, drawn from observation of real bodies rather than copies of antique statues. Look at the Charity in that light. She's a young woman, not a goddess. The proportions are human, not idealised. Her drapery falls in naturalistic folds. The standing child leaning into her lap is drawn from observation of a real child, not from a Roman putto. Her expression is tender without being theatrical. She isn't performing motherhood, she's doing it. The subject is the Christian virtue of Charity, traditionally personified as a woman surrounded by children, but Bartolini has stripped the religious iconography back almost completely. No flame above her head. No inscription. She could be a saint, a Madonna, or just a young mother on a quiet afternoon. That ambiguity is the whole point. The sculpture sits in the centre of this room to give the eye a place to rest. Salon-style hanging, paintings stacked floor to ceiling on every wall, needs a still object somewhere in the room, or the eye has nowhere to go.
