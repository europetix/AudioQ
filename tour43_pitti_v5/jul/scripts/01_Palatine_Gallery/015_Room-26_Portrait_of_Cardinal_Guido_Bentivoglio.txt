Van Dyck was barely into his twenties when he painted this. He had just left Rubens's workshop in Antwerp and arrived in Italy to study Titian at the source, and what you are looking at is a young master announcing himself. The sitter is Cardinal Guido Bentivoglio: churchman, scholar, and diplomat. He had served as the papal ambassador to Flanders, Van Dyck's own homeland, so the two of them shared a world, and you can feel that ease between painter and subject. Look first at the red. The cardinal's watered silk ripples with light, the rippled sheen of the fabric caught so exactly that you half expect to hear it rustle. That kind of bravura with surfaces is what would make Van Dyck the most sought-after portraitist in Europe within just a few years. Then look at the face, alert, quick, faintly guarded, a sharp mind caught a half-second into a thought. That pairing of gorgeous cloth and real psychology became the blueprint for grand portraiture for the next two centuries; you can draw a straight line from this canvas to Velázquez's popes, and on to the swaggering court portraits Van Dyck himself would later paint for Charles the First of England. It hangs here in the Mars Room for a reason. This is where the gallery shows off the Medici taste for northern European painting, which is exactly why the great Rubens canvases you've just seen share these walls with it.
