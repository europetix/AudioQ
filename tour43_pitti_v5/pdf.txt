 How to use this guide
 This is a room-by-room audio guide to the whole Palazzo Pitti complex — the palace museums, the
 Boboli Gardens, the Bardini Garden, and the Porcelain Museum. The guide is built around two kinds of
 tracks. Knowing the difference will let you move at your own pace.


 R-tracks — room introductions
 Tracks beginning with R (R02, R03, and so on) are short room introductions. Play each R-track as you
 walk into a new room or garden space. It tells you what's there, what to look for first, and which works
 will get their own track. R-tracks usually run between one and a half and two and a half minutes.

 W-tracks — single works
 Tracks beginning with W (W06_01, W08_01, and so on) are focused on a single painting, sculpture, or
 fountain. Play a W-track when you are standing in front of the work it describes. The track code is just a
 sequential label for the audio file; the official gallery room is printed at the top of every track (ROOM
 24, and so on) and named in the text, so you can match the tour to the museum's own room signs.
 W-tracks usually run between two and three minutes.

 Pauses and movement
 Each track ends with a short silent pause built into the audio. That's your moment to look, photograph if
 you want to, and walk to the next stop. When you reach a new room, play the next R-track. When you're
 standing in front of a marquee work, play its W-track. The audio always tells you what to play next at
 the end of each track. You don't have to remember anything.

 On honesty and change
 The Pitti is in a long restoration cycle. As this guide was recorded in May 2026, the Treasury of the
 Grand Dukes was fully closed, the Imperial and Royal Apartments required a timed reservation, the
 Boboli Gardens were mid-restoration, and individual rooms were closed for maintenance. The tour
 names these honestly rather than pretending otherwise — but things change. Check the live notices page
 at uffizi.it before you queue.

 The visit, in shape
 T00 in Piazza Pitti → Treasury and Russian Icons on the ground floor → the Palatine Gallery and the
 Royal Apartments on the first floor → the Gallery of Modern Art and the Costume Museum on the
 second floor → out to the Boboli Gardens → the Bardini Garden and the Porcelain Museum → T-LAST
 back in the piazza. The palace museums share one ticket; Boboli, Bardini, and the Porcelain Museum
 share a separate ticket. Allow the better part of a day.

 Piazza Pitti — Before You Go In
    WHERE YOU ARE
    On Piazza Pitti, standing in front of the long stone façade of the palace before you go in.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    The palace fills the whole far side of the square. Two hundred and five metres of rusticated stone, three floors
    visible, two side wings angling forward to form a small forecourt. The square itself slopes gently down
    toward you.
    LISTEN FOR
    How this guide works, what's inside the palace, and the one booking you'll want to sort out before you go in.


 Welcome to Florence. And welcome to Palazzo Pitti, which is large enough that the first thing to do is
 just stand here for a second and look at the front of it.

 Two hundred and five metres across. Three floors of rusticated stone — the surfaces deliberately rough,
 the blocks oversized. The two side wings angle forward to gather the square in front of you. Look at the
 windows while you're standing here: the same round-arched window, repeated in a steady rhythm across
 all three floors and the full width of the front. The palace was begun in the mid-fourteen-hundreds for a
 Florentine banker named Luca Pitti — possibly to a Brunelleschi design — and within a century his
 family went bankrupt and sold it to the Medici. Cosimo the First moved his court here in fifteen fifty,
 and from that moment until nineteen-nineteen this was the home of whoever ruled Tuscany. Medici,
 then Habsburg-Lorraine, then the kings of a united Italy. Three dynasties under one roof, layered on top
 of one another in the rooms you're about to walk through.

 What's inside, and the shape of the day. You'll start one floor up, in the Palatine Gallery — the crown
 jewel, with the largest concentration of Raphael in the world. From there you go up again to the second
 floor, for the Gallery of Modern Art and the Museum of Costume and Fashion. Then you work your
 way back down: the Imperial and Royal Apartments on the first floor, and last of all, on the ground floor
 on your way out, the Museum of Russian Icons and the Palatine Chapel. After that, the Boboli Gardens
 behind the palace.

 One thing to sort out now. The Imperial and Royal Apartments need a separate timed reservation. If you
 haven't booked one, you may be turned away at their door — so reserve a slot before you visit if you
 want to see them.

 All right. Let's go in. Once you're through the ticket check and across the Ammannati Courtyard, find
 the staircase up to the first floor and follow signs for the Palatine Gallery. Play the next track when you
 reach the gallery's entrance.

 Palatine Gallery — Antechambers and Gallery of
 Statues
    WHERE YOU ARE
    At the top of the stairs to the first floor, in the long corridor of antechambers that opens into the Gallery of
    Statues — the formal route into the Palatine.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A series of high-ceilinged antechambers lined with paintings hung floor to ceiling, opening into a long gallery
    filled with classical marble statues. The ceilings carry late-eighteenth-century neoclassical frescoes. Daylight
    comes in through tall windows on your left, overlooking the Boboli Gardens.
    LISTEN FOR
    What the Palatine Gallery actually is, the route through it, and what to expect from a museum that doesn't
    hang things the way modern museums do.


 You're now inside the Palatine Gallery — the main reason most people come to Pitti, and the densest
 concentration of Italian Renaissance and Baroque painting anywhere outside the Uffizi.

 The Palatine isn't really a museum in the modern sense. It's a Habsburg-Lorraine picture gallery from
 the late seventeen-hundreds and early eighteen-hundreds, hung the way the grand dukes wanted to be
 seen. About five hundred paintings on damask walls, stacked two and three deep, floor to ceiling, in
 heavy carved frames. No labels next to the works — only small numbered tags. It was hung to impress,
 not to study; Henry James said the masterpieces here jostle each other in their splendour. The rooms are
 the show as much as the paintings are.

 What you walk through first: long antechambers, where court visitors once waited to be received. Then
 a long gallery of classical marble statues, most from the Villa Medici in Rome. The gallery used to be an
 open loggia overlooking Ammannati's courtyard — you can still feel its proportions, even though it was
 glassed in long ago.

 The route here has been reversed from the original. The grand dukes wanted you to begin with the
 Room of Venus and work through the Planet Rooms in Ptolemaic order, ending at Saturn. The modern
 museum sends you in from the other end, for crowd flow. So we'll start with the Castagnoli Room and
 the Room of the Iliad, then enter the Planet Rooms in reverse, ending at Venus.

 Take a slow walk through the statue gallery. When you reach the Castagnoli Room — you'll know it
 from the large flower-mosaic table at its centre — play the next track.

 Castagnoli Room — The Hub of the Gallery
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In the Castagnoli Room, the small octagonal hub where the gallery splits in two directions. A large
    flower-mosaic table stands in the centre.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    An octagonal room with ceiling frescoes by Giuseppe Castagnoli (early nineteenth century) showing the
    Medici and Habsburg-Lorraine ruling families. Walls covered in court portraits. At the centre, a remarkable
    inlaid-stone table mounted on a bronze base — the Table of the Muses.
    LISTEN FOR
    Why this room is the practical hub of the Palatine, who Castagnoli was, and what's branching off from here.


 You're standing in the practical hub of the Palatine. Two routes leave this room. Through the doorway
 on your right is the Volterrano Wing — the private apartments of Grand Duchess Vittoria della Rovere
 and the Habsburg-Lorraine grand duchesses who followed her, with smaller rooms named for their
 ceiling subjects. Straight ahead through the Hall of Music are the Grand Duke's reception rooms — the
 famous Planet Rooms. We'll do the Volterrano Wing on the way back; for now, we head straight to the
 Planet side.

 The ceiling frescoes here are by Giuseppe Castagnoli, painted in the early eighteen hundreds, and they
 show the two ruling families this palace served — the Medici and the Habsburg-Lorraine — receiving
 allegorical tributes. Castagnoli was a competent court painter rather than a great one, and the frescoes
 function more as decoration than as argument. The room takes his name partly because of him, partly
 because the curators needed a name for the room and there it was on the ceiling.

 The walls are court portraits of the Medici and Habsburg-Lorraine families — keep your eye out for the
 small full-length of Gian Gastone de' Medici, the last male Medici grand duke, who died in seventeen
 thirty-seven and ended the dynasty. He stands solemnly in his curled seventeenth-century wig, with the
 city of Florence dissolving in stormy haze behind him. The painter knew what he was doing.

 But the most extraordinary thing in this room isn't on the walls or the ceiling. It's the table in the centre.

 The Table of the Muses
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Beside the Table of the Muses in the centre of the Castagnoli Room. The piece is a large round table of inlaid
    coloured stones, mounted on a sculpted bronze base.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A circular tabletop, about a metre and a half across, made entirely of cut and inlaid semi-precious stones
    forming a carpet of flowers and a central medallion with the nine Muses. The supporting base is a bronze
    structure of figures and ornament by the Florentine sculptor Giovanni Dupré, dated 1851.
    LISTEN FOR
    What this table actually is, how it was made, and why it took so long to finish.


 Walk all the way around the table. You're looking at one of the last great showpieces produced by the
 Opificio delle Pietre Dure, the Medici workshop of semi-precious stone inlay, which had been founded
 by Ferdinando the First in fifteen eighty-eight. By the time this table was completed in eighteen
 fifty-one, the Opificio had been working continuously for two hundred and sixty-three years.

 Look at the surface. It isn't painted. Every leaf, every petal, every shading of colour is a separate piece
 of stone — lapis lazuli, malachite, agate, jasper, mother-of-pearl, porphyry — cut to shape and fitted
 together so tightly that you can run a finger over the seams and barely feel them. The technique is called
 commesso fiorentino, Florentine mosaic. Each petal might have taken a craftsman a full day to shape,
 polish, and set. The whole tabletop took something like twenty years of intermittent work.

 The central medallion shows the nine Muses — the Greek goddesses of inspiration, who give the table
 its name. Around them, a riot of flowers, fruits, birds, and small landscapes. It's the kind of object
 Habsburg-Lorraine Florence wanted to be seen producing: at once intricate, learned, and impossibly
 expensive.

 The bronze base underneath was made by Giovanni Dupré, a young Florentine sculptor who would later
 become one of the leading nineteenth century Italian sculptors. He cast it in eighteen fifty-one to match
 the tabletop. The figures around the base — small allegories, drapery, scrollwork — are worth
 crouching for if you have the knees.

 When you've looked enough, walk through the Hall of Music toward the Grand Duke's apartment. The
 first of the great Planet Rooms you'll enter is the Sala di Venere, the Room of Venus. Play the next track
 when you arrive.

 Sala di Venere — The First Room
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In the Sala di Venere, the last Planet Room on your modern route — but the first room of the original Medici
    reception sequence. The ceiling shows Venus with the young prince.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A grand reception room with a Pietro da Cortona ceiling fresco of Venus — the goddess of love — gently
    steering a young prince away from her embrace and toward the path of virtue. At the centre of the room, on a
    low pedestal, Canova's marble Venus Italica. Walls hung with Titian and seventeenth-century landscapes.
    LISTEN FOR
    Why Venus is at the start of the prince's education, what Canova's statue is doing in the centre of the room,
    and the Madonna del Granduca that's currently relocated here.


 You've reached the first of the Planet Rooms — and, under the Medici, the original beginning of the
 route. Under the Medici, visitors entered the Grand Duke's reception suite here. Cortona's ceiling shows
 you what the room was about: Venus, goddess of love, pulling a young prince toward herself, and the
 prince — gently, sadly — turning away. The pleasures of the senses are the first thing the young ruler
 has to learn to refuse before he can be educated by Apollo, hardened by Mars, crowned by Jupiter and
 honoured by Saturn.

 Look at the figure standing in the centre of the room. Antonio Canova carved this marble — the Venus
 Italica — between eighteen-oh-four and eighteen-twelve, on a direct commission from Napoleon.
 Napoleon had just stripped the Pitti of one of its most famous antique sculptures — the so-called Medici
 Venus, an ancient Roman copy of a Greek original — and shipped it to Paris. Canova, the most
 celebrated sculptor in Europe, was commissioned to replace it. He didn't make a copy. He made a
 modern Venus, a younger and more vulnerable figure than the antique one, and placed her in the same
 spot.

 After Waterloo the original Medici Venus came home from Paris. It's now in the Tribuna of the Uffizi.
 The Canova replacement stayed at the Pitti because by then the Florentines had grown fond of her.

 Antonio Canova — Venus Italica
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of Canova's Venus Italica, on the low pedestal in the centre of the Sala di Venere. The marble is
    life-sized, of a young woman pulling a cloth around herself.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A white marble sculpture, 1804-1812, of a young Venus stepping forward and slightly turning, drawing a
    length of damp cloth around her body in a gesture of modesty. Her weight rests on one leg. Her head is bent
    slightly forward, looking down and to the side.
    LISTEN FOR
    What Canova was responding to, what makes this Venus different from the antique one she replaced, and
    why she's been called the most romantic statue of the neoclassical age.


 Walk around her. She's meant to be seen from every side, and the carving changes each time you move.

 Antonio Canova was the most famous sculptor in Europe in eighteen-oh-four when he took this
 commission. The brief was difficult. Napoleon had taken the antique Medici Venus from the Pitti to
 Paris, and Canova was supposed to make a replacement. He could have made a copy. He chose not to.

 Look at her pose. The antique Medici Venus stands frontally, weight balanced, hands raised in a
 symmetrical gesture of modesty. Canova's Venus is doing the opposite. She's caught mid-movement,
 weight on one leg, turning her body away from us, pulling a cloth around her in a single twisting
 gesture. Her head is bent. Her gaze is downward. She isn't posing. She's reacting to having been seen.

 The cloth is the most extraordinary thing in the carving. Canova was famous in his lifetime for his
 ability to make stone look like cloth, and look at this drapery — the wet weight of it where it clings to
 her thigh, the soft folds where it falls away from her hand, the slight rumple at the small of her back.
 She's just stepped out of a bath. The cloth is what she's reached for.

 There's a story Canova told about the carving. He said that finishing the surface of a Venus was always
 the hardest part. The marble had to be worked and worked with fine abrasives and wax until it lost the
 look of stone and started to look like skin. Stand close. The shoulder catches the light differently from
 the back. The cheekbone differently from the forehead. It isn't a flat polish — it's a modulated surface
 that responds to where the light is coming from.

 Her pose has been called the most romantic of the neoclassical age. Looking down, vulnerable, turning
 away — she isn't a goddess in glory. She's a young woman caught off guard. The shift from the antique
 to the modern, here, is the shift from cosmic order to private feeling.

 When you've finished, find the Titian Concert on the wall — a small painting of three musicians, one of
 them turning to look out at us. Play the next track.

 Who they were. A small group of Tuscan painters, mostly based in Florence, who began working in the
 early eighteen sixties under the informal leadership of Giovanni Fattori, Silvestro Lega, and Telemaco
 Signorini. Their name, macchiaioli, from macchia, the Italian word for patch or stain, was originally a

 blended tones. The painters adopted the slur as a badge of honour.

 Their argument was that painting should come from direct observation, from outdoors, from real people
 in real light. They went out into the Tuscan countryside with paint boxes. They painted soldiers in the
 Italian Wars of Independence. Peasants, women at windows, Florentine streets, the Maremma marshes.
 They anticipated French Impressionism by about a decade. They were less radical than the
 Impressionists would be, they kept their drawing tight, their compositions careful, but they were the first
 European movement to argue that ordinary observed reality was a fit subject for serious painting.

 Walk the rooms in their own order. The landscape room gathers the mid-century painters who first went
 outdoors, among them the School of Staggia, the small group founded in eighteen fifty-four by Serafino

 heart of the wing is the Diego Martelli room, Room eleven, named for the critic and patron who

 Lo staffato is an Italian cavalry term — it means a rider whose foot has caught in the stirrup as he falls,
 so that he is dragged behind his horse instead of being thrown clear. It is one of the worst ways to die in
 warfare, and a soldier dragged by a panicked horse can be killed or mutilated within minutes.

 Fattori painted this in eighteen-eighty. He was fifty-five years old, the established master of the
 Macchiaioli school, and he had been painting the Italian Wars of Independence for twenty years. The
 war he is thinking about here is none of them specifically and all of them together — the long
 catastrophic series of nineteenth-century conflicts that produced the unified Italian state. Fattori had not
 himself fought. But several of his closest friends had. He spent his career painting their experience.

 Look at the composition. Fattori has put the horse in the centre-right, in full gallop, moving leftward
 across the canvas. The soldier's body is dragged behind, head thrown back, one foot still caught in the
 stirrup. The landscape is empty. No witnesses. No officers. No enemy. The horizon line is low. The sky
 is enormous. The cavalry soldier — a man with a name, a family, an Italian city he came from — is
 being killed in the middle of nowhere, by no one in particular, in absolute silence.

 Look at the trail of blood. A long dark line across the dry ground, the way a brush leaves a mark on a
 sheet of paper. Fattori has painted the trace with deliberate economy — almost a single gesture.

 The painting is not heroic. It is not patriotic. It is an argument against the rhetoric of war painted in the
 same decade the unified Italian state was building its first monumental war memorials. Fattori's version
 is what war actually looks like when you strip out the speeches and the flags.

 Italian critics have argued for a hundred and fifty years about which of Fattori's small panels is the

 Stop here a moment. Look around the curve of the amphitheatre. You're inside one of the great spaces of

 It was built from fifteen ninety-nine for the most spectacular Medici wedding ever held. Maria de'
 Medici, daughter of Grand Duke Francesco the First, was being married by proxy to Henri the Fourth of
 France, and Florence was determined to outshine every previous royal celebration. The amphitheatre

 combinations of music, dance, and stage machinery — and was later used for tournaments, mock naval
 battles, and operatic performances. When flooded for the naval shows, the whole performance space
 became a lake.

 Six tiers of grass step down to the central oval. In Medici times those tiers carried wooden benches.
 Several thousand spectators could watch, with the Grand Duke and his closest courtiers in the highest
 tier.

 The obelisk in the middle is older than everything else in this garden by a wide margin. It was carved in
 Egypt around thirteen hundred BCE for the pharaoh Ramesses the Second. The Romans brought it to
 Italy in the first century. Grand Duke Pietro Leopoldo brought it to Florence in seventeen eighty-nine,
 where it has stood at the centre of the amphitheatre ever since — a three-thousand-year-old Egyptian
 monument anchoring a Renaissance Italian theatre.

 Walk up to the rim of the basin and take it in from a few angles.

 Stoldo Lorenzi cast this Neptune in fifteen sixty-five for Cosimo the First, who had just become Grand
 Duke of Tuscany and wanted the gardens of his new palace to announce his maritime ambitions.
 Cosimo was building a navy. He wanted Florence to compete with Genoa and Venice as a
 Mediterranean power, and a bronze Neptune on the highest visible point of his gardens was political
 iconography as much as decoration.

 Florentines, though, have a habit of renaming great art with affectionate irreverence. The big marble
 Neptune in the Piazza della Signoria was nicknamed il Biancone — the big white one — because the
 city didn't think much of its proportions and said so. The Boboli Neptune got off more lightly: they
 called it the Fontana del Forcone, the Fountain of the Big Fork, after the prominent trident. By the late
 fifteen hundreds, il forcone was simply what everyone said.

 Look at Neptune's pose. He stands in contrapposto — weight on one leg, the other bent — with the
 trident raised high, the twist of his torso lifting the bronze against the line of the hill behind him. The
 muscle, the beard, the wave of the hair are all high Mannerist: elongated, slightly nervous, deeply
 polished. He isn't a calm classical god. He's restless, energetic, almost theatrical.

 The figure is Abundance — Abbondanza — a personification of plenty and good harvest, holding a
 sheaf of wheat and a cornucopia spilling fruit. She's a standard allegorical subject, but her history is
 anything but standard.

 Giambologna — the Flemish-born sculptor who dominated late sixteenth-century Florence, whose
 Oceanus Fountain you'll meet shortly down the hill — began this figure in the early sixteen hundreds. It
 was originally intended as a portrait statue of a Habsburg archduchess, meant to crown a column
 elsewhere in Florence. Giambologna died in sixteen-oh-eight with the statue unfinished. His most gifted
 pupil, Pietro Tacca, completed the figure together with another assistant, Sebastiano Salvini. They
 reworked the unfinished portrait into a generic Abundance and, in the sixteen thirties, set her at the very
 top of the Boboli axis.

 And that placement is everything. Look back down the hill from where you stand. The whole garden
 was designed around a single straight sight line — from the courtyard of the palace, through the
 Artichoke Fountain, up through the amphitheatre and the obelisk, past the Neptune Fountain, all the way
 up to here. Abundance is the full stop at the end of that sentence. Everything in the central garden points
 at her, and from her feet the entire composition resolves back down toward the palace.

 The Viottolone was laid out in sixteen thirty-seven, in the second great expansion of Boboli under

 new design. The trees were planted young, in matched pairs, in a perfectly straight line, with the
 intention that they would grow into the dark towering colonnade you walk through today.

 the family's villas in Rome. Some are high-quality ancient marbles; some are seventeenth and
 eighteenth-century Florentine carvings. The mixing was deliberate — the Medici wanted the garden to
 feel like a continuous civilisation reaching back to antiquity. Don't worry about identifying them; many
 are unlabelled.

 they make a dark grid you walk through. And look back over your shoulder now and then — the long
 view back up the hill toward the palace is one of the great Florentine vistas, and most visitors miss it
 entirely.

 Giambologna — born Jean Boulogne in Flanders in fifteen twenty-nine — was the dominant sculptor of
 late sixteenth-century Florence. He came to Italy in his twenties, stayed for life, and became official

 figures: the personified rivers Nile, Ganges, and Euphrates — Africa, Asia, and the ancient Near East.
 The four waters together stood for the whole world, with Medici Tuscany imagined at its commercial
 centre.

 The Limonaia — The Medici Obsession with Citrus
    WHERE YOU ARE
    At the Limonaia, the long Lemon House on the western side of the gardens — a low building painted

    WHAT YOU SEE
    The garden's Lemon House (Orangery), built 1777-78 by Zanobi del Rosso under Grand Duke Pietro
    Leopoldo to shelter the Medici citrus collection through winter. A long heated building painted the same
    green as the Kaffeehaus. Still holds part of the historic collection of rare and ancient citrus varieties in

    LISTEN FOR
    Why the Medici were obsessed with lemons, what a 'bizzarria' is, and how the pots you've been seeing all

 Those lemon trees in pots you've seen all over the garden — around the Isolotto, on the terraces — live
 here for half the year. This is the Limonaia, the Lemon House, and the story behind it is one of the most
 characteristically Medici things in the whole garden.

 The Medici were obsessed with citrus. Not casually fond — obsessed. From the sixteenth century on,
 the family assembled one of the largest collections of citrus varieties in Europe: hundreds of kinds of
 lemons, oranges, citrons, and stranger things. Citrus was a status symbol — hard and expensive to keep
 alive through a Tuscan winter, exotic, and producing fruit, flowers, and scent all at once. A grand duke
 who could show you fruiting lemon trees in January was showing you his wealth and his mastery over
 nature.

 The strangest part of the collection were the bizzarrie — oddities. Grafted, deliberately deformed citrus,
 bred to produce knobbled, fingered, or fused fruit in bizarre shapes. The Medici prized them precisely
 because they were strange. The most famous, the Bizzarria, is a single tree producing lemon, orange,
 and citron all at once. Some of these ancient varieties survive here and almost nowhere else.

 The building went up in seventeen seventy-seven, by Zanobi del Rosso — the same architect as the
 Kaffeehaus — painted the same Lorraine green. It's long, low, and heated, with tall windows for the
 winter sun. Every autumn the potted trees are wheeled in out of the cold; every spring back out. A
 four-hundred-year-old seasonal ritual still being performed.
