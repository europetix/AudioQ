[curious] Napoleon gave Antonio Canova five sittings in Paris, and he wasn't an emperor yet. It was 1802. He was First Consul of France, thirty-three years old, and he wanted his face fixed in marble by the most famous sculptor in Europe.

Canova, it is said, took the job reluctantly. He was Italian, and French armies had been carrying Italian art off to Paris. If you've walked through the Palatine Gallery, you've already seen the proof. Canova's Venus Italica stands there because the ancient Medici Venus had been taken to France. [pause] But he went, and he modelled the head in clay, from life.

Now look at the scale. This isn't life-size. It's colossal. Canova idealised him, the way the ancients idealised their rulers. But not too far. The brows are heavy. The mouth is set. Whatever he felt about the sitter, he carved the face of a real man.

[pause] Here's the twist. This marble probably isn't all Canova's own work. The museum gives it to Canova and his workshop, around 1810, and says it is only traditionally believed to be his. The head became a kind of bestseller. Copies were turned out in the marble workshops of Carrara, sponsored by Napoleon's sister Elisa, the woman you may have just seen in Bezzuoli's portrait.

[wry] And then there's how this one reached Florence. In 1845, Napoleon's brother Louis Bonaparte gave it to Grand Duke Leopold the Second, to thank him for his hospitality during Louis's exile in Florence. Leopold's family, the Lorraine, had only been able to come home to Florence once Napoleon fell. So the conqueror's face ended up in their palace, as a thank-you present.

As for Canova, he turned down Napoleon's invitation to move to Paris as his sculptor. After Waterloo, he worked to bring Italy's looted art back home.

[lightly] The enormous painting in Room three, next door, is about another ruler who outstayed his welcome.
