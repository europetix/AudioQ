[lightly] Every museum has a problem with light. A fashion museum has a worse one. Silk fades, linings weaken, and a dress left out for too long simply starts to fall apart.

So this museum works in rotation. Its collection covers the eighteenth century to today: clothes, jewellery and accessories, even stage costumes worn by stars of film, theatre and opera. Only a part is on show at any one time, and every so often, a new selection comes out to replace it.

[pause] Which means I can't tell you exactly what you'll see. But I can tell you what to watch for, whatever it is. Watch the shape of the body.

In the seventeen hundreds, court dress was built like architecture, with hoops at the hips pushing skirts out sideways. Around 1800, all that collapsed. Waistlines jumped up under the bust, and skirts fell straight and light, like a classical statue. Then the nineteenth century put the structure back, until the crinoline made skirts as wide as doorways again. And the twentieth century threw away the corset, and kept reinventing the body every decade or so.

[warmly] One chapter of that story happened right here. In February 1951, a Florentine businessman, Giovanni Battista Giorgini, showed Italian designs to a small group of American buyers in his own home. It went well. From 1952 the shows moved into the Sala Bianca, the White Hall of this palace. That's where the world started to take Italian fashion seriously.

When you've seen the galleries, go back the way you came to the landing, and take the stairs or a lift down to the courtyard. The Russian Icons and the Palatine Chapel are on the ground floor, opening off the courtyard. Follow the signs for the Palatine Chapel.
