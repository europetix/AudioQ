[curious] Nobody knows who he is. Not his name, not his city, not even whether he was English.

The title is a nickname, and no one knows when it began. The painting is first documented in the Medici collections by sixteen ninety-eight, and nothing there explains the name. He might have been one of the diplomats or merchants who passed through Titian's studio in Venice. In those years, Titian was painting half the courts of Europe.

What we do know is what we can see. Titian painted him in the early fifteen-forties, when the painter was in his fifties and at the height of his powers. And the portrait is all about what's missing. There's no architecture, no coat of arms, no inscription. Not even a hat. Just a young bearded man in black, a pair of gloves in his hand, against a plain background.

[quietly] So everything rests on his eyes. They're grey-blue, and they carry the whole picture. The colour isn't flat. It's built from small touches of blue and grey. There's a glint of moisture at the inner corners. He isn't posing for you. He's *looking* at you.

[pause] Now try something. Step back two or three paces. From there, the face is completely alive. Then come close again. Up close, you start to see the separate strokes of paint, quick and almost casual. Titian was already moving toward the free, broken brushwork of his old age, paint that only turns into a face when you stand back.

He'd worked out, three centuries before the Impressionists, that the eye finishes what the brush only suggests.

[warmly] He's been anonymous for almost five hundred years. It hasn't done him any harm.
