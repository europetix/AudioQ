[curious] In nineteenth-century Italy, a painting of the distant past was often a comment on the present. You've just seen one. This room is full of them.

The paintings here sit between two worlds, Romanticism and Realism. Romanticism reached Italy in the eighteen twenties, and it brought big subjects, strong colour and high emotion. The great name was Francesco Hayez, trained in Venice and settled in Milan, the painter of The Kiss. The gallery owns work of his as well.

[pause] Here, look for Giuseppe Bezzuoli's Entry of Charles the Eighth into Florence, painted in 1829. A French king enters the city, and the painting turns the moment into a grand spectacle. Bezzuoli taught at the Florence Academy, and this is the manner his students were trained in.

Look too for Giovanni Dupré's sculpture of Abel, dying. No crowd, no costume. Just a young body, and a stillness that feels very close to life.

[conspiratorial] And next door, a rebellion starts. In the rooms that follow, a group of painters in Florence begin to turn their backs on all this theatre. Room six shows their first experiments, like Giuseppe Abbati's view of the cloister of Santa Croce. Then come rooms of portraits and a room of landscapes.

One of the rebels was a student of Bezzuoli's own, Giovanni Fattori. Keep his name in mind. Walk on through the portrait and landscape rooms to Room ten, where the Macchiaioli rooms begin in earnest, and play its track as you go in.
