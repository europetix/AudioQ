[curious] She may not have been meant as Abundance at all. The story goes that this colossal woman began as a portrait of a real one, Joanna of Austria, a Habsburg archduchess and the wife of Grand Duke Francesco the First. The face, at least, is still said to be hers.

The sculptor was Giambologna, the Flemish-born master who dominated Florentine sculpture in the late fifteen hundreds. He began the figure, and died in 1608 with it unfinished. [pause] His pupil Pietro Tacca took it on. Somewhere along the way, the portrait became an allegory. She was given a sheaf of wheat and a horn of plenty, and turned into Abundance, the promise of good harvests under a wise ruler.

In 1637 she was set up here, at the very top of the garden's central line. [warmly] And that placement is the point. Turn around and look back down the hill. The garden was laid out along this one straight line. It runs from the palace courtyard, past the Artichoke Fountain, through the amphitheatre and its obelisk, past Neptune, all the way up to here. She's the full stop at the end of the sentence.

[quietly] Imagine who stood here before you. A grand duke, looking down at his palace and his city, with a giant figure of plenty at his back. That is exactly how the Medici wanted to be seen. Rich, generous, and firmly in charge of the view.

From here, head west along the top of the hill to the head of the great cypress avenue, the Viottolone, and start down it. Play its track once you're walking under the cypresses.
