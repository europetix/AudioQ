[lightly] Imagine being called a stain, and deciding to keep the name. Macchia is Italian for a patch, or a smudge. In the early eighteen sixties, critics used it to mock a group of painters in Florence who built their pictures from patches of colour instead of smooth, careful blending. The painters took the insult and wore it with pride.

Their idea was simple and stubborn. Paint what you actually see, out of doors, in real light. Soldiers, farm workers, the coast, a quiet afternoon at home. This gallery holds one of the richest collections of their work anywhere.

[pause] Take these rooms one at a time. Look for the pictures of Cristiano Banti, painter and collector, and for Giovanni Boldini's portrait of Alaide Banti.

Then look for Diego Martelli, the critic and patron who championed these painters. Federico Zandomeneghi painted him twice. [curious] And look for two landscapes by Camille Pissarro, a rare French Impressionist voice in this Tuscan company.

In Room twelve, Silvestro Lega's Singing a Stornello, a folk song in a quiet interior. And in Room thirteen, Fattori's Italian Camp after the Battle of Magenta. Notice the title. Not the battle, with its charge and its glory. The camp, afterwards.

[warmly] One more of Fattori's best-loved pictures is in this gallery, a tiny panel of women under an awning by the sea. Its story comes a little later. When you're ready, follow the room numbers on to Room seventeen, where the last stretch of the loop begins.
