[lightly] Here's a strange thought to start with. The palace behind you came out of this hole.

This hollow in the hillside was a quarry, and its stone went into the walls of Palazzo Pitti. When Eleonora di Toledo began these gardens around 1550, her architect Tribolo had a better idea than filling it in. He turned it into a theatre of greenery, carved out of the hill itself. Almost a century later, Giulio Parigi turned it into the walled amphitheatre you see now, and finished in 1634.

[warmly] And then the Medici threw parties here. Picture the tiers packed with spectators, the grand ducal family watching in state, and in the arena below, the kind of spectacle Florence did better than anyone. Music, dancing, costumes, painted stage machinery rolling across the grass. For the Medici, a show like this was a statement. It told every visiting ambassador exactly how rich, and how civilised, this little state was.

[quietly] The shows didn't last forever. The last great performance here was in 1739, to welcome a new ruler, Francis Stephen of Lorraine, after the Medici line had died out. Not long after, the seats were planted over, and the music stopped.

[pause] Now the centrepiece. The obelisk in the middle is the oldest thing in this garden by a very long way. It was carved in Egypt for the pharaoh Ramesses the Second, more than three thousand years ago. The emperor Domitian brought it to Rome for a temple of Isis. A Medici cardinal bought it for his villa in Rome, and in 1790 it finally came here. The great red granite basin at its foot came from the same villa, and joined it fifty years later.

[warmly] So in one view, you've got an Egyptian obelisk, a Medici villa, a Renaissance quarry, and a Baroque theatre. The path climbing the hill beyond the obelisk leads to the Neptune Fountain.
