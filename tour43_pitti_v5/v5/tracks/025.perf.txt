[quietly] Most painters who took on this story painted the killing itself, the sword coming down. Artemisia Gentileschi paints the minute after.

In the old story, the widow Judith slips into an enemy camp and beheads its general, Holofernes, to save her town. Here she and her maid, Abra, are still inside his tent, in the dark. His head is half-bundled into a basket between them. And they've both stopped, turned toward something off to one side, a sound we can't hear. [pause] You're watching two women a heartbeat from being caught.

Look at the sword. Holofernes' own heavy blade rests back against Judith's shoulder, its edge close to her bare neck. It's a reminder of exactly what she's just done, and of what will happen to her if the camp wakes. But look at her grip, and at her face. This isn't panic, it's control. The light is Caravaggio's kind of light, one hard source and deep shadow, with the two faces lifted out of the black. The steadiness, though, is Artemisia's own.

So is the partnership. Judith's hand rests on Abra's shoulder. Painters often made the maid old, or frightened, or barely there. Not here, though. Abra isn't simply a servant doing a job. She's an accomplice, and the two women hold the danger between them.

[warmly] It's worth knowing who painted it. Artemisia came to Florence from Rome as a very young woman. She became the first woman admitted to the city's Academy of Drawing, in a profession that barely let women through the door. She worked for Grand Duke Cosimo the Second. She came back to Judith again and again across her life. The Uffizi has her version of the beheading itself.

This is the quieter, tenser sequel, where nobody screams and nobody moves. [long pause] They just listen.
