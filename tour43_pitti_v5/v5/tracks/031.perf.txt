[lightly] This is the painting Raphael walked away from.

He began it around fifteen-oh-seven, for the Dei family's chapel in the church of Santo Spirito, here in this same part of Florence. It was his first major Florentine commission, and his only big altarpiece for the city. The Virgin sits enthroned beneath a great canopy, the baldacchino that gives the picture its name, with the Christ Child on her lap. Four saints stand around her: Peter and Bernard, James and Augustine. At the foot of the throne, two small angels read from a scroll.

Look at how calmly the figures fill the space. You can feel Raphael learning from the painters around him in Florence. Leonardo's soft modelling and his pyramid of figures. Fra Bartolomeo's grand, still architecture. He was in his mid-twenties, taking in everything the city could teach him.

[pause] And then he left. In fifteen-oh-eight, Pope Julius the Second called him to Rome, to paint the papal apartments. Raphael went, and the altarpiece stayed behind, unfinished. It never reached Santo Spirito.

Instead it began a long journey. Raphael's friend Baldassarre Turini bought it in Florence for his family's chapel in the cathedral of Pescia, a small town in Tuscany. Nearly two centuries later, in sixteen ninety-seven, the Medici Grand Prince Ferdinando bought it, and had the panel enlarged with a strip along the top. Look up there and you're looking at the prince's addition, not Raphael's.

[warmly] So this picture is a hinge. On one side is the young Raphael of the small Florentine pictures you've just met, La Gravida and the Granduca. On the other is the Raphael who, within a few years, would be painting the School of Athens in the Vatican. The Baldacchino was among the last things he worked on in Florence, and his most ambitious Florentine picture. [quietly] Stand in front of it, and you're standing at the turn.

Last in this room, look for a pair of matching portraits, a husband and his young wife. They're Agnolo and Maddalena Doni.
