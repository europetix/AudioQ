[curious] Nobody knows how these two little panels reached Florence. But we do know where they were in 1639. An inventory of that year lists them in the Chapel of Relics here in the palace, among the treasures of Grand Duke Ferdinando the Second and his wife Vittoria. That makes them the earliest icons recorded in Florence. [pause] They waited almost four centuries for a museum of their own. A diplomatic gift is the usual guess, but nobody has found the document that proves it.

Both came from workshops in Moscow. The Mother of God was painted in the last quarter of the fifteen hundreds. The Beheading is a little younger, from around the turn of the century. Both were dressed in precious metal, gilded silver, fine filigree and touches of enamel, laid over the painted surface.

Start with the Beheading, which tells the story in several scenes at once. First, a soldier leads John out of prison. Then he waits, bound, for the sword. And his head already lies in a golden basin. [quietly] Look for a small tree with an axe at its root. It recalls John's own warning, that the axe is laid to the root of the trees. Nothing here shouts, because the story is told as something finished, and holy.

Now the other icon. Its title comes from a hymn, All creatures rejoice in you. Mary sits on a throne with the Child, who raises his hand in blessing. Angels pray around her, inside a church, with paradise opening beyond. [pause] Below, look at the ranks of the righteous, lined up like a choir. Two saints lead them, John the Baptist on the left, and on the right John of Damascus, who wrote the hymn. *The poet is in the picture.*

[warmly] So the same John appears twice, in two small panels that were in this palace before Cosimo the Third was even born. When you're ready, walk through to the central hall, the largest room here, with an altar at one end. That's the Palatine Chapel. Play its track there.
