[lightly] If you've been reading the ceilings, here's a small surprise. You've been reading the story backwards.

Under the Medici, a guest crossed these five Planet Rooms in the opposite direction. He began in the Room of Venus and ended in the Room of Saturn. The ceilings told the education of a young prince in that order. First pleasure, then learning, then war, then the crown. Today's route runs the other way. So you've just left the crowned prince in the throne room, and stepped back onto the battlefield.

Look up. Pietro da Cortona painted this ceiling in the mid-sixteen-forties, and it is not a victory parade. Mars sits at the centre, sword raised. Around him, bodies tumble, weapons clash, and smoke rolls across the sky. [pause] Cortona doesn't make war glorious. He makes it violent and confused. The lesson for the prince was a hard one. One day he would have to fight, and fighting costs.

[warmly] The walls carry the same argument. This is the room of Peter Paul Rubens, the Flemish painter who was also a diplomat, and who spent eight years in Italy as a young man. His two paintings here come from different decades of his life. One is a quiet room full of friends. The other is war itself.

Look out, too, for a Titian portrait of a young man in black, known as the English Gentleman. He's usually counted among this room's treasures, though he has also hung next door, in the Room of Apollo.

Begin with the smaller Rubens, four men gathered around a table, far calmer than anything else in here. It's called the Four Philosophers. Play its track in front of it.
