[conspiratorial] Somewhere in this crowd, the painter has hidden himself. Keep an eye out for an armed man with a bandaged head. We'll come back to him.

The story is from July 1343. Walter of Brienne, the Duke of Athens, ruled Florence, and Florence had had enough of him. Stefano Ussi took the scene straight from Machiavelli's Florentine Histories. We're inside the Bargello palace, and the duke is signing away his power.

[pause] Look at the faces around him. Behind him stands his councillor, Cerretieri Visdomini, grim as a closed door. On the left, calm and composed, are Archbishop Acciaioli and the noblemen who plotted against him. From the right, a soldier gestures urgently. And through the window, the city is already celebrating.

Now look at the size of it. More than three metres high and almost four and a half metres wide. Ussi built it up from drawings and sketches, and some of the figures were modelled by his friends.

[curious] But this isn't really a painting about 1343. Ussi painted it in 1860, and to Florentines that year, the subject was a mirror. Tuscany had just seen off its own ruler, the Lorraine grand duke Leopold the Second, the same man who was given Napoleon's marble head in the room you've just left. Ussi meant the old story as a tribute to that new one, the end of rule by foreign princes.

The painting was a triumph. It won first prize at the National Exhibition in Florence, and a gold medal at the Paris Exhibition a few years later.

[long pause] [quietly] Now find him. Among the armed men, there's one with his head bandaged, as if he'd been in the fight himself. That's Ussi. He didn't just paint the crowd that threw out a tyrant. He put himself in it.

When you're ready, walk on through Room four to Room five, where the grand history paintings carry on.
