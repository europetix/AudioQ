[warmly] Imagine coming home after twenty years away. That's the story on this ceiling. Ulysses, or Odysseus as the Greeks called him, spent ten years fighting at Troy and ten more trying to sail home. When he finally reached his island, he found his wife Penelope still faithful, his son grown, and his house overrun by suitors.

The ceiling was painted by Gaspero Martellini after Napoleon's fall, and the timing is the point. Tuscany's grand duke, Ferdinando the Third, had just come back to Florence after the Napoleonic years had kept him away. [pause] [amused] A rightful ruler, returning to reclaim his house. You can see why the subject appealed. It's a political claim, dressed up as a Greek myth.

Now come down to the walls. They hold a strong group of sixteenth-century paintings, including an altarpiece by Andrea del Sarto, known as the Gambassi Altarpiece. But the painting most people come for is a Raphael, from his first years in Rome. It's called the Madonna dell'Impannata.

To find it, look for a Virgin and Child with two women saints, one older and one younger, and the infant John the Baptist below them. Behind the group, an archway opens onto a softly lit window. That window gives the painting its name. Its track is best heard right in front of it.
