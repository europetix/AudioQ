[warmly] At the end of January, fifteen-oh-four, a Florentine wedding joined money to a great name. He was Agnolo Doni, a rich cloth merchant, about thirty years old. She was Maddalena Strozzi, from one of the noblest families in the city. She was about fourteen.

And Agnolo wanted portraits. He went to a young painter from Urbino who had recently arrived in town, and ordered a matching pair. Raphael painted them as a set, and they were once hinged together, so they opened and closed like a book.

Look at Maddalena first. Her pose may seem familiar. The museum points out links to Leonardo's Mona Lisa so close that Raphael seems to have studied it, here in Florence. [pause] But look at the difference. Leonardo melts every edge into soft haze. Raphael makes every shape and every colour sharp and clear.

[curious] Then look at her jewel. It's a gold pendant with a mount shaped like a unicorn, set with a ruby, an emerald and a sapphire, and finished with a pearl. On a bride of fourteen, that's quite a statement. Across from her is Agnolo, the merchant who ordered and paid for both panels.

Now the part that's easy to miss. The hinges once let people turn the panels and see the backs, because the backs are painted too. If they're on view, find them. On Agnolo's is the Flood. On Maddalena's is what came after, the survivors Deucalion and Pyrrha bringing humanity back to life. [quietly] A disaster on his side, and a new beginning on hers. It's hard not to read that as a wish for a marriage.

More than three centuries later, in eighteen twenty-six, Grand Duke Leopold the Second bought the pair from the couple's heirs. They went into the picture gallery he was building here at the Pitti.
