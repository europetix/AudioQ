[quietly] She's crying. Come close and you'll see the tears, caught in her eyes, painted as clear as glass.

In the Gospels, Mary Magdalene is the first witness to the Resurrection. Over the centuries, legend added a great deal more. It merged her with the sinful woman who anointed Christ's feet. It said she spent her last years as a hermit in a cave in France, doing penance, with only her long hair to cover her. Almost none of that is in the Bible. But by Titian's day, the picture was fixed. The penitent Magdalene, her hair her only garment, her eyes turned to heaven.

Titian probably painted this one in Venice in the early fifteen-thirties, for Francesco Maria della Rovere, the Duke of Urbino. Look at what he does with the hair. It falls over her shoulders in a cloak of copper and gold, catching the light as it goes. [pause] And down at the bottom left, she holds a jar, the ointment jar from the anointing story. Look at it closely. Titian has signed his name on it.

[wry] Now, the obvious thing. The hair is there to cover her, and that's what makes this a devotional picture. It's also exactly what makes us look. Titian knew that perfectly well. Penitence and beauty, in the same brushstroke.

It was a hit. When the writer Giorgio Vasari saw it at the court of Urbino in fifteen forty-eight, he called it a rare thing. Titian and his workshop copied it again and again. This version is generally considered the earliest of the type.

[warmly] And here's how it reached Florence. In sixteen thirty-one, it came with the inheritance of Vittoria della Rovere, part of the great Urbino collection. Keep that name in mind. You'll meet it again in the next room.
