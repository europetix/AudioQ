[curious] Most paintings leave us guessing what the artist meant. For this one, we have his explanation, in his own words.

It began with a favour between friends. Justus Sustermans was a Flemish painter working in Florence as court painter to the Medici. In sixteen thirty-seven he asked Rubens for a painting. Rubens sent it with a letter, dated the twelfth of March sixteen thirty-eight. In it he went through the canvas figure by figure, telling his friend exactly what each one meant. Very few paintings come with the painter's own key.

So let's read it with him. At the centre, Mars strides out in armour, sword raised. Venus, the goddess of love, clings to him and tries to hold him back. He's tearing himself away. Ahead of him, the Fury Alecto urges him on with her torch.

[pause] Now look down, under their feet. There's a mother with a child at her breast, an architect with his drawings and his compass, and a musician with a broken lute. Family, building, music. Everything that peace makes possible, trampled.

On the far left, a woman in black raises her arms to the sky. That's Europe, in mourning. Near her stands the temple of Janus, with its doors flung open. In ancient Rome those doors were shut in peacetime, and opened for war.

[quietly] Rubens knew exactly which war he meant. The Thirty Years' War had been tearing central Europe apart for almost twenty years. He had spent years as a diplomat, working for peace. Now, near the end of his life, he made the argument in paint instead.

And here's the part people often get wrong. This was not a gift to a grand duke. It was a painter's picture, made for a fellow painter. It only entered the Medici collections in sixteen ninety-one, when Grand Prince Ferdinando bought it.

[long pause] War isn't glorious here. It's simply what the painting shows.

Next, something far quieter. Look for Titian's portrait of a young man in black, the English Gentleman. If he isn't on these walls, you'll find him in the next room, the Room of Apollo.
