[curious] Four men sit around a table in this painting. When Rubens painted it, two of them were already dead.

It looks like a meeting of scholars. It's really a family picture. Rubens painted it in Antwerp around sixteen eleven, a few years after he came home from Italy. Every man in it was tied to the others by friendship, by teaching, or by blood.

Start at the far left. The man standing a little apart is Rubens himself. He wasn't a scholar, and he paints himself as a witness rather than a member of the circle. Beside him sits his older brother Philip, a brilliant Latin scholar. Next, at the open book, is Justus Lipsius, the great Flemish humanist and the most influential teacher of his generation. And on the right is Joannes Woverius, another of Lipsius's former students.

[pause] Now the turn. Lipsius had died in sixteen-oh-six. [quietly] And Philip died in Antwerp in the summer of sixteen eleven, just around the time this was painted. So this calm conversation is partly a memorial. Two of the four men were gone before the paint was dry.

Look above them. The marble bust on the shelf was believed, in Rubens's day, to show Seneca, the Roman Stoic philosopher. Lipsius spent years editing Seneca's writings. So the bust presides over the table almost like a fifth member of the group. Beside it stand a few tulips. Flowers that open and fade so quickly are often read as a gentle reminder of how short life is.

[warmly] Take a moment with the faces. Nobody is posing for posterity. They're listening to each other, the way old friends do. Rubens has painted grief here, but it isn't bitter. It's four men in one room, kept together the only way a painter could.

When you're ready, look for the other Rubens, much larger and much louder. It's called the Consequences of War.
