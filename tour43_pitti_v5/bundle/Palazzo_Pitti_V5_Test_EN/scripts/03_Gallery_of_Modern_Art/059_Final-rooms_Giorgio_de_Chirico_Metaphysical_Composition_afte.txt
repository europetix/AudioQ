Is this a copy? Giorgio de Chirico would have enjoyed you asking.

In 1914, De Chirico painted a picture called The Song of Love. A classical head of Apollo. Beside it, a red rubber glove. A ball, balanced where no ball should balance, and far off, the outline of a steam train. That painting is now in New York, at the Museum of Modern Art. The Surrealists, Breton and Dalí among them, revered his early work.

Then De Chirico did something unexpected. He turned his back on that early style. And later he began repainting his own early masterpieces, again and again, blurring the line between original and copy.

This is one of them. He painted it sometime between 1950 and 1960, decades after the first. The museum calls it a faithful replica. Faithful, but not identical. The outline of the train is a little different, and there are dotted lines on the ball.

Now look at the two main objects. The head is a statue, solid and calm. The glove is soft, empty, collapsed. They're both shells. Neither one has anyone inside it. The museum reads this Apollo as standing for life and death at once. And the glove, an everyday thing pulled out of its everyday world, becomes the strangest object in the picture. Nothing is where it belongs, and the space around them doesn't quite add up.

It came to the gallery in 1964, a gift from the collector Leone Ambron.

So which is the real Song of Love? The one in New York, or this one, by the same hand? I suspect De Chirico's answer was both.
