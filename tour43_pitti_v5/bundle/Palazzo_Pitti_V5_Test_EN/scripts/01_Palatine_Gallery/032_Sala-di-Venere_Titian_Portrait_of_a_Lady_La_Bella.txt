We know exactly what she wore. We just don't know who she was.

The man who bought it never said. He was Francesco Maria della Rovere, the Duke of Urbino, and he acquired it from Titian in Venice in the later fifteen-thirties. When he wrote to his agent in Venice to hurry the painting along, he didn't give her a name. He called it simply that portrait of the woman wearing the blue dress.

So who is she? Most scholars now think she isn't anyone in particular. She's an ideal, a picture of perfect feminine beauty, which was a favourite subject at the Italian courts. Even her name came later. She was only called La Bella, the Beautiful One, in the late seventeen-hundreds.

But the dress is very real. Look at the blue damask, worked with fine gold embroidery. Sleeves of burgundy velvet, slashed so that puffs of white fabric show through. A fur draped over her right hand, with a kind of casual elegance. Then look at her face. Honey-coloured hair in knotted braids. Pale cheeks touched with red. And dark eyes, with a gaze that is very much awake.

Now remember the Magdalene, back in the Room of Apollo. Same painter, and very probably the same patron, the Duke of Urbino. And the same journey. In sixteen thirty-one, both paintings came to Florence together, in Vittoria della Rovere's collection. One woman in tears and penitence, the other in blue damask and perfect composure. Two ideals, from one duke's collection.

She may never have existed. But on this whole long walk, nobody is better dressed.

That's the end of the Palatine Gallery. The way out leads through the Sala delle Nicchie to the entrance atrium. From there, take the stairs or the lift up to the second floor, for the Gallery of Modern Art. The Royal Apartments start from this same atrium, and the guide brings you back here for them on your way down.
