This is the face of the most feared writer in sixteenth-century Italy. And he didn't think much of the portrait.

Pietro Aretino was a poet, a satirist and a pamphleteer. He made his living partly by praising the powerful, and partly by threatening to ruin their reputations if they didn't pay. He was also one of Titian's closest friends. In Venice, the two of them and the sculptor Jacopo Sansovino formed the inner circle of the city's artistic life.

Aretino ordered this portrait himself, early in fifteen forty-five. That October he sent it as a gift to Duke Cosimo de' Medici, here in Florence. That's how it came to the Medici.

Look at how it's painted. The face is finished, alert and watchful. The long beard, the proud set of the shoulders, the heavy gold chain. But the great red cloak that fills most of the canvas is painted with astonishing freedom. Broad strokes, folds and reflections suggested rather than spelled out.

Aretino noticed. In a letter, he grumbled that if he'd paid Titian more coins, the strokes would have been brighter, softer and more solid. In other words, you get the cloak you pay for.

But here's the turn. What Aretino called unfinished is exactly what we now treasure. It's Titian's free, sketching style, where the paint stops describing cloth and simply becomes it. Stand close and you see only strokes. Step back and a living man fills the frame.

Now turn and look back across the room at the Concert. Same painter, about thirty-five years apart. There, everything is soft and hushed. Here, the brush moves fast and doesn't apologise.

The sharpest pen in Italy, painted by the friend who knew him best.
