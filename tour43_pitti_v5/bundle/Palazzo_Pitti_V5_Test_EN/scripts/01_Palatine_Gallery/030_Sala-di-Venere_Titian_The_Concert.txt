Something has just happened in this painting. We're never told what.

Three men are making music. In the middle, a man in a dark blue cloak trimmed with fur sits at a spinet, a small keyboard instrument. His hands are still on the keys. Behind him stands a monk in the black and white of the Dominicans, a viola da gamba at his side. The monk has reached out and touched the player's shoulder.

And the player has turned his head. He isn't looking at us. He's looking at the monk. The two of them share a glance, and whatever passes between them, it isn't for us.

On the left stands the third man, a young singer. He wears a feathered cap, which in Titian's Venice was a coveted accessory, a sign of boldness and virility. He's dressed for attention. And yet he's the one left outside the moment, like us.

For centuries, this painting carried another name. When Cardinal Leopoldo de' Medici bought it in sixteen fifty-four, it was sold as a work by Giorgione. He was the mysterious Venetian painter who died young, around fifteen ten, and whose few pictures changed Venetian painting. You can see why people thought so. The warm, smoky light. The story that never explains itself. Today, though, scholars unanimously give it to Titian, painted when he was a young man, in Giorgione's world and very nearly in his shadow.

We don't know who these three men were, or what they were playing. People have proposed many readings, and none of them has won.

What we have is the moment. The hand on the shoulder. The music stopped mid-phrase. Two men who understand each other, and a third who is just a little too late. Five centuries on, we're still standing where the singer stands.
