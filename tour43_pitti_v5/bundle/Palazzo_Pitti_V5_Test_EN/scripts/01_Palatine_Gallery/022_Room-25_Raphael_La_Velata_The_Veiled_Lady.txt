There's a mystery in this portrait, and it starts with a man who saw it almost five hundred years ago. Giorgio Vasari, the painter who wrote the first great book of artists' lives, saw her in a Florentine merchant's house. And he wrote that she was the woman Raphael loved all his life. Later tradition gave her a name. Margherita Luti, the baker's daughter. La Fornarina.

It's a wonderful story. And it may not be true.

Look at what she's wearing. That isn't a baker's daughter's dress. The white silk sleeves are slashed and gathered so the fine linen puffs through. There's gold thread on the bodice and a string of beads at her throat. This is a very expensive outfit, and many scholars read it as a commissioned portrait of a young noblewoman.

Then there's the veil, the detail that gives the painting its name. In Raphael's world, a veil over the hair meant one thing. She was married. Which makes the love story a little more complicated.

Here's what isn't in doubt. Raphael painted her in Rome, around 1512 to 1515, when he was about thirty and the most celebrated painter in the city. And a face very like hers turns up again in Rome, in his painting known as La Fornarina, where she's half undressed and wears an armband with Raphael's own name on it. Many see the same eyes and the same mouth. So whoever she was, she was probably more than just another client.

Now watch the silk. Raphael paints white on white, the dress, the veil, the linen, and never lets it go flat. Every fold catches a slightly different light. And her hand, resting on her chest, is half a gesture of modesty, and half the gesture of someone about to speak.

Five centuries on, we still can't be sure of her name. Raphael, I suspect, would have liked it that way.
