The man at the table was probably the most lavish patron in Europe. And the detail everyone talks about in his portrait is a reflection on the knob of his chair.

He's Giovanni de' Medici, son of Lorenzo the Magnificent, Florentine to the bone. In fifteen thirteen he became Pope Leo the Tenth. He's the pope who excommunicated Martin Luther, and he spent on art and pleasure on a grand scale. On becoming pope, he's supposed to have said, God has given us the papacy, let us enjoy it. Or so the chroniclers report. Whether he said it or not, he lived it.

Raphael painted him in fifteen eighteen, two years before his own death. Behind the pope stand two cardinals, both his cousins. On the left is Giulio de' Medici, the future Pope Clement the Seventh. On the right, his hand on the pope's chair, is Luigi de' Rossi, who died the following year.

Now the table. The pope has an illuminated manuscript open in front of him, and he's just lifted a gold-rimmed magnifying glass from the page. It may hint that he was short-sighted. It certainly says he loved books. That manuscript is real. It was made in Naples in the fourteenth century, and it survives today in a museum in Berlin.

Now go closer still, to the golden knob on the back of the chair, behind his shoulder. In its polished surface, Raphael painted a tiny reflection of the window in the room. Vasari, writing about this picture a generation later, singled out that detail as proof of what Raphael could do.

For a long time, some scholars thought the two cardinals had been added later by Raphael's workshop. Then Florence's great conservation laboratory spent more than two years restoring it. Infrared scans showed the whole group drawn in underneath, in Raphael's own underdrawing, and the pope's figure reworked freehand.

A pope, his family's future, and a window caught in a golden ball. Raphael was showing off. He had earned it.
