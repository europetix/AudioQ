She's been called La Gravida, the pregnant woman, since the nineteenth century. We don't actually know that she was.

Look at her hands. One rests on a ledge, holding a glove, and the other rests on her rounded belly. That single gesture is where her name comes from. Some nineteenth-century viewers looked at it and decided she was expecting a child, and the title stuck. Maybe she was, or maybe they simply saw what they expected to see. The painting doesn't say.

Nor does it tell us who she is. Raphael painted her around fifteen-oh-seven, in the last years of his time in Florence, when he was in his mid-twenties. Scholars have suggested names over the years: a woman of the Bufalini family, perhaps, or Emilia Pia, a daughter of the Pio family. None of the ideas has held up, and for now, as the museum puts it, she's still a mystery.

What she wore, we know exactly. A white gown, a yellow bodice with a black sash, and wide sleeves of red damask. Her hair is caught up in a tightly woven net. There are rings on her fingers, and a gold chain whose pendant slips out of sight into her neckline. This is a well-off woman, dressed with care for an important picture.

And then there's her face. She's set against a plain black ground, with no landscape, no symbols, no story to hide behind. Her look is calm and direct. She knows she's being looked at, and she looks straight back, and Raphael lets that be enough.

So here she is, five centuries on. Raphael gave her everything except a name of her own. Later viewers gave her one, and it may not even be true.
