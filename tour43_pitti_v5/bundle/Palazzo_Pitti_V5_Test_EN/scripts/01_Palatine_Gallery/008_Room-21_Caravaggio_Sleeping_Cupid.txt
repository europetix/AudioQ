This little painting reached Florence while the man who painted it was on the run.

Caravaggio painted it in 1608, on the island of Malta. Two years earlier he had killed a man in Rome and fled with a price on his head. In Malta he was welcomed and admitted to the Knights of Malta. One of his fellow knights was a Florentine, Fra Francesco dell'Antella, and this Cupid was painted for him.

Then everything went wrong again. Caravaggio was thrown into prison there, escaped to Sicily, and went back to life as a fugitive. Meanwhile, by the summer of 1609, dell'Antella had sent the painting home to his brother's house in Florence. It caused a stir. Poets wrote sonnets about it. So while Florence admired his Cupid, Caravaggio was still on the run in the south. He died the following year, at Porto Ercole on the Tuscan coast. The Medici acquired the painting later in the century.

Now look closely, because this is not quite the sweet picture its title promises. The subject is an old one, Love asleep, his weapons laid aside. But this child is too thin. You can see his ribs. His skin is pale, almost grey, with a greenish tinge in the shadows. His mouth hangs open and his head has fallen back. Some have wondered whether Caravaggio painted a sick child, or even a dead one. Nobody knows.

Look at the light. One hard beam picks out his face and chest, and the rest falls away into darkness. This is Caravaggio's great trick, the one that changed European painting, here in a single quiet image.

Love, exhausted and defenceless. Standing where you are, you're close enough to wake him.

The Sala della Stufa is next on the route. Its track starts there.
