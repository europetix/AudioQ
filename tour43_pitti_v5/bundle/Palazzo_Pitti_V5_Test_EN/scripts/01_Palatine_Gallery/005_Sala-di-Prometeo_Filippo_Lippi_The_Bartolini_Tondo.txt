The man who painted this was a friar. Filippo Lippi belonged to the Carmelite order, and he was one of the great storytellers of early Renaissance Florence. Around 1452 he painted this round panel for Leonardo Bartolini, probably to hang in his family's house.

A few years later, this same friar would run off with a young nun, Lucrezia Buti. Their son, Filippino, grew up to be a great painter in his own right. But all that was still ahead of him. What he made here is a calm, devout picture of a family.

Start in front. The Virgin sits on a throne with the Child on her knees. She's picking seeds out of a pomegranate and holding them out to him. A pomegranate, packed with seeds, stood for fertility. It also hinted at the suffering that lay ahead of him.

Now let your eye travel back, into the rooms behind her. On the left is the birth of the Virgin herself. Her mother, Saint Anne, lies in bed, and women bustle around to care for her and the newborn, and to bring gifts. This is a fifteenth-century Florentine bedroom. It's how a wealthy family really celebrated a birth.

On the right, up on a staircase, Anne meets her husband, Joachim. Tradition linked that meeting to the conception of the Virgin.

So look at what Lippi has done. There are three moments in one circle. The Virgin's parents meeting. The Virgin being born. And the Virgin, grown up, holding her own son. A generation after Brunelleschi taught Florence how to build space with perspective, Lippi uses those receding rooms as little stages, one story to each.

The deeper you look into the painting, the further back in time you go.

Stay in the Sala di Prometeo and look for a Raphael: a young woman against a black background, one hand resting on her belly. That's La Gravida. If she isn't on these walls, she has gone back to her old place in the Sala dell'Iliade, so keep her track for that room, and walk on through a few smaller rooms to the Sala di Ulisse.
