This enormous house was built for a man who wasn't a prince. Luca Pitti was a Florentine banker, and in the middle of the fourteen-hundreds he began a palace here, on the slope below the hills. Florentines have long told the story that he meant to outshine the Medici. Some say Brunelleschi drew the first design. Nobody can prove either.

Here's what is certain. Within a century the Pitti family had run short of money, and they sold the house to the very family it was meant to outshine. Around 1550 the Medici moved their court in. And from then until 1919, it belonged to whoever ruled Tuscany. The Medici first, then the Habsburg-Lorraine grand dukes, and finally the kings of a united Italy. With a short Napoleonic interruption in the middle.

Take a moment with the front before you go in. More than two hundred metres of rough, oversized stone, and the same round arch repeated across the whole width. It was built to look as if nothing could ever move it.

Inside, the museums are stacked by floor. One floor up is the Palatine Gallery, the Medici paintings in the old state rooms. The museum calls it the greatest concentration of Raphael's work anywhere in the world. The Imperial and Royal Apartments are on the same floor. They're seen on a short visit led by museum staff, starting from the Palatine entrance hall. This guide takes you there on your way back down from the second floor, with a track to play while you wait. On the second floor are the Gallery of Modern Art and the Museum of Fashion and Costume. The Russian icons and the Palatine Chapel open off the courtyard on the ground floor. And the gardens behind the palace have a section of their own, so you can walk them first or last.

Once you're through the central doorway, you'll be in the Ammannati Courtyard. The main staircase and the lifts are on its right-hand side. Go up to the first floor, to the entrance of the Palatine Gallery. We'll start again there, in the first room.
