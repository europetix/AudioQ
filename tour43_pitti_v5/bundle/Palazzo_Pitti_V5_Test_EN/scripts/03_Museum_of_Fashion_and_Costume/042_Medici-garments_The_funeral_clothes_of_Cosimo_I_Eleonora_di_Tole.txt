These clothes spent centuries in the dark, on the people who were buried in them. By rights, they shouldn't have survived at all.

Cosimo the First was the first Medici grand duke of Tuscany. Eleonora di Toledo was his wife, the daughter of the Viceroy of Naples, mother of eleven children, and one of the most powerful women in Italy. Don Garzia was one of their younger sons, born in 1547.

In the late autumn of 1562, Eleonora travelled into the Maremma, the marshy coast of southern Tuscany, with Garzia and his older brother, Cardinal Giovanni. All three fell ill. Giovanni died first, in November. Garzia followed in December. And Eleonora died five days after him.

People talked, of course. A rumour went round, and lasted for centuries, that Cosimo had killed his own son in a rage, and that grief had killed Eleonora. It's almost certainly false. Doctors' reports from the time point to malaria, and studies of Eleonora's bones show she also had tuberculosis.

Cosimo, Eleonora and Garzia were all laid in the Medici vault at San Lorenzo, dressed in the clothes you see here. The tombs were opened in the nineteenth century and again in the twentieth. What came out was badly decayed, and it took long and painstaking restoration before these garments could go on show.

Now, Eleonora's dress. You may have heard that it's the very dress she wears in Bronzino's famous portrait of her, in the Uffizi. It's a lovely legend. It isn't true. The portrait dress and the burial dress are two different gowns.

But don't be disappointed. The painted dress belongs to a picture. This one belonged to her, and she wore it on her last journey. Look at the cut of the bodice, the way the fabric was shaped to a real woman's body. More than four hundred and fifty years later, it still holds the outline of the person inside it.
