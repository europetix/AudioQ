You've just seen the famous grotto. This is the little one it grew from. The Grotta di Madama is the oldest grotto in the garden, built between 1553 and 1555 under Davide Fortini, the son-in-law of the garden's first designer, Tribolo.

It's usually closed, opened only on special occasions, so you'll most likely see it from outside. Look at the front. A wall of artificial rock, set inside a neat stone frame with a gable on top, and a small doorway framed in white marble in the middle. Wild nature, held in a classical picture frame.

Inside, the walls are rough like sponge, with stalactites hanging down and sculpted animals, goats and rams among them, emerging from the rock around a basin. Bandinelli carved the central goat. The vault was painted with grotesques.

Why did a Renaissance garden need fake caves at all? The answer goes back to ancient Rome. Roman villas had nymphaea, cool shaded grottoes with water, where the nymphs of springs and woods were imagined to live. Florentines, rediscovering antiquity, wanted their own. A grotto was a retreat on a hot day, a piece of theatre, and an argument about nature and art, all at once.

The name came later. According to the museum, the name probably comes from Maria Maddalena of Austria, wife of Cosimo the Second, whose Habsburg arms were on the grotto's wooden door.

Fortini made the modest first attempt. Thirty years on, Buontalenti took the same idea and let it run wild just below you. From here, make your way up and across to the great amphitheatre behind the palace. Play its track from the path at its edge.
