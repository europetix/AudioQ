Behind the grandest house in Florence, the Medici kept a whole hillside. The Boboli Gardens cover more than thirty hectares, terraces and avenues, fountains and woods, climbing away from the palace toward the old city walls.

It began with a woman. In 1549 Eleonora di Toledo, the Spanish-born wife of Cosimo the First, set the work in motion, with Niccolò Tribolo as her designer. Tribolo died the following year. Ammannati, who built this courtyard, took over, and then Buontalenti. For four centuries, Medici, Lorraine and Savoy rulers kept adding. The result became a model for court gardens across Europe. Eleonora's own burial clothes, by the way, are on display in the palace, in the Costume Museum on the second floor.

An honest word before you set off. The garden is going through a long restoration programme called Boboli Twenty-Thirty. You'll meet scaffolding and fenced-off areas along the way, but most of what's behind the fences can still be seen from the paths.

The design hangs on one idea, the axis. From here a single straight line climbs the hill, past the Artichoke Fountain above you, up through the amphitheatre, past the Neptune Fountain, to a colossal statue at the top. Almost everything in the central garden is arranged around it.

But the first stop is off to the side. Head to your left, to the east, toward the little square of the Bacchino fountain. There's a small, round figure riding a turtle there, and he's well worth meeting. Play his track when you're beside him.
