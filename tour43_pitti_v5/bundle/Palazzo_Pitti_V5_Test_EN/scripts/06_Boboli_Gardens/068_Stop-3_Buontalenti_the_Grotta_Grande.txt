This started life as a water tank. In the 1550s the Medici needed a reservoir for a new aqueduct, and they built one here. Then Vasari gave it a sober front of columns. And then, a generation later, Bernardo Buontalenti got hold of it and turned it into the strangest building in the garden.

Look at the façade. Two niches hold Apollo and Ceres, by Baccio Bandinelli, gods of light and of the harvest, all order and calm. Above and around them, the stone starts to melt. It drips. It swells into rocky crusts, as if the building were slowly turning back into a cave.

That was the whole idea. Inside, if the gate is open today, the first chamber is worked to look like a wet cave. Stalactites, sponge stone and shells, with shepherds and animals half emerging from the rock. In its four corners stand four of Michelangelo's unfinished Prisoners. Or rather, they did. The Medici set the originals here in 1585, figures struggling out of raw marble, placed in rock that only pretended to be raw. In 1908 they left for the Accademia Gallery, and what stands here now are replicas.

So where does nature end and art begin? The cave looks natural, and every inch of it is made. Florentines of the time loved that question, and this is their answer in stone and water.

Further in, the second chamber holds a marble of Theseus carrying off Helen, by Vincenzo de' Rossi. The third holds Giambologna's Venus, a young woman stepping from her bath, with satyrs climbing the basin to spy on her.

Even from outside, you can feel the joke. A god's calm face, and right above it, the rock beginning to drip. When you're done here, look for the small path that climbs behind and above the grotto. It leads to the Grotta di Madama, the oldest grotto in the garden. Play its track there.
