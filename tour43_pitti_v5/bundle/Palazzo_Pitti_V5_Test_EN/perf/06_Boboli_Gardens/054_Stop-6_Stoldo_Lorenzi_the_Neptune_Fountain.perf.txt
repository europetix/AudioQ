[amused] Florentines have a habit of giving their great sculptures nicknames, and they aren't always kind. Down in the Piazza della Signoria, the big marble Neptune became il Biancone, the big white one, and nobody meant it as a compliment. This one got off lightly. Florentines call it the Fontana del Forcone, the fountain of the big fork. [pause] Look at the trident, and you'll see why.

The bronze is by Stoldo Lorenzi, made between 1565 and 1568 for Cosimo the First. At that point Cosimo was Duke of Florence and Siena, and he wanted more. In 1569 he would become Grand Duke of Tuscany. He was also building a navy, with ambitions in the Mediterranean. So a sea god, high on the hill behind his palace, was a message as much as an ornament.

The basin is fenced off for restoration at the moment, but the figure is easy to see from the path. [curious] Take him in from more than one angle if you can. He stands on a rock, swinging his trident down toward the water, as if about to strike. The beard, the hair and the long, stretched muscles all belong to the late Renaissance style called Mannerism, elegant and a little restless. He isn't a calm classical god. He's caught in motion, almost on stage.

[warmly] And he was put here to be seen from below. Look back down the hill toward the amphitheatre and the palace, and you'll understand the choice. A duke's sea god, standing guard over his garden.

When you're ready, take the path up and to the left, to the east, toward a small pavilion with a domed, turreted roof on the terrace above. That's the Kaffeehaus. Play its track at the edge of its terrace.
