[curious] For decades, these rooms were offices, and hardly anyone looked up at the ceilings. Then they were restored and opened as a museum in 2022, and the seventeenth-century frescoes came back to life. Now Russian icons hang in front of them.

The rooms themselves belong to a wedding. In 1661 the future Grand Duke Cosimo the Third married a French princess, Marguerite Louise d'Orléans, and this ground floor was decorated as the couple's summer apartment. [pause] The icons came by a slower road. A few had belonged to the Medici. Two were in the palace even before the wedding, kept in a chapel of relics by Cosimo's mother, the Grand Duchess Vittoria della Rovere. Most of the rest arrived after the Medici line died out, when the new Habsburg-Lorraine grand dukes kept collecting. Today there are seventy-eight. It's described as the oldest collection of its kind anywhere outside the lands where icons were made.

[quietly] As you walk, notice the size. These are small panels, made for prayer at home or on a journey, not altarpieces for a church. And the gold isn't decoration. In the Orthodox tradition it stands for the light of heaven. The figures inside it are meant to be present with you, not painted at a distance.

Look out for a Mother of God of Tikhvin, signed by its painter, Vasily Gryaznov, and dated 1728.

But start with the earliest icons ever recorded in Florence. The small Beheading of Saint John the Baptist hangs in this first room, and its companion, a Mother of God surrounded by crowds, should be close by. Play their track in front of them.
