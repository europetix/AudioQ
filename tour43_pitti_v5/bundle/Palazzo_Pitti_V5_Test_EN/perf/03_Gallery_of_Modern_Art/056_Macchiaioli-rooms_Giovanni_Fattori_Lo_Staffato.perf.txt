[quietly] There's an Italian cavalry word with no neat English equivalent. Staffato. It means a rider whose foot has caught in the stirrup as he falls, so his own horse drags him, instead of throwing him clear.

That's all this painting shows. And it's enough.

Fattori painted it around 1880. He was in his mid-fifties by then, the established master of the Macchiaioli, and he had been painting Italy's wars for almost twenty years. [pause] He had never fought in one himself.

Look at the horse first. It's in full gallop, running in blind panic, and the saddle is empty. Then follow the line back. The soldier is behind it, on the ground, still in uniform, one foot trapped in the stirrup. His arms trail behind his head. He has no say in where he's going.

Now look for everything that isn't there. No enemy. No officer. No comrade riding to help. No flag, no battle, no reason given. Just a man being dragged across empty, sunbaked ground, and a long dark trail where he's been.

[pause] Notice how little paint Fattori needs for that trail. A few strokes, almost a single gesture, and you feel the whole distance the horse has run.

The new Italy liked its war pictures heroic, full of charges and flags. Fattori gives you the horse, and takes the hero away.

[quietly] He never fought. But I think this might be the most honest war picture in the building.

When you're ready, look for a much smaller picture by Fattori, the Rotonda di Palmieri. It may be close by. It's small enough to walk straight past, so take your time.
