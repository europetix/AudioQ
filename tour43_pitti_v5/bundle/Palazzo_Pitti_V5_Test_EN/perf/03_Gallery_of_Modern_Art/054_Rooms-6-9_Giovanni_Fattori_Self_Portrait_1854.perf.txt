[quietly] He doesn't know yet. That's what makes this small picture worth a long look.

Giovanni Fattori was born in Livorno, on the Tuscan coast. At about twenty-one he moved to Florence to study at the Academy, under Giuseppe Bezzuoli, the painter of that grand entry of a French king back in Room five. By 1854, when he painted this, he had just finished his training. And he still painted the way he'd been taught. Careful brushwork. Restrained colour. A young man being very serious about himself.

[pause] Look at his eyes. There's something watchful in them, almost wary.

Within a few years, Fattori would turn his back on much of what the Academy had taught him. With a group of friends in Florence, he began to build pictures out of patches of colour, and to take his subjects from life. Soldiers. Peasants. Ordinary life in ordinary light. Hostile critics called them the Macchiaioli, the patch painters. It was meant as an insult. The painters kept the name.

He became a great painter of war without ever fighting in one. In 1862 he painted the Italian camp after the Battle of Magenta, which hangs in Room thirteen. A few years later came a tiny panel of women in the shade by the sea at Livorno. And around 1880, a horse dragging a fallen rider. Both of those are in this gallery too, and each has its own story if you come across it.

[warmly] So take one more look at the young man here. Serious, careful, a little stiff. He has no idea he's about to become one of the great Italian painters of his century.

From here, walk on through the portrait and landscape rooms to Room ten, where the Macchiaioli rooms begin in earnest.
