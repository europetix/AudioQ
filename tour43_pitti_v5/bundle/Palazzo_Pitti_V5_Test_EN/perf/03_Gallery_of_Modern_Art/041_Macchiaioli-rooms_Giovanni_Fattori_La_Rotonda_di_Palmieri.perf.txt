[lightly] One of the most loved paintings in this gallery is twelve centimetres high. If you've found it, well done. Plenty of people walk straight past.

Fattori painted it in 1866, at the Bagni Palmieri, a bathing establishment on the seafront at Livorno, his home town. The rotonda of the title was part of the bathing establishment, out over the sea. Over it stretches a long ochre awning for shade. And under the awning, in a row, sit seven women.

They aren't here to swim. They've come to sit out of the sun and take the sea air, fully dressed. Nothing is happening. [pause] That's the subject.

Now lean in. None of the figures is bigger than your fingernail, and yet each one is a person. One leans forward. One sits back. One turns toward the sea. You can't make out a single face. You can read every posture.

[warmly] And look at how it's made. The ochre band of the awning, the blue of the water, the soft greys of the dresses, the strip of coast. Each one is a patch of colour, set down beside the next without blending. This is the macchia at its purest. Fattori was about forty. Monet's Impression, Sunrise was still six years away.

He probably worked from quick studies, then built this up later. Either way, it doesn't feel composed. It feels glimpsed.

[amused] The costume museum on this floor even keeps a woman's garden dress whose shape its curators compare to the dresses worn right here.

When you're ready, follow the numbers on. Rooms fourteen and fifteen bring back the big history paintings, Room sixteen has a bust of Verdi by Gemito, and Room seventeen begins the last stretch of the loop.
