[warmly] In the rooms you're about to visit, three ruling families lived one after another. And none of them ever quite cleared out the one before.

First came the Medici. In the late sixteen hundreds this wing was the apartment of Grand Prince Ferdinando, the brilliant, short-lived son of Cosimo the Third. Then the Lorraine grand dukes, who reworked the rooms from the later eighteenth century into the nineteenth. And last the House of Savoy, kings of a newly united Italy, who moved in when Florence became the capital in 1865. They kept the palace for half a century, before handing it to the state.

[pause] So the visit is fourteen rooms and three layers of taste. Here's what to watch for.

In the Green Room, where you begin, look up at the ceiling canvas by the Neapolitan painter Luca Giordano, an allegory of peace between Florence and Fiesole. In the Throne Room, the Savoy throne waits under a canopy with their coat of arms. The Blue Room is full of faces. Ten Medici princes, cardinals and wives, painted by the Flemish court artist Justus Sustermans between about 1621 and 1645. [quietly] Then the Chapel. It began as Ferdinando's own alcove, and it's the one room where the Medici decoration survives almost intact.

[curious] In the Parrot Room, take a good look at the birds woven into the silk on the walls. Are they really parrots? [pause] Hold that thought. After that come the Queen's rooms, the King's rooms, and finally the King's Antechamber, the old entrance to the private apartment.

Museum staff lead each small group through in about half an hour. Please pause this audio while you're inside, so that you can hear them, and so can everyone else. When you come back out, the story of these rooms continues under After your visit.
