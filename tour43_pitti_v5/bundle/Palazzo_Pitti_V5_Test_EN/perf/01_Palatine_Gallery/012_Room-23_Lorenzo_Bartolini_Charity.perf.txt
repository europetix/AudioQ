[warmly] At first sight, she's the gentlest thing in this room. A young mother, her children, smooth white marble. But this sculpture is making an argument. And the argument is written down.

Lorenzo Bartolini was born in a village near Prato and trained partly in Paris. In his day, the grand style for marble was Canova's: ideal bodies, cool and perfect, modelled on ancient statues. Bartolini went looking for something more natural. He studied real bodies, and he studied the Florentine Renaissance, and he tried to bring the two together.

He designed this group for the chapel of the grand-ducal villa at Poggio Imperiale, and worked on the marble for years afterwards. It was still in his studio in eighteen thirty-five. [pause] Look at how human she is. She isn't a goddess on a pedestal. She's a mother busy with her children.

Charity was an old subject. Traditionally she's a woman surrounded by children, feeding them, sheltering them, giving. Bartolini gives her something else to do. [curious] Walk round until you find the scroll in a child's hands. It's in Latin, and it's a line from the Gospels. Love the Lord your God with all your heart, with all your soul and with all your mind, and your neighbour as yourself.

This mother is making her child study. Charity, for Bartolini, means feeding the mind and the conscience as well as the body. Scholars call her Charity the Educator, partly because she appeared on the title page of a magazine called The Educator.

She has stood in this room since eighteen sixty-one. And she fits it rather well. Up on the ceilings of the Planet Rooms ahead, painters spent years showing a prince being educated by the gods. [warmly] Down here, at eye level, a mother is quietly doing the same job.

One more work in this room, and it's a hard turn from this quiet mother. Look for Artemisia Gentileschi's Judith and her Maidservant: two women in the dark, a severed head, a sword.
