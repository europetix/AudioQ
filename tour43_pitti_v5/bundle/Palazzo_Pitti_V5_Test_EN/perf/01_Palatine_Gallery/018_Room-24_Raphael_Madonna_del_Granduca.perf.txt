[quietly] The darkness around this Madonna is the most famous thing about her. And Raphael didn't paint it.

He painted her around fifteen-oh-six, during his years in Florence, around the same time as La Gravida, which you've just seen. He was in his early twenties and learning fast. From Leonardo he took the soft play of light and shadow. From the Florentine sculptors before him, Donatello and Luca della Robbia among them, he took something else: their countless Madonnas in terracotta and marble. You can feel that in her, almost a statue coming alive.

[pause] Then there's the black. X-rays show that Raphael set her in a room, with a pillar holding up arches, and a glimpse of countryside beyond. At some later date, someone painted all of that out. What's left is a mother and child alone in the dark.

[reverent] Look at what that does. Her eyes are lowered, and she seems sad. The child looks calmly out at you. The museum reads it as love, together with a painful awareness of what lies ahead for him. Without the room around them, there's nowhere else for your eyes to go. It's an accident of history, but the accident is beautiful.

The name came much later. Around eighteen hundred, Ferdinand the Third, Grand Duke of Tuscany, authorised her purchase, and she came to the Pitti. [warmly] He loved her. When Napoleon's armies drove him into exile, he took her with him. When he came back, he kept her in his private rooms, and she went on public view only when he was away. So she became the Madonna del Granduca, the Grand Duke's Madonna.

She has hung here in the Sala di Saturno since eighteen eighty-two.
