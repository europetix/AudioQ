[curious] There's a lovely story about this painting. Raphael is walking through Rome when he sees a peasant woman in a doorway, sitting on a low stool with her child in her arms. And he paints exactly what he saw. [pause] It's a charming legend, and almost certainly invented. The chair gives it away.

You only glimpse the chair, an upright decorated with round knobs. And it's no peasant's stool. It's a chamber chair, the kind reserved for high dignitaries of the papal court, and those knobs may be a nod to the balls on the Medici coat of arms. Many think the painting was made for the Medici pope, Leo the Tenth, whose portrait hangs in this same room. Raphael painted it around fifteen twelve, in his Roman years.

So she's probably not a woman from a doorway. [warmly] But look at how Raphael makes her feel like one. Mary lifts one knee to hold her son and clasps him against her chest. Her cheek rests against his temple, yet her eyes turn out to you. On the right, the young Saint John joins his hands in prayer and gazes at them. Their arms lock together, and the three bodies follow the round edge with perfect ease.

Now the colour. A white turban shot with gold thread, and a scarf of greens and reds. A red sleeve against the Child's yellow tunic, and the deep ultramarine of her dress. And in the glances, a little melancholy, as if she already knows what will happen to this child.

By fifteen eighty-nine it hung in the Tribune of the Uffizi. Later, the Grand Prince Ferdinando brought it to the Pitti and kept it in his bedroom. Napoleon's armies took it to Paris, and it came home after his fall. It became one of Raphael's most copied paintings.

[quietly] The legend gets one thing right. Whoever she was, she doesn't look like a queen of heaven. She looks like a mother, holding on.

Next, look for the portrait of a young woman in wide red sleeves, one hand resting on her belly. That's Raphael's La Gravida.
