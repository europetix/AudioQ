[curious] There isn't a drop of paint on this tabletop. Every colour you see is the natural colour of a stone.

The technique is called commesso fiorentino, Florentine mosaic. Craftsmen sawed hard stones into thin slices and cut each slice to the shape of a petal, a leaf or a fold of cloth. Then they fitted the pieces together so tightly that the joins almost disappear. The Medici took this art so seriously that, back in the fifteen-hundreds, they set up a court workshop just for it, the Opificio delle Pietre Dure. [pause] This table comes from the far end of that long story.

Work on the top began in the 1830s, under Aristodemo Costoli and Giovan Battista Giorgi. It was not a quick job. The bronze base came later still, in the 1850s. It was modelled by a rising sculptor from Siena, Giovanni Dupré, and cast by Clemente Papi. In 1855 the finished table was set up in this room. Four names on the record, and many more hands that nobody wrote down.

[quietly] Now look closely. The centre is usually described as Apollo with the Muses, the goddesses of the arts, who give the table its name. Follow the changes of colour inside a single shape, and you can watch the craftsman choosing stone the way a painter mixes paint. A vein in the stone becomes a fold. A darker patch becomes a shadow.

Then look down. The bronze base carries the Seasons, with small children among them. [amused] They're worth crouching for, if your knees agree.

[warmly] By the time this table was finished, the last of the Medici had been dead for more than a century. Florentine stone inlay was still making wonders for the family that came next.

To the right lies the Volterrano Wing, once a grand duchess's private apartment. Its rooms are often closed for restoration. If they're open, feel free to wander through. Either way, follow the route signs on to the Sala di Prometeo, a room hung with round paintings. Look for Filippo Lippi's large round panel, with tiny figures moving through rooms behind the Virgin, and play its track in front of it.
