[curious] Almost every painting in the rooms ahead once belonged to the Medici. But it wasn't the Medici who hung them here. [pause] It was the family that came after them.

The Habsburg-Lorraine grand dukes created this gallery between the end of the seventeen-hundreds and the early eighteen-hundreds. They chose around five hundred works from the great Medici collections and put them in the palace's state rooms. And they hung them in a deliberately old-fashioned way, the way picture galleries were hung in the sixteen-hundreds. Frame against frame, wall to wall, with sculpture, vases and stone-inlaid tables in between.

[lightly] So don't expect a modern museum, with one painting on a white wall and a long label beside it. Here the rooms are part of the show. Silk on the walls, gilded frames, painted ceilings overhead. You're meant to feel the richness first, and find the masterpieces second.

You begin with this antechamber and the long Gallery of the Statues, lined with classical marble. Then the route runs on through the Castagnoli Room and a chain of smaller rooms. It keeps the five great Planet Rooms, with ceilings by Pietro da Cortona and his pupil Ciro Ferri, for the very end. [pause] Under the Medici, important guests walked those rooms the other way round. So for the last stretch, you'll be following the grand duke's ceremonial route backwards.

Walk the length of the statue gallery to the Castagnoli Room. You'll know it by the great round stone table in the middle. Play its track there.
