 The Grottoes — Grotta di Madama
    WHERE YOU ARE
    At the Grotta di Madama, the small early grotto set into the slope below the larger Buontalenti Grotto, on the
    path up from the Bacchus Gate.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    The oldest grotto in the Boboli, designed by Niccolò Tribolo and built in the 1550s, before Buontalenti's
    larger Grotta Grande. A small artificial cave decorated with sponge stone, stalactites, and sculpted goats and
    animals, built into the hillside. Often gated; viewable through the entrance grille if closed.
    LISTEN FOR
    What a Renaissance grotto was for, why there are several of them in Boboli, and how this small one prepared
    the way for the famous one just above it.

 Before you reach the famous grotto, here's its older, smaller predecessor — and it helps to see them in
 order.

 This is the Grotta di Madama, the oldest grotto in the gardens, designed by Niccolò Tribolo — the same
 architect who laid out the first plan of Boboli — and built in the fifteen fifties. It's small. A single low
 chamber set into the hillside, decorated with sponge stone, artificial stalactites, and sculpted goats and
 animals emerging from the rock. It may well be gated when you arrive, in which case you can look in
 through the grille.

 The question worth asking is why a sixteenth-century garden needed artificial caves at all. The answer
 goes back to ancient Rome. Roman villas had nymphaea — cool, shaded, water-filled grottoes where
 the family escaped the summer heat and where, in myth, the nymphs and minor gods of springs and
 woods were thought to live. Renaissance Florentines, rediscovering antiquity, wanted their own. A
 grotto was a cool retreat on a hot day, a piece of theatrical garden architecture, and a philosophical
 statement about the boundary between nature and human art, all at once.

 Tribolo's Grotta di Madama is the modest first attempt. Thirty years later, Buontalenti would take the
 same idea and turn it into the extraordinary three-chamber Grotta Grande just above you — bigger,
 stranger, far more ambitious. Seeing the small one first lets you watch the idea grow up.

 Climb the path to the Grotta Grande — the Buontalenti Grotto — marked by a deeply carved stone
 façade with statues in two niches. Play the next track when you arrive.
