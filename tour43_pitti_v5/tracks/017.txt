 Raphael — La Velata (The Veiled Lady)
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of Raphael's La Velata, in the Sala di Giove. The painting is medium-sized — about eighty by sixty
    centimetres — and shows a single young woman in three-quarter view.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    An oil-on-panel portrait painted around 1515-1516. A young woman in a white silk dress with sleeves of
    slashed and gathered fabric, a gold-trimmed bodice, and a transparent white veil draped over her hair. She
    wears a single pearl pendant. Her right hand rests across her chest. She looks directly at us.
    LISTEN FOR
    Who she might be, the famous theory that connects this painting to two of Raphael's other portraits, and what
    to look at in her dress.

 She's traditionally identified as Margarita Luti — the woman known as la Fornarina, the baker's
 daughter, who was Raphael's mistress and longtime model during his Roman years. The identification
 isn't certain, but it's been argued for centuries. The same face appears in at least one other Raphael
 portrait — the more famous Fornarina at the Galleria Barberini in Rome, where she's painted with bare
 shoulders, a turban, and a band on her arm bearing Raphael's name. The two paintings are almost
 certainly the same model.

 If she is la Fornarina, the painting is a private declaration — Raphael painting his lover in the most
 expensive clothes he could imagine and giving her the dignity of a noble portrait. If she isn't, she's an
 unidentified Roman gentlewoman, painted by Raphael at the height of his powers, in a manner that
 established the standard for female portraiture for the next two hundred years.

 Now look at the dress. It's the dress that makes the painting. The white silk catches the studio light and
 gives it back in folds, ridges, ripples. The sleeves are slashed in the Roman court fashion of the
 fifteen-tens, showing puffs of fine white linen underneath. The gold-thread embroidery on the bodice
 shimmers but doesn't distract. The veil over her hair is the same white silk — almost the same colour as
 the dress — and it tells you this isn't a wedding portrait. In Italian tradition a married woman covered
 her hair. Whoever she is, she's been deliberately framed as both modest and very visible.

 Look at her right hand on her chest. The pose is calm, slightly defensive, slightly wary — a gesture of
 self-containment. The single pearl pendant rests above her hand, on her heart.

 When you've looked enough, find Andrea del Sarto's Young Saint John the Baptist — a half-length
 figure of a young man with curly hair and a reed cross. Play the next track.
