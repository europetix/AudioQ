 Gallery of Modern Art — Second Floor, A
 Different Century
    WHERE YOU ARE
    At the entrance to the Gallery of Modern Art, on the second floor. Through the windows you can see the
    Boboli Gardens stretching up the hill behind the palace.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    The Gallery of Modern Art occupies thirty rooms on the second floor of the palace — the rooms that the
    Habsburg-Lorraine grand dukes used as their everyday family residence in the early 19th century. The
    collection runs from the late 1700s to the early 1900s, organised chronologically, with Italian neoclassicism,
    romanticism, and the Tuscan Macchiaioli school as the major movements.
    LISTEN FOR
    What this gallery is, what 'Modern Art' meant in 1924 when the museum opened, and the route through the
    next ten tracks.

 You've come up to the top floor. Look out the window if you want, the Boboli Gardens stretch up the
 hill behind the palace, and on a clear day you can see all the way to Forte Belvedere.

 The Gallery of Modern Art opened in nineteen twenty-four. The word 'modern' in the title is a
 mid-twenties usage, it means, roughly, painting from the eighteenth century forward. There is no
 Picasso here. No Matisse. No twentieth-century abstraction. What there is, in thirty rooms, is one of the
 best collections anywhere of Italian painting between the late seventeen hundreds and the early nineteen
 hundreds, and in particular, the most concentrated collection in existence of the Tuscan Macchiaioli, the
 nineteenth-century Italian movement that anticipated Impressionism by a decade.

 The rooms themselves are part of the show. These were the everyday family rooms of the
 Habsburg-Lorraine grand dukes, less formal than the Palatine, less ceremonial than the Royal
 Apartments. Frescoed ceilings, stucco mouldings, windows on both sides looking out over Florence and
 the Boboli Gardens.

 The thirty numbered rooms run in a loop around the courtyard, and the layout is roughly chronological.
 Neoclassicism in rooms one to four. Romanticism and history painting in five to seven. Then the great
 Macchiaioli and mid-nineteenth-century band in eight to sixteen. The donated collections in seventeen
 to twenty-one. The Italian schools and Symbolism in twenty-two to twenty-six. And the early twentieth
 century in twenty-seven to thirty. Off to one side sits the grand Ballroom, the Sala da Ballo, and a
 mezzanine called the Andito degli Angiolini that holds temporary shows.

 Start at Room one, the first of the Neoclassical rooms, and follow the numbers. Play the next track.
