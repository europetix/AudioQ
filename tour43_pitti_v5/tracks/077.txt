 The Two Oldest Icons — 1639 Inventory
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of either the small Beheading of Saint John the Baptist, or the Mother of God 'In You Let Every
    Creature Rejoice' — whichever you find first. Both are among the smaller panels in the collection.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Two early seventeenth-century icons, smaller than most others in the room. Muscovite workshop. The
    Beheading shows the saint's body and a sword-bearing executioner against a gold ground. The Mother of God
    shows the Virgin holding the Christ Child, surrounded by a crowd of small kneeling figures — the 'every
    creature' of the title.
    LISTEN FOR
    How these two icons reached Florence in the first place, what the long title of the Mother of God means, and
    why icons aren't pictures the way Western paintings are pictures.

 These are the two oldest pieces in the museum. Both were already in the Pitti by sixteen thirty-nine —
 eighty-three years before there was a museum here to put them in. They're listed in the household
 inventory of Grand Duke Ferdinando the Second and his wife Vittoria della Rovere, kept in their Chapel
 of Relics. How they got to Florence in the first place is less certain than we'd like — most likely as
 diplomatic gifts, possibly through the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, possibly through Habsburg
 connections.

 The Beheading of Saint John the Baptist is straightforward in subject — the moment described in the
 Gospels, when Herod's executioner takes the saint's head — but look at how it's painted. The body is
 calm. The executioner is calm. There's no Western theatrical horror here. Orthodox icons deliberately
 strip out the drama because the saint, in the Orthodox understanding, is already in heaven; the event
 we're looking at is past, finished, sanctified.

 The other one, the Mother of God titled 'In You Let Every Creature Rejoice,' takes its name from a
 hymn — a few lines of liturgical poetry praising Mary as the meeting point of all creation. So the
 iconographer has painted exactly that: Mary in the centre, the Christ Child on her lap, and all around her
 every order of being — angels in the sky, saints, ordinary people, kneeling, looking up. The icon is the
 hymn given a form you can stand in front of.

 That's the important thing to know about looking at icons generally. They're not paintings of religious
 subjects. They're objects made for a specific kind of attention — closer to prayer than to viewing. The
 gold ground isn't a decorative choice; it's the light of the divine, and the figures inside that light are
 meant to be present in the room with you, not pictured at a distance. You don't have to share the
 theology to feel the difference. Stand in front of one for a full minute and you'll feel it.

 When you've looked at both, walk through to the central hall — it's the largest room in this ground-floor
 apartment, with its own ceiling frescoes and an altar at one end. That's the Palatine Chapel. Play the next
 track when you're inside it.
