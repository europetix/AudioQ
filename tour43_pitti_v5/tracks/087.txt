 Giambologna and Pietro Tacca — The Statue of
 Abundance
    WHERE YOU ARE
    At the Statue of Abundance, at the top of the central garden axis on the upper terrace — the highest sculptural
    point of the garden, where the whole sight line from the palace terminates.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A colossal marble female figure holding a sheaf of wheat and a cornucopia, standing at the summit of the
    Boboli's central axis. Begun by Giambologna in the early 1600s, left unfinished at his death in 1608, and
    completed by his pupils Pietro Tacca and Sebastiano Salvini, who set it in its present commanding position in
    the 1630s.
    LISTEN FOR
    What this statue represents, the unusual story of how it was begun and finished, and why it stands exactly
    where it does.

 Stand near her base and then turn around to look back down the hill. That view down the axis is the
 reason she's here.

 The figure is Abundance — Abbondanza — a personification of plenty and good harvest, holding a
 sheaf of wheat and a cornucopia spilling fruit. She's a standard allegorical subject, but her history is
 anything but standard.

 Giambologna — the Flemish-born sculptor who dominated late sixteenth-century Florence, whose
 Oceanus Fountain you'll meet shortly down the hill — began this figure in the early sixteen hundreds. It
 was originally intended as a portrait statue of a Habsburg archduchess, meant to crown a column
 elsewhere in Florence. Giambologna died in sixteen-oh-eight with the statue unfinished. His most gifted
 pupil, Pietro Tacca, completed the figure together with another assistant, Sebastiano Salvini. They
 reworked the unfinished portrait into a generic Abundance and, in the sixteen thirties, set her at the very
 top of the Boboli axis.

 And that placement is everything. Look back down the hill from where you stand. The whole garden
 was designed around a single straight sight line — from the courtyard of the palace, through the
 Artichoke Fountain, up through the amphitheatre and the obelisk, past the Neptune Fountain, all the way
 up to here. Abundance is the full stop at the end of that sentence. Everything in the central garden points
 at her, and from her feet the entire composition resolves back down toward the palace.

 When you've taken in the view, you'll begin the long descent down the western side of the garden. Find
 the head of the great cypress avenue — the Viottolone — and start down it. Play the next track when
 you're walking under the cypresses.
