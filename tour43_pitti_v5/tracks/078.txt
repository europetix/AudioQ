 The Palatine Chapel — Ademollo's Frescoes
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Inside the Palatine Chapel — the large central room of the ground-floor summer apartment, now consecrated
    as a chapel with an altar at one end.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A grand hall with an altar at one end, walls and ceiling completely covered in early nineteenth-century
    frescoes by Luigi Ademollo — biblical scenes, allegorical figures, illusionistic architecture. The lighting is
    dim and warm, designed to let the gold and the painted depth do their work.
    LISTEN FOR
    Why a Habsburg-Lorraine grand duke turned a Medici summer apartment hall into a chapel, who Ademollo
    was, and what to look for in the frescoes.

 Take a slow look around. The room is rectangular, with the altar at the far end, and every surface that
 isn't furniture is painted. The frescoes are by Luigi Ademollo, a Milan-born painter who spent most of
 his working life in Florence and is almost forgotten outside it. He painted this room in the eighteen
 twenties.

 Why this room is a chapel at all is part of the story. When Cosimo the Third and Marguerite Louise
 married in sixteen sixty-one, this was their summer apartment's grand reception hall — a secular room
 for entertaining. A century later, in seventeen sixty-six, the new Habsburg-Lorraine ruler Pietro
 Leopoldo — the Enlightenment reformer who later became Holy Roman Emperor — decided the palace
 needed a court chapel on the ground floor, accessible without climbing stairs. He consecrated this hall.
 Ademollo's frescoes came later, after Napoleon.

 What Ademollo does is combine neoclassical figure painting with the older Italian tradition of
 illusionistic architecture. Walls open into painted colonnades. Ceilings open into painted sky. Stand near
 the centre and tilt your head back, and the room becomes taller than it is.

 The proximity here is the museum's deliberate point. Orthodox icons next door, Catholic chapel here —
 two traditions of Christian devotional art under one roof, kept separate by history and connected by the
 same building.

 When you've taken it in, you've finished inside the palace. The last part of the tour is outdoors — the
 Boboli Gardens behind the palace, on a separate ticket. Step out into the Ammannati Courtyard and
 through the back gate to the gardens' threshold. Play the next track when you arrive.
