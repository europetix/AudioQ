 Lorenzo Bartolini — Charity
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of Bartolini's Charity, the marble sculpture at the centre of the Sala dell'Iliade.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A white marble of a seated young woman holding a small child against her shoulder, with a second child
    standing at her knee leaning into her lap. The figure is calm, almost domestic. The drapery is simple. Finished
    1835.
    LISTEN FOR
    Who Bartolini was, what argument this sculpture is making, and why it sits in the middle of a room full of
    paintings.

 Walk around her. The piece reads differently from each side, and Bartolini intended that.

 Lorenzo Bartolini was born in seventeen seventy-seven in a Tuscan village, trained partly in Paris, and
 became the most influential Italian sculptor of his generation. By the eighteen twenties, when this
 Charity was commissioned, the dominant style across Europe was the high neoclassicism of Antonio
 Canova — figures of ideal proportion, smooth as porcelain, modelled on Greek and Roman antiquity.
 Bartolini thought that whole tradition had grown cold. His campaign through the eighteen thirties was
 for what he called naturalismo — a return to warmer, less idealised, more domestic figures, drawn from
 observation of real bodies rather than copies of antique statues.

 Look at the Charity in that light. She's a young woman, not a goddess. The proportions are human, not
 idealised. Her drapery falls in naturalistic folds. The standing child leaning into her lap is drawn from
 observation of a real child, not from a Roman putto. Her expression is tender without being theatrical.
 She isn't performing motherhood, she's doing it.

 The subject is the Christian virtue of Charity — traditionally personified as a woman surrounded by
 children — but Bartolini has stripped the religious iconography back almost completely. No flame
 above her head. No inscription. She could be a saint, a Madonna, or just a young mother on a quiet
 afternoon. That ambiguity is the whole point.

 The sculpture sits in the centre of this room to give the eye a place to rest. Salon-style hanging —
 paintings stacked floor to ceiling on every wall — needs a still object somewhere in the room, or the eye
 has nowhere to go.

 When you're ready, there's one more work in this room — and it's a hard turn from this quiet mother.
 Look for Artemisia Gentileschi's Judith and her Maidservant: two women in the dark, a severed head, a
 drawn sword. Find it and play the next track.
