 Caravaggio — Sleeping Cupid
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of Caravaggio's Sleeping Cupid in the Sala dell'Educazione di Giove. The painting is small, about a
    metre wide, and shows a single sleeping child low on the wall.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    An oil-on-canvas painting from 1608. A small winged child, perhaps two years old, lies asleep on a draped
    cloth, his bow and arrows fallen beside him, his quiver under his head. He's nude. His face is turned slightly
    toward the viewer. The light falls hard from above-left, illuminating his face and chest while leaving the
    lower half of the body in shadow.
    LISTEN FOR
    Where Caravaggio was when he painted this, who he was painting for, and why this small painting is darker
    than it first appears.

 Stand close.

 Caravaggio painted this in sixteen-oh-eight, in Malta, in one of the strangest periods of a strange career.
 He had fled Rome two years earlier after killing a man in a duel — there was a price on his head — and
 had eventually made his way to Malta, where he had been received with honour and admitted to the
 Knights of Malta. The Knights wanted his paintings. He needed their protection. The arrangement
 worked for about a year before he was thrown into a Maltese prison, escaped to Sicily, and resumed his
 fugitive existence.

 The Sleeping Cupid was painted for one of the Maltese knights, a man named Fra Francesco
 dell'Antella, who was also a Florentine. After Caravaggio's death in sixteen-ten — at thirty-eight, on a
 beach in Tuscany, of uncertain causes — dell'Antella brought the painting back to Florence, where it
 eventually entered the Medici collection.

 Look at the painting. The traditional subject is light — Cupid asleep, his weapons set aside, an emblem
 of love at rest. The iconography is centuries old. But Caravaggio has painted something unsettling. The
 child is too thin. His ribs show. His skin has the wrong colour — too pale, almost greyish, tinged with
 green in the shadows. His mouth is slightly open, his head fallen back. Some scholars have argued —
 controversially — that Caravaggio painted from a corpse. Others believe the child was alive but
 seriously ill, perhaps tubercular. The painting is not the innocent allegory the title suggests.

 Look at the light. Caravaggio's signature trick — the single hard light from upper left, the deep shadows,
 the figure emerging from blackness — is doing its full work here. The dramatic theatrical lighting that
 revolutionised Italian and European painting for the next fifty years is concentrated in this one quiet
 image.

 The painting is a meditation on mortality disguised as a mythology piece. Cupid, exhausted. Love,
 vulnerable. The viewer is standing close enough to wake him.

 you arrive.
