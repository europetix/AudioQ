 Raphael — Madonna della Seggiola
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of Raphael's Madonna della Seggiola in the Sala di Saturno. The painting is a tondo — round —
    about seventy-one centimetres in diameter, framed heavily and hung at eye level.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    An oil-on-panel tondo painted around 1513-1514. The Virgin sits holding the Christ Child against her chest,
    with the young Saint John the Baptist crowded into the right edge of the composition. Mary wears a striped
    headcloth, the Christ Child is bare. All three figures press close together, almost filling the round frame.
    LISTEN FOR
    How a tondo works as a composition, what's strange about Mary's pose, and why this small painting became
    the most reproduced Madonna in Western art.

 Stand close. The composition is unusually intimate.

 It's a tondo — a round painting — a Florentine format that goes back to wedding gifts of the
 fourteen-hundreds. The round frame forced painters to think differently from a rectangular panel.
 Curves had to follow the edge. Figures had to nest into one another. Raphael, painting this around
 fifteen-thirteen during his Roman years under Pope Leo the Tenth, used the constraint as an invitation.

 Look at how Mary is sitting. She's on a low chair — la seggiola, the small seat — and she's turned to
 embrace the Christ Child without facing him. Her cheek presses against the top of his head. He turns to
 look at us with the alert seriousness of a small child who's been disturbed mid-task. The pose is the
 opposite of formal. There is no throne. No framing arch. No floor. Just three figures packed into a circle.

 Now look at her clothing. The striped cloth on her head, the rough green shawl across her shoulders, the
 simple dark gown underneath. Tradition says Raphael saw a peasant woman sitting with her child on a
 low stool in a Roman doorway and painted what he'd seen. The story is almost certainly invented, but it
 shows how unusual the painting felt even to contemporaries. Mary as a real woman with a real toddler
 in real clothes.

 The painting was already in the Tribuna of the Uffizi by fifteen eighty-nine. Napoleon took it to France
 in seventeen ninety-nine; it came back in eighteen-fifteen and has been in Florence ever since. From the
 moment it returned, copying this tondo became an almost mandatory exercise for young artists on the
 Grand Tour. Reproductions of it have hung in more European homes than any other Renaissance
 Madonna.

 When you've looked enough, find Raphael's Portrait of Pope Leo the Tenth with Two Cardinals — a
 large, dark, three-figure portrait of a seated pope. Play the next track in front of it.
