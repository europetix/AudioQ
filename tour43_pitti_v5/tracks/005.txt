 Sala di Venere — The First Room
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In the Sala di Venere, the last Planet Room on your modern route — but the first room of the original Medici
    reception sequence. The ceiling shows Venus with the young prince.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A grand reception room with a Pietro da Cortona ceiling fresco of Venus — the goddess of love — gently
    steering a young prince away from her embrace and toward the path of virtue. At the centre of the room, on a
    low pedestal, Canova's marble Venus Italica. Walls hung with Titian and seventeenth-century landscapes.
    LISTEN FOR
    Why Venus is at the start of the prince's education, what Canova's statue is doing in the centre of the room,
    and the Madonna del Granduca that's currently relocated here.

 You've reached the first of the Planet Rooms — and, under the Medici, the original beginning of the
 route. Under the Medici, visitors entered the Grand Duke's reception suite here. Cortona's ceiling shows
 you what the room was about: Venus, goddess of love, pulling a young prince toward herself, and the
 prince — gently, sadly — turning away. The pleasures of the senses are the first thing the young ruler
 has to learn to refuse before he can be educated by Apollo, hardened by Mars, crowned by Jupiter and
 honoured by Saturn.

 Look at the figure standing in the centre of the room. Antonio Canova carved this marble — the Venus
 Italica — between eighteen-oh-four and eighteen-twelve, on a direct commission from Napoleon.
 Napoleon had just stripped the Pitti of one of its most famous antique sculptures — the so-called Medici
 Venus, an ancient Roman copy of a Greek original — and shipped it to Paris. Canova, the most
 celebrated sculptor in Europe, was commissioned to replace it. He didn't make a copy. He made a
 modern Venus, a younger and more vulnerable figure than the antique one, and placed her in the same
 spot.

 After Waterloo the original Medici Venus came home from Paris. It's now in the Tribuna of the Uffizi.
 The Canova replacement stayed at the Pitti because by then the Florentines had grown fond of her.

 Two more works here get their own track. Walk to the Canova and play the next track.
