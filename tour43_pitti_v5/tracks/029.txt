 Artemisia Gentileschi — Judith and her
 Maidservant
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of Artemisia Gentileschi's Judith and her Maidservant, in the Sala dell'Iliade — the same room as La
    Gravida and the Charity. Oil on canvas, about a hundred and fourteen by ninety-four centimetres.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A dark, near candle-lit scene of two women still inside Holofernes' tent, just after the killing. Judith, in a gold
    gown, holds a large sword back against her shoulder; her maidservant Abra bends to bundle the severed head
    into a basket. Both have turned to look off to one side, alert to a sound. Painted in Artemisia's Florentine
    years, around 1618.
    LISTEN FOR
    Why Artemisia paints the minute after the killing rather than the killing itself, and what the two women's
    partnership says.

 Stand close. This one doesn't ask to be liked.

 Most painters who took on this subject painted the killing itself — the sword going in. Artemisia
 Gentileschi paints the minute after. Judith and her maidservant Abra are still inside Holofernes' tent, in
 the dark, his severed head half-bundled into a basket between them. And they've both stopped — turned
 toward something off to one side, a sound we can't hear. You're watching two women a heartbeat from
 being caught.

 Look at the sword. Holofernes' own oversized blade rests back against Judith's shoulder, its edge close
 to her bare neck — a reminder of exactly what she's just done, and of what happens to her if the camp
 wakes. But look at her grip and her face. This isn't panic. It's control. The light is pure Caravaggio —
 one hard source, deep shadow, the two faces lifted out of the black — but the steadiness is Artemisia's
 own.

 So is the partnership. Judith's hand rests on Abra's shoulder, and the maid isn't a servant doing a job;
 she's an accomplice, about the same age, in it together. Painters before Artemisia tended to make the
 maid old, or frightened, or barely there. Here the two women hold the danger between them.

 Worth knowing who painted it. Artemisia was the first woman admitted to Florence's academy of
 drawing, working for Grand Duke Cosimo the Second — in a profession that barely let women through
 the door. She returned to Judith again and again across her life. The Uffizi has her version of the
 beheading itself; this is the quieter, tenser sequel.

 When you've looked enough, leave the Sala dell'Iliade through the doorway on the far wall. From here,
 continue to the Sala dell'Educazione di Giove, the Room of the Education of Jupiter. Play the next track
 when you're inside it.
