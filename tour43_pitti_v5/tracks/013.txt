 Peter Paul Rubens — The Consequences of War
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of Rubens's Consequences of War, in the Sala di Marte. The painting is large — over two metres
    wide — and dominates one of the long walls of the room.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A 1638 allegorical painting. Mars, in armour, strides forward dragging the goddess Venus, who is trying to
    hold him back. Behind them, the Fury Alecto leads with a torch. Around them, fallen figures of motherhood,
    the arts, and architecture lie scattered. To the left, a woman in black raises her arms in lamentation. The
    colours are smoky reds and twilight blues.
    LISTEN FOR
    What Rubens is actually depicting, why he wrote a letter explaining the painting, and what specific war he
    was thinking about.

 Step back to see it whole, then come close.

 Rubens painted this in sixteen thirty-eight, near the end of his life. He sent it as a gift to Ferdinando the
 Second de' Medici — the same grand duke who commissioned the Planet Room ceilings — and it has
 been at the Pitti ever since. Unusually, Rubens also wrote a detailed letter to the Florentine painter
 Justus Sustermans, who would soon hang the painting, explaining exactly what every figure meant. It's
 one of the few paintings in art history for which we have the artist's own line-by-line interpretation.

 At the centre, Mars — the god of war, in armour, sword raised — is striding forward. Venus, the
 goddess of love and his consort, is trying to hold him back. He's pulling away from her with violent
 force. Ahead of him, the Fury Alecto carries a torch — war's incitement. Below them, in the wreckage,
 lie a woman with a child at her breast — fertility, motherhood — an architect with his compass and
 drawings — the arts of building — and a lute-player with his broken instrument — music, the arts of
 peace. War crushes them under its feet.

 On the far left, the figure dressed in black raising her arms is Europe — the continent itself in mourning.
 Above her, in the dark sky, a faint temple of Janus stands with its doors open. In ancient Roman
 tradition, the temple of Janus was open in time of war and shut in time of peace. By Rubens's time, the
 doors had been open for two decades.

 The war Rubens was thinking about is the Thirty Years' War — the catastrophic conflict that had been
 tearing apart central Europe since sixteen eighteen and would not end for another ten years. He had
 spent his diplomatic career trying to stop it. By sixteen thirty-eight he had given up on diplomacy and
 was making the argument in paint instead. War is not glorious. War is what the painting shows.

 When you've looked enough, find the Titian portrait nearby — a half-length portrait of a bearded man in
 a black hat, sometimes called the Portrait of an English Gentleman. Play the next track.
