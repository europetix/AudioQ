 Bernardo Buontalenti — The Grotta Grande
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of (or, if you've timed it well, inside) the Grotta Grande — Buontalenti's great grotto. The exterior is
    a deeply carved stone façade with niches holding Apollo and Ceres. The interior — three chambers — opens
    only at 11:00, 13:00, 15:00, and 16:00.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    An artificial grotto designed by Bernardo Buontalenti between 1583 and 1593 for Grand Duke Francesco I de'
    Medici. Three interconnected chambers of artificial stalactites, sponge stone, and shell, populated by sculpted
    figures emerging from the rock. Until 1924 it held Michelangelo's unfinished Prisoners in the corners of the
    first chamber; the third chamber holds Giambologna's Bathing Venus.
    LISTEN FOR
    What makes this grotto extraordinary, the Michelangelo connection, and what to look for in the three rooms.

 Look at the façade first. Two niches flank the entrance, holding statues of Apollo and Ceres — gods of
 light and harvest, of civilisation — framing the descent into something wilder. The carving, with its
 stone swags imitating flowing water and dripping rock, is the work of Bernardo Buontalenti, the most
 inventive theatrical and garden designer in Italy in the fifteen eighties.

 If you're inside, take the chambers in order. The first is the famous one. Walls and ceiling are worked to
 look like the inside of a wet cave — stalactites, sponge stone, embedded shells — and from the rough
 rock, half-emerging, are figures of shepherds and animals. Look into the four corners. Until nineteen
 twenty-four, those corners held Michelangelo's unfinished Prisoners — the great slave figures
 struggling to free themselves from raw marble, which he never completed for the tomb of Pope Julius
 the Second. Buontalenti installed the originals here, where they seemed to climb out of the artificial rock
 the same way they climb out of real stone. The originals are now in the Accademia, protected; what you
 see are fine casts.

 That placement was the whole argument of the grotto. The shepherds emerge from the rock the way the
 slaves do. The cave looks natural but is entirely made. The made rock holds real shells. Where does
 nature end and human art begin? Sixteenth-century Florentines thought hard about that question, and the
 grotto is their answer built in stone and water.

 The second and third chambers are smaller and darker. The third holds Giambologna's Bathing Venus
 from fifteen seventy-three — a small marble of a young woman stepping from her bath, surprised by
 satyrs climbing the basin to spy on her.

 When you've finished — or settled for the façade — climb on into the gardens proper, up behind the
 palace to the great amphitheatre. Play the next track when you arrive.
