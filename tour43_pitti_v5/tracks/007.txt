 Titian — The Concert
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of Titian's Concert in the Sala di Venere. The painting is medium-sized — about a metre wide — and
    shows three half-length figures arranged around a clavichord.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    An oil-on-canvas painting from around 1510-1512. A young man at a clavichord, hands on the keys, turns his
    head back over his shoulder to look at us — interrupted mid-performance. Behind him, an older man with a
    beard rests a hand on his shoulder; he wears a feathered cap and looks intently at the player. To the right, a
    younger figure looks on quietly.
    LISTEN FOR
    Why this painting was attributed to Giorgione for centuries, what the moment in the painting is, and who the
    three figures might be.

 Give this one a slow minute, up close.

 The painting is small enough that the three figures crowd close together — almost too close. The young
 musician at the keyboard has turned his head back over his shoulder, as if he heard a noise behind him.
 His hands are still on the keys. Whatever he was playing has stopped, mid-phrase. The older bearded
 man behind him rests a hand on his shoulder — supportive or restraining, hard to tell. The younger
 figure on the right looks on with a kind of waiting attention.

 The painting is one of the most haunting in the Palatine. For about three centuries it was attributed to
 Giorgione — the mysterious Venetian painter who died young in fifteen ten and whose few surviving
 works changed Venetian painting forever. The attribution was compelling: the lush warmth, the silent
 narrative, the figures caught in a moment that doesn't explain itself. Twentieth-century scholarship
 moved it back to Titian, painted around fifteen ten or fifteen twelve, when Titian was a young man in
 his early twenties working in Giorgione's studio and absorbing everything he could.

 It probably is Titian. But the painting still belongs to Giorgione's world — the world of small, intimate
 Venetian paintings that don't tell you what they mean. We don't know who the three figures are. We
 don't know what music was being played. We don't know what made the young man turn around. Critics
 have proposed all kinds of identifications — a music master with two students, a Christian morality
 scene about temptation, a portrait of three real Venetians. None has won.

 What we have is the moment. The interruption. The hand on the shoulder. The pause in the music. The
 eyes of the young player meeting ours from inside the painting. Five centuries later, we are the noise
 behind him.
