 Raphael — Madonna del Granduca
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of Raphael's Madonna del Granduca, in the Sala di Saturno. The painting is small — about eighty by
    fifty-five centimetres — and very dark.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    An oil-on-panel painting from around 1505. The Virgin stands holding the Christ Child in her arms against an
    almost completely black background. She wears a red dress and a blue mantle, both deepened by age. Her
    gaze is downward and inward; the Christ Child looks calmly out. The black ground feels like deep night.
    LISTEN FOR
    Why this Madonna is so dark, who the 'Grand Duke' of the title was, and how this small painting almost never
    made it to Florence.

 She's worth standing in front of for a full minute before reading anything about her.

 Raphael painted her around fifteen-oh-five, in Florence, during the same period that produced La
 Gravida next door. He was twenty-two. The painting is small and immediately quiet — a Virgin
 standing against pure darkness, holding a child who isn't doing anything in particular. She isn't
 enthroned. There's no symbolic background. No saints attending. The frame and the dark together
 isolate her.

 About that darkness. Raphael didn't paint a black ground. Originally the figure stood in a softly lit
 interior, with a window and a glimpse of landscape behind her. Sometime in the late seventeen hundreds
 or early eighteen hundreds, the background was overpainted black, probably to focus attention on the
 figures in the manner fashionable at the time. The overpaint has been examined many times, and the
 current curatorial decision is to leave it — partly because removing it would risk damaging the rest,
 partly because the dark has become part of how the painting works on us. It feels older and more sacred
 for being veiled. That's an accident of history, but the accident is beautiful.

 The Grand Duke of the title is Ferdinando the Third of Habsburg-Lorraine, the early nineteenth-century
 grand duke of Tuscany. He bought the painting in eighteen-hundred from a private Florentine collector
 and reportedly took it with him everywhere — into exile during Napoleon's annexation, back to
 Florence on his restoration. He slept in the same room with it. When he died, it went to the Pitti and
 became the Madonna of the Grand Duke.

 When you've looked enough, there's one more Raphael in this room, and it's the biggest: the Madonna
 del Baldacchino, an enthroned Virgin and Child beneath a great canopy. Play the next track in front of
 it. It's the throne room of the original Medici reception suite. Play the next track when you arrive.
