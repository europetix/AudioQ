 Raphael — Pope Leo X with Two Cardinals
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of Raphael's Portrait of Pope Leo X with Cardinals Giulio de' Medici and Luigi de' Rossi, in the Sala
    di Saturno. The painting is large — about a metre and a half tall — and hangs at the centre of one of the walls.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A three-figure portrait painted in 1518. Pope Leo X — a heavy-set, middle-aged man in red papal robes —
    sits behind a table holding an illuminated manuscript and a magnifying lens. Behind him stand two cardinals
    in red robes. The lighting is intimate, almost candle-lit. A burnished gold ball on the back of the pope's chair
    reflects the window of the studio.
    LISTEN FOR
    Who Pope Leo X was, who the two cardinals behind him are, and the tiny extraordinary detail that made the
    painting famous in Raphael's own lifetime.

 Get close. This portrait was painted in fifteen eighteen, two years before Raphael's death, when he was
 already the most sought-after artist in Italy and Pope Leo the Tenth was the most powerful patron in
 Europe.

 The pope is Giovanni de' Medici — second son of Lorenzo the Magnificent, Florentine to the bone —
 who took the name Leo the Tenth when he became pope in fifteen thirteen. He's the Medici pope who
 excommunicated Martin Luther, began the rebuilding of Saint Peter's, and drove the papacy nearly to
 bankruptcy with the scale of his patronage. He famously said, on becoming pope, 'God has given us the
 papacy. Let us enjoy it.' Or so the chroniclers report. Whether he said it or not, he meant it.

 The two cardinals are his cousins. Behind him on the right is Giulio de' Medici, the future Pope Clement
 the Seventh, who would later have Rome sacked under his watch and supervise the construction of the
 Medici Chapels in San Lorenzo. On the left is Luigi de' Rossi, the pope's first cousin, who would die a
 year later of plague.

 Now look at the tiny extraordinary thing in the painting. On the back of the pope's chair, behind his
 shoulder, there's a small burnished gold ball — a finial. Look closely at it. You can see, reflected in its
 surface, the window of the room the painting was made in. Vasari, writing about this painting fifty years
 later, thought this single detail alone proved Raphael could do anything he wanted with paint.

 One more thing to know. For a long time some scholars believed the two cardinals were added later by
 Raphael's workshop. A two-year restoration completed in twenty twenty showed definitively that no —
 Raphael painted all three figures, every detail of every face, down to the worry lines and the strands of
 white in the pope's eyebrows.

 When you've looked enough, find the third Raphael in this room — the Madonna del Granduca. It's a
 smaller, very dark, almost silhouetted Virgin holding a baby. Play the next track.
