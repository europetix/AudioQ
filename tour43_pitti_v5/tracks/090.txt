 The Limonaia — The Medici Obsession with Citrus
    WHERE YOU ARE
    At the Limonaia, the long Lemon House on the western side of the gardens — a low building painted
    Habsburg-Lorraine green, where the garden's citrus collection overwinters.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    The garden's Lemon House (Orangery), built 1777-78 by Zanobi del Rosso under Grand Duke Pietro
    Leopoldo to shelter the Medici citrus collection through winter. A long heated building painted the same
    green as the Kaffeehaus. Still holds part of the historic collection of rare and ancient citrus varieties in
    terracotta pots, wheeled out in summer and back in for the cold.
    LISTEN FOR
    Why the Medici were obsessed with lemons, what a 'bizzarria' is, and how the pots you've been seeing all
    over the garden connect to this one long building.

 Those lemon trees in pots you've seen all over the garden — around the Isolotto, on the terraces — live
 here for half the year. This is the Limonaia, the Lemon House, and the story behind it is one of the most
 characteristically Medici things in the whole garden.

 The Medici were obsessed with citrus. Not casually fond — obsessed. From the sixteenth century on,
 the family assembled one of the largest collections of citrus varieties in Europe: hundreds of kinds of
 lemons, oranges, citrons, and stranger things. Citrus was a status symbol — hard and expensive to keep
 alive through a Tuscan winter, exotic, and producing fruit, flowers, and scent all at once. A grand duke
 who could show you fruiting lemon trees in January was showing you his wealth and his mastery over
 nature.

 The strangest part of the collection were the bizzarrie — oddities. Grafted, deliberately deformed citrus,
 bred to produce knobbled, fingered, or fused fruit in bizarre shapes. The Medici prized them precisely
 because they were strange. The most famous, the Bizzarria, is a single tree producing lemon, orange,
 and citron all at once. Some of these ancient varieties survive here and almost nowhere else.

 The building went up in seventeen seventy-seven, by Zanobi del Rosso — the same architect as the
 Kaffeehaus — painted the same Lorraine green. It's long, low, and heated, with tall windows for the
 winter sun. Every autumn the potted trees are wheeled in out of the cold; every spring back out. A
 four-hundred-year-old seasonal ritual still being performed.

 When you've seen it, you're near the southern edge of the garden. Walk on toward the exit. Play the next
 track.
