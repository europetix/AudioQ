 Valerio Cioli — The Fountain of Bacchino
 (Morgante on the Turtle)
    WHERE YOU ARE
    At the Fountain of Bacchino, near the Bacchus Gate at the lower northeast corner of the gardens, backing
    onto the wall beneath the Vasari Corridor, overlooking the path that leads up to the Buontalenti Grotto.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A marble fountain figure by Valerio Cioli, carved around 1560 and turned into a fountain in 1572. A nude,
    heavy-set dwarf sits astride a large turtle, one arm raised. A jet of water flows from the turtle's mouth into the
    basin below. The statue you see is a copy; the original marble has been kept indoors in the Kaffeehaus since
    2013.
    LISTEN FOR
    Who the man riding the turtle actually was, why the turtle matters, and how to look at a
    four-hundred-year-old joke with the dignity it's owed.

 Stop here. People walk straight past this one on their way up the hill, and they shouldn't.

 The figure on the turtle is a real person. His name was Braccio di Bartolo, and he came from the
 countryside near Bologna. He had dwarfism, and as a child he was handed over to the Medici court of
 Cosimo the First and Eleonora by a family that, the records suggest, could not keep him and hoped the
 court would give him a safer, steadier life than they could. At court he was renamed Morgante, after the
 giant in a popular comic poem of the time — a deliberate irony at the expense of his size. He became
 one of the most celebrated figures at the Medici court, a jester and companion, and Cosimo eventually
 gave him a farm of his own near Arezzo.

 There's no way to look at this fountain honestly without holding two things at once. A disabled man was
 made into court entertainment, carved nude, turned into a comic fountain. That was the casual cruelty of
 the period. And at the same time, this is one of the warmest, most human pieces of sculpture in the
 whole garden — the artist clearly knew Braccio, observed him closely, and rendered him as a specific,
 dignified individual rather than a generic figure of fun.

 Look at the turtle. It isn't random. The turtle with a sail on its back was Cosimo the First's personal
 emblem, paired with the motto festina lente — make haste slowly. So Braccio is riding the Grand
 Duke's own symbol of patient, measured rule.

 Now look at his raised arm. He's lifting it in the gesture of a Roman general about to address his troops
 — the exact pose of the ancient bronze of Emperor Marcus Aurelius on horseback in Rome. A nude
 dwarf on a turtle, raising his hand for silence like an emperor. The joke is sharp and the carving is
 affectionate, both at once.

 The painter Bronzino painted Braccio too, around the same time — a famous double-sided nude
 portrait. More people across the centuries have seen Braccio di Bartolo's face than have ever seen a
 portrait of Cosimo himself.

 When you've looked, walk up the path toward the grottoes. Play the next track.
