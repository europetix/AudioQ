 Antonio Canova — Bust of Napoleon
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of Canova's marble bust of Napoleon, in Room 2 of the Modern Art Gallery. The bust is life-sized
    and shows Napoleon in idealised classical profile.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A marble bust from around 1805-1810. Napoleon is shown in three-quarter view, bare-headed, his hair
    cropped close in the deliberately Roman style he adopted as Emperor. The neck is bare, draped only in the
    toga-like fold suggesting an imperial garment. His face is idealised — younger and more classically beautiful
    than Napoleon actually was.
    LISTEN FOR
    How Canova came to make this bust, what tension it carries, and why Napoleon and Canova never really
    liked each other.

 Look at the face first. Then we'll talk about the politics, because the politics are the thing.

 Canova carved this bust around eighteen-oh-five. Napoleon had just crowned himself Emperor of the
 French and was at the apex of his power across Europe. He wanted a portrait by the most famous
 sculptor on the continent, and Canova was that sculptor. The brief was clear: Napoleon was to be shown
 as a Roman emperor — bare-chested, idealised, as if a Greek god had been born in Corsica.

 Canova hated the assignment. He had spent thirty years arguing for the moral seriousness of
 neoclassical art, and the idea of carving a flattering portrait of the dictator who had stripped Italy of its
 art treasures was, personally, repugnant. The Pitti's Venus Italica which you saw earlier was Canova's
 replacement for the antique Medici Venus that Napoleon had taken to Paris. The two men had history.

 Canova made the bust anyway. He worked from life sittings in Paris, and what he saw wasn't a Roman
 god — it was a short, tight-faced thirty-six-year-old man with a pronounced cleft in his chin and the
 early signs of the weight Napoleon would put on by the time of his exile. Canova carved an idealised
 version. But look closely — the idealisation is restrained. The face has structure. The brows are heavy.
 The mouth is set. Whatever Canova's feelings, he made the face of a man, not a fantasy.

 Napoleon and Canova met in Paris during these sittings. By all accounts they argued. Napoleon at one
 point told Canova he should move to Paris and become his court sculptor. Canova refused. He returned
 to Rome. He spent his last years working diplomatically — and successfully — to get the looted Italian
 art returned after Waterloo.

 The bust came to the Pitti through the Habsburg-Lorraine collection after eighteen fifteen. When you've
 looked enough, walk on into rooms five through seven, where Romanticism and history painting begin.
