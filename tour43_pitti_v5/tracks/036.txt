 Sala di Prometeo — A Hall of Tondi
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In the Sala di Prometeo, named for the ceiling fresco of Prometheus stealing fire from the gods.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A small to medium room with a neoclassical ceiling fresco by Giuseppe Collignon (early nineteenth century)
    showing Prometheus stealing fire from Olympus. Walls hung with a strong concentration of Italian fifteenth
    and early sixteenth-century paintings, several of them tondi — the round wedding-gift format that flourished
    in Florence.
    LISTEN FOR
    What a tondo is, why this room is full of them, and the one extraordinary tondo that gets its own track.

 You're now standing in a room of tondi — round paintings, the format that flourished in Florence in the
 late fourteen hundreds and early fifteen hundreds. Tondi were almost always commissioned for
 weddings or the birth of a child, hung in the bedroom or main living room of the new household, and
 depicted the Virgin and Child or the Holy Family. The round format was associated with the Florentine
 domestic interior the way the rectangular panel was associated with churches.

 Take a slow walk around the room. You'll see tondi by Sandro Botticelli, by Domenico Ghirlandaio, by
 Lorenzo di Credi — most of them small and intimate, painted for specific Florentine families whose
 names sometimes survive in the inventories and sometimes don't. This is one of the densest
 concentrations of Florentine fifteenth-century tondi anywhere outside the Uffizi.

 One of these gets its own track. Find the tondo by Filippo Lippi — the elder Lippi, father of Filippino
 — showing the Virgin and Child with episodes from the life of the Virgin in the background. It's
 sometimes called the Tondo Bartolini after the family that commissioned it. Find it and play the next
 track.
