 Cristofano Allori — Judith with the Head of
 Holofernes
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of Cristofano Allori's Judith with the Head of Holofernes, in the Sala dell'Educazione di Giove. Oil
    on canvas, about a hundred and forty by a hundred and twenty centimetres.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A young woman in a shimmering gold-and-blue silk dress stands calmly, looking out past the viewer, holding
    a man's severed head by the hair in her lowered hand. An older servant looks on behind her. Painted around
    1610 to 1612; the surface is luminous and exquisitely detailed.
    LISTEN FOR
    The autobiographical secret hidden in the faces, why the dress matters in Florence, and how this Judith
    connects to Artemisia's in the Iliad Room.

 Get close, then step back. This is the painting the room is known for.

 At first it reads as almost serene. A beautiful young woman in a gold-and-blue silk dress, calm, upright,
 looking out past you. Then your eye drops to her lowered hand, and the serenity curdles: she's holding a
 man's severed head by the hair.

 That contrast — the exquisite surface, the horror inside it — is the whole painting. Cristofano Allori
 finished it around sixteen eleven, and he loaded it with his own life. The head of Holofernes is a
 self-portrait. The radiant Judith is a Florentine woman known as la Mazzafirra, who had recently left
 him. The story told at the time was that Allori painted his own face onto the dead general as a kind of
 confession — this is what loving her cost me. True or not, he made the most personal version of this
 subject anyone in Florence had attempted.

 Look at the dress. That shimmering brocade isn't only Allori showing off; it's a tribute to the thing
 Florence was richest at — cloth. The city's whole fortune was built on textiles, and fabric painted this
 convincingly was a kind of civic boast.

 And one thread ties this room back to the Iliad Room you walked through earlier. Allori learned this
 trick of lifting figures out of pure darkness partly from Caravaggio, and partly through Artemisia
 Gentileschi, who was working for the Medici at the very same moment. Her Judith and his Judith are
 cousins, painted streets apart.

 When you've looked enough, stay in this room for its second work with a track: Caravaggio's Sleeping
 Cupid, a small dark canvas low on the wall. Play the next track in front of it.
