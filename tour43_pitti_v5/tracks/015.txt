 Anthony van Dyck — Portrait of Cardinal Guido
 Bentivoglio
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Still in the Sala di Marte. On the wall, a seated cardinal in shimmering scarlet silk, one hand resting on the
    arm of his chair, turned slightly as if you'd just interrupted him.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Anthony van Dyck, Portrait of Cardinal Guido Bentivoglio, painted around sixteen twenty-three in Rome. Oil
    on canvas, almost two metres tall — a seated churchman in watered-red robes against a quiet, dark ground.
    LISTEN FOR
    A very young Flemish painter loose in Italy, a worldly cardinal from his own corner of Europe, and the most
    dazzling red in the room.

 Van Dyck was barely into his twenties when he painted this. He had just left Rubens's workshop in
 Antwerp and arrived in Italy to study Titian at the source — and what you are looking at is a young
 master announcing himself.

 The sitter is Cardinal Guido Bentivoglio: churchman, scholar, and diplomat. He had served as the papal
 ambassador to Flanders, Van Dyck's own homeland, so the two of them shared a world, and you can
 feel that ease between painter and subject.

 Look first at the red. The cardinal's watered silk ripples with light, the rippled sheen of the fabric caught
 so exactly that you half expect to hear it rustle. That kind of bravura with surfaces is what would make
 Van Dyck the most sought-after portraitist in Europe within just a few years. Then look at the face —
 alert, quick, faintly guarded, a sharp mind caught a half-second into a thought. That pairing of gorgeous
 cloth and real psychology became the blueprint for grand portraiture for the next two centuries; you can
 draw a straight line from this canvas to Velázquez's popes, and on to the swaggering court portraits Van
 Dyck himself would later paint for Charles the First of England.

 It hangs here in the Mars Room for a reason. This is where the gallery shows off the Medici taste for
 northern European painting, which is exactly why the great Rubens canvases you've just seen share
 these walls with it. When you've taken the cardinal in, walk through into the next room, the Sala di
 Giove, the Room of Jupiter. Play the next track when you're inside it.
