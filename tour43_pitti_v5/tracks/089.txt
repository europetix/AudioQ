 Giambologna — Oceanus at the Isolotto
    WHERE YOU ARE
    At the Isolotto, the circular island-pool at the southern end of the Viottolone. A balustrade rings the pool; at
    the centre, on its island, stands a colossal Oceanus on a marble plinth, with statue groups around the water
    and lemon trees in terracotta pots along the rim.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A colossal bronze of Oceanus by Giambologna, 1571-1575, on a marble base ringed by three reclining
    river-gods — the Nile, Ganges, and Euphrates. It stands on an island in the Vasca dell'Isolotto, the garden's
    largest water basin, built in 1618. Around the pool, statue groups including Perseus on horseback emerging
    from the water and Andromeda, plus folkloric groups of peasants and playing children. The Oceanus here is a
    copy; the original is in the Bargello.
    LISTEN FOR
    Who Giambologna was, what the four waters mean, and the smaller statues around the pool that are easy to
    miss.

 Circle the whole pool before you fix on the giant in the middle. The fountain works from every angle,
 and there's more here than him.

 Giambologna — born Jean Boulogne in Flanders in fifteen twenty-nine — was the dominant sculptor of
 late sixteenth-century Florence. He came to Italy in his twenties, stayed for life, and became official
 sculptor to the Medici grand dukes. The equestrian bronze of Cosimo the First in the Piazza della
 Signoria is his. The Rape of the Sabines under the Loggia dei Lanzi is his. So is this.

 He cast the Oceanus between fifteen seventy-one and fifteen seventy-five for Francesco the First.
 Oceanus, in classical cosmology, was the great river encircling the whole flat earth — the source of all
 other waters. He stands tall, bearded, holding a long staff. Around the base of his plinth, three reclining
 figures: the personified rivers Nile, Ganges, and Euphrates — Africa, Asia, and the ancient Near East.
 The four waters together stood for the whole world, with Medici Tuscany imagined at its commercial
 centre.

 Now look around the pool, not just at the middle. The Vasca dell'Isolotto, built in sixteen eighteen, is the
 largest body of water in the garden, and its rim and approaches hold a scatter of statue groups. Look for
 Perseus on horseback, rising up out of the water as if swimming his horse across, and Andromeda
 nearby. And look for the smaller, warmer groups — peasants, and children at play — set lower down.
 After all the gods and emperors, the garden lets in a few ordinary people too.

 Note: the Oceanus you see is a high-quality copy. The original was moved indoors for conservation in
 nineteen ninety-eight and is now in the Bargello in central Florence — where you can see
 Giambologna's actual hand.

 Notice the lemon trees in their big terracotta pots, ringing the island. They matter for the next stop.
 When you're ready, walk on toward the Lemon House. Play the next track.
