 Sala dell'Educazione di Giove — A Smaller Room,
 a Bigger Reach
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In the Sala dell'Educazione di Giove, a smaller room past the Planet Rooms with a single dominant ceiling
    fresco.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A more modestly sized room than the Planet Rooms, with a ceiling fresco of the infant Jupiter being suckled
    by the goat Amalthea — the mythological education of the young god. Walls hung with smaller works:
    portraits, devotional pictures, two small Caravaggio-influenced paintings, and a sleeping Cupid attributed to
    Caravaggio himself which is one of the marquee small works of the Palatine.
    LISTEN FOR
    What this room was for, the ceiling story it tells, and what to look for on the walls.

 You've left the grand reception suite. From here on, the rooms are smaller and more private — these
 were the corridors and antechambers connecting the Grand Duke's ceremonial rooms to the more
 intimate parts of the apartment.

 The ceiling here shows the infant Jupiter — the same king of gods you saw crowning a prince two
 rooms back, now painted as a baby. The legend was that the infant Jupiter, hidden from his father Saturn
 who ate his children, was raised on a hidden island by nymphs and nursed by a divine goat called
 Amalthea. The fresco shows the moment of his nursing. The iconographic logic for the Medici was clear
 enough: even the king of the gods had to be a child first, hidden and protected.

 The room has rotated through different hangings over the centuries, and today it holds a more eclectic
 group than the Planet Rooms. Two works in this room get their own track. One is a small Sleeping
 Cupid by Caravaggio, low on the wall — we'll come to it in a moment. The other, and the work this
 room is best known for, is Cristofano Allori's Judith with the Head of Holofernes.

 Look up at Jupiter and Amalthea first. Then find the Allori — a beautiful young woman, calm as
 anything, holding a man's severed head. It gets its own track. Play the next track in front of it.
