 Rooms 17–30 — Collections, Symbolism, and the
 Twentieth Century
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In the final rooms of the Modern Art Gallery, rooms 17 through 30, covering the donated collections,
    late-19th-century Italian schools and Symbolism, and early-20th-century painting.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Rooms 17 to 21: the donated collections (Boldini, De Nittis, municipal acquisitions, the Ambron collection).
    Rooms 22 to 26: late-19th-century Italian schools, Symbolism, and Divisionism, the Italian counterpart to
    French Pointillism. Rooms 27 to 30: early-20th-century Italian painting including Morandi and De Chirico,
    ending with the 1922 Primaverile acquisitions. The Andito degli Angiolini mezzanine holds temporary
    shows.
    LISTEN FOR
    What replaced the Macchiaioli, what to look for in these final rooms, and one painter who gets a single track
    at the end.

 The Macchiaioli were the high point of Italian nineteenth-century painting. What follows fills the rest of
 the floor in three bands, and you can walk it briskly.

 First, rooms seventeen through twenty-one: the donated collections. These are the works that came to
 the gallery by gift and bequest rather than by ducal inheritance, society portraits by Boldini and De
 Nittis, the city's own municipal acquisitions of the nineteen tens and twenties, and several private
 collections that carry names like Ambron. It's a different kind of museum-making: the taste of
 individual Florentine collectors rather than the state.

 Then rooms twenty-two through twenty-six: the Italian schools and Symbolism of the end of the
 century. In the late eighteen eighties and nineties Italian painters split. Some pursued Symbolism,
 moody, half-mystical, literary paintings full of personifications and dreams. Others took up
 Divisionism, the Italian counterpart of French Pointillism, small dots and strokes of pure colour laid side
 by side to vibrate optically. Walk slowly and you'll see the two running in parallel, alongside study
 collections of works on paper.

 Finally, rooms twenty-seven through thirty: the early twentieth century, where Italian painting breaks
 into the modern moment. You'll find Giorgio Morandi, the Bolognese painter who spent his life painting
 the same bottles and jars on the same table and made out of that extreme restriction some of the most
 quietly moving paintings of the century, and, in the avant-garde rooms, a small Giorgio de Chirico, the
 founder of Metaphysical Painting, with his haunted empty piazzas and impossible shadows. The very
 last room gathers the works the gallery acquired from the Primaverile exhibition of nineteen
 twenty-two.

 If you have time, the Andito degli Angiolini, a mezzanine off this floor, holds the gallery's temporary
 exhibitions, which change through the year.

 One work in these final rooms gets its own track. Find De Chirico's Metaphysical Piazza, a small
 painting of an empty Italian square with strange shadows. Play the next track.
