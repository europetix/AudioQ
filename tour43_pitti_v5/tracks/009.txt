 Titian — Mary Magdalene
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of Titian's Mary Magdalene in the Sala di Apollo. The painting is medium-sized — about a metre tall
    — and shows a half-length figure of a young woman with abundant red-gold hair.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    An oil-on-panel painting from around 1531. A young woman, nude, modestly covered by her own loose,
    abundant red-gold hair which falls forward over her body in long waves. Her face is tilted upward, mouth
    slightly open, eyes brimming. Behind her, a glimpse of dark landscape and an angle of sky.
    LISTEN FOR
    Who Mary Magdalene was, what tradition Titian is drawing on here, and what's happening in her face.

 She's worth a slow look.

 Mary Magdalene, in the Gospels, is the woman who anointed Christ's feet and was the first witness to
 his Resurrection. By the medieval period a whole legendary tradition had grown up around her — that
 she had been a prostitute before her conversion, that she had spent her later years as a hermit in a French
 cave doing penance, that her hair had grown long enough to cover her body. Almost none of that comes
 from the actual biblical text; most of it is fourteenth-century French legend. But by Titian's time the
 iconography was fixed: the penitent Magdalene was nude or near-nude, her long hair her only garment,
 eyes raised in religious ecstasy.

 Titian painted this around fifteen thirty-one for Federico Gonzaga, the Duke of Mantua, who had asked
 for a Magdalene 'as tearful as possible.' Titian gave him a Magdalene balanced between two states.
 Look at her face. Her eyes are raised, her mouth is parted, her expression is uncertain. Is she weeping in
 penitence? Is she experiencing mystical rapture? Is she just looking up because something has caught
 her eye? Titian deliberately leaves the question open. The ambiguity is the painting.

 Look at the hair. Every strand is painted differently. Some are tight curls, some loose waves. The light
 catches the top of her head and runs down the surface of the hair as a kind of slow gold river. The hair
 both covers her body — making this religious art — and reveals it. Critics complained at the time.
 Federico Gonzaga didn't.

 The Pitti's Magdalene is one of at least seven Magdalenes Titian painted over his career, each slightly
 different. He returned to the subject again and again. The version here is the earliest of the type and the
 one most scholars consider the best.

 When you've looked enough, there's one more Titian in this room: his Portrait of Pietro Aretino, a
 heavy-set bearded man in a russet-and-gold robe. Play the next track in front of it.
