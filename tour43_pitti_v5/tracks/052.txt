 Macchiaioli Core — Rooms 8–16
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In the Macchiaioli wing of the Modern Art Gallery, rooms 8 through 16, the core of the Macchiaioli
    collection.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A series of rooms with the most important collection anywhere of the Tuscan Macchiaioli, Giovanni Fattori,
    Silvestro Lega, Telemaco Signorini, Cristiano Banti and others, centred on the Diego Martelli room, Room
    11. Major works include Fattori's Battle of Magenta, Lo Staffato, and the Rotonda di Palmieri, Lega's Song of
    the Stornello, and Signorini's Leith.
    LISTEN FOR
    Who the Macchiaioli were, why they matter, and which two Fattori masterpieces each get their own track.

 You're now in the most important cluster of rooms in the Modern Art Gallery, rooms eight through
 sixteen. Slow down. This is the densest concentration of Macchiaioli painting anywhere on earth.

 Who they were. A small group of Tuscan painters, mostly based in Florence, who began working in the
 early eighteen sixties under the informal leadership of Giovanni Fattori, Silvestro Lega, and Telemaco
 Signorini. Their name, macchiaioli, from macchia, the Italian word for patch or stain, was originally a
 slur from hostile critics who mocked them for painting in patches of pure colour instead of carefully
 blended tones. The painters adopted the slur as a badge of honour.

 Their argument was that painting should come from direct observation, from outdoors, from real people
 in real light. They went out into the Tuscan countryside with paint boxes. They painted soldiers in the
 Italian Wars of Independence. Peasants, women at windows, Florentine streets, the Maremma marshes.
 They anticipated French Impressionism by about a decade. They were less radical than the
 Impressionists would be, they kept their drawing tight, their compositions careful, but they were the first
 European movement to argue that ordinary observed reality was a fit subject for serious painting.

 Walk the rooms in their own order. The landscape room gathers the mid-century painters who first went
 outdoors, among them the School of Staggia, the small group founded in eighteen fifty-four by Serafino
 de' Tivoli, who had brought the French Barbizon practice of painting from nature back to Tuscany. The
 heart of the wing is the Diego Martelli room, Room eleven, named for the critic and patron who
 championed the Macchiaioli and whose portrait they painted again and again. Around it: Lega's quiet
 domestic interiors of women reading, mending, talking; Cristiano Banti's collection; Signorini's harsher
 urban scenes, including his Leith of eighteen eighty-one, painted on a trip to Scotland; and the rooms of
 patriotic and historical genre that carry the wing toward Post-Unification and the Risorgimento.

 Fattori's late masterpieces hang in these rooms, and two of them get their own track. Find Lo Staffato
 first, a horizontal canvas of a black horse running across a parched landscape. Play the next track.
