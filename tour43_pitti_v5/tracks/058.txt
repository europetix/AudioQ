 The Funeral Garments of Cosimo I, Eleonora di
 Toledo, and Don Garzia
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of the climate-controlled case displaying the three sixteenth-century Medici funeral garments —
    Cosimo I, his wife Eleonora di Toledo, and their teenage son Don Garzia.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Three sixteenth-century garments preserved from the tombs of three Medici family members: Cosimo I de'
    Medici, the first Medici grand duke; his Spanish-born wife Eleonora di Toledo; and their fifteen-year-old son
    Don Garzia. The garments — silk damask, gold thread, intricate cuts — have been completely restored from
    severely decayed condition recovered from their tombs. They are the oldest surviving pieces of Italian
    Renaissance court costume.
    LISTEN FOR
    Who these three Medici were, how they died, why their clothes survived, and what the garments tell us about
    sixteenth-century Florentine life.

 Stand close. You're looking at three garments that should not exist.

 Cosimo the First de' Medici was the first Medici grand duke of Tuscany, who consolidated the family's
 hold on Florence in the fifteen thirties and forties. Eleonora di Toledo was his Spanish-born wife —
 daughter of the Viceroy of Naples, mother of eleven children, and one of the most powerful women in
 sixteenth-century Italy. Don Garzia was their fifth son, born in fifteen forty-seven.

 Their deaths form one of the darkest chapters of Medici family history. In November of fifteen
 sixty-two, Eleonora, Don Garzia, and his older brother Cardinal Giovanni were travelling together in the
 Tuscan Maremma — the marshy coastal region — when all three caught malaria. Giovanni died on the
 twentieth of November. Don Garzia died on the twelfth of December. Eleonora died on the seventeenth
 of December, less than a month after her son. A persistent rumour at the time — and for centuries after
 — held that Cosimo had murdered Don Garzia in a rage for some unspecified crime, and that the grief
 had killed Eleonora. The rumour is almost certainly false. Modern medical analysis of the remains has
 confirmed malaria. But the rumour stuck.

 Their garments survived because they were buried in them. The Medici family vault in the Cappelle
 Medicee in San Lorenzo, where the three were interred, kept the silk and gold thread of their funeral
 clothes in tolerable condition for four hundred years. In the late twentieth century the garments were
 exhumed for study and restoration, and have been on permanent display here since the museum opened.

 Look at Eleonora's dress. The silk is heavy damask, the embroidery is in real gold thread, the cut is the
 high Renaissance Spanish style she had brought with her from Naples. Bronzino had painted her in this
 same kind of dress — different colour, different season — in his famous portrait now in the Uffizi. The
 dress you are looking at is the same dress as the painted dress. Cloth history made visible.
