 Andrea del Sarto — Assumption of the Virgin
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of Andrea del Sarto's Assumption of the Virgin (the Passerini Assumption), one of the large
    altarpieces on the wall of the Sala dell'Iliade.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A tall altarpiece, oil on panel, painted around 1526. In the upper half, the Virgin rises into heaven on clouds
    surrounded by angels. In the lower half, the apostles cluster around her empty tomb, looking up or out in
    mingled awe and confusion. The colours are warm — terracotta reds, dusty blues, golds.
    LISTEN FOR
    Who Andrea del Sarto was, why this painting is in two parts, and the strange thing the apostles in the lower
    half are actually doing.

 Andrea del Sarto, who painted this around fifteen twenty-six, was for many Florentines the most
 accomplished painter working in the city between Raphael's departure for Rome and the rise of the next
 generation. Vasari, who knew him, called him pittore senza errori — the painter without errors. Which
 sounds like high praise until you realise Vasari also half-meant it as a criticism: too smooth, too correct,
 without the divine roughness of Michelangelo or the inventive fire of Raphael.

 Look at the painting. The composition is split into two zones: heaven above, earth below. In the upper
 half, the Virgin rises on a cloud carried by angels, in the assumption — the traditional Catholic belief
 that Mary was taken bodily into heaven at the end of her life, without dying. In the lower half, the
 apostles gather around her empty tomb. Some look up. Some look at one another. Some look at her
 empty grave-clothes laid on the ground.

 Now look more carefully at the apostles. They aren't all rapturous. The figure in the lower right is
 leaning down and reaching into the tomb, almost suspicious — checking whether she's really gone.
 Another, on the left, is gripped by a much older apostle, as if being told to calm down. This is what
 Andrea del Sarto was particularly good at: giving every figure in a crowd a different psychological
 reaction to the same event. The miracle is happening above. The human response to the miracle is
 happening below.

 The painting was commissioned by Margherita Passerini for the high altar of a church in Cortona, which
 is why it's sometimes called the Passerini Assumption. It came into the Medici collection in the
 eighteenth century and has been at the Pitti since.

 When you've looked enough, look for one more work in this room — Lorenzo Bartolini's Charity, the
 seated marble figure in the centre you noticed when you walked in. Play the next track in front of her.
