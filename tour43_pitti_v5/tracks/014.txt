 Titian — Portrait of a Man (The English
 Gentleman)
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of Titian's Portrait of a Man — sometimes called the Portrait of an English Gentleman — in the Sala
    di Marte. The painting is a half-length portrait of a bearded man in a dark coat and black hat.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A circa-1545 oil-on-canvas portrait. A bearded man, perhaps in his thirties, three-quarter view, looking
    directly out at us. He wears a high-collared black coat over a white shirt, and a soft black hat tilted slightly.
    The background is plain — a brownish-grey. His grey-blue eyes carry the painting.
    LISTEN FOR
    Who the sitter is — or isn't — and what makes a Titian portrait do what no other Renaissance portrait does.

 We don't know who he is. The painting has been called the Portrait of an English Gentleman since at
 least the seventeenth century, but no documentation supports the identification. He might be English. He
 might be Venetian. He might be one of the international diplomats or merchants who passed through
 Titian's Venetian studio in the fifteen forties, when he was painting portraits of half the courts of
 Europe.

 What we know is what we can see. Titian painted this around fifteen forty-five, when he was perhaps
 fifty-five and at the height of his powers. The portrait is, in a way, all about what's missing. There's no
 architectural setting. No symbolic objects. No coat of arms. No inscription telling you the sitter's name
 or station. Just a man in front of a flat background, looking at you.

 Look at his eyes. This is what Titian could do that no other Renaissance painter could quite manage.
 Look at the way the iris isn't a flat colour but a structure of small touches of different blues and greys.
 Look at the moisture at the inner corner of each eye. Look at the slightly different angle of each eye —
 not looking straight forward, but each focused at a slightly different distance, the way real eyes are
 when they're really looking at something.

 Now step back two paces. From two metres away, the brushwork dissolves and the face becomes real.
 Step close again. Up close, you can see every individual stroke of paint — rough, deliberate, almost
 casual. Titian in his late career painted with what his contemporaries called pittura di macchia — the
 painting of the patch. Strokes that resolve into faces only at distance. He had figured out, two hundred
 years before the Impressionists, that the eye completes what the brush only suggests.

 When you've finished, there's one more painting in this room worth meeting — a Van Dyck, on the next
 track.
