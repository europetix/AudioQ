 Antonio Canova — Venus Italica
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of Canova's Venus Italica, on the low pedestal in the centre of the Sala di Venere. The marble is
    life-sized, of a young woman pulling a cloth around herself.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A white marble sculpture, 1804-1812, of a young Venus stepping forward and slightly turning, drawing a
    length of damp cloth around her body in a gesture of modesty. Her weight rests on one leg. Her head is bent
    slightly forward, looking down and to the side.
    LISTEN FOR
    What Canova was responding to, what makes this Venus different from the antique one she replaced, and
    why she's been called the most romantic statue of the neoclassical age.

 Walk around her. She's meant to be seen from every side, and the carving changes each time you move.

 Antonio Canova was the most famous sculptor in Europe in eighteen-oh-four when he took this
 commission. The brief was difficult. Napoleon had taken the antique Medici Venus from the Pitti to
 Paris, and Canova was supposed to make a replacement. He could have made a copy. He chose not to.

 Look at her pose. The antique Medici Venus stands frontally, weight balanced, hands raised in a
 symmetrical gesture of modesty. Canova's Venus is doing the opposite. She's caught mid-movement,
 weight on one leg, turning her body away from us, pulling a cloth around her in a single twisting
 gesture. Her head is bent. Her gaze is downward. She isn't posing. She's reacting to having been seen.

 The cloth is the most extraordinary thing in the carving. Canova was famous in his lifetime for his
 ability to make stone look like cloth, and look at this drapery — the wet weight of it where it clings to
 her thigh, the soft folds where it falls away from her hand, the slight rumple at the small of her back.
 She's just stepped out of a bath. The cloth is what she's reached for.

 There's a story Canova told about the carving. He said that finishing the surface of a Venus was always
 the hardest part. The marble had to be worked and worked with fine abrasives and wax until it lost the
 look of stone and started to look like skin. Stand close. The shoulder catches the light differently from
 the back. The cheekbone differently from the forehead. It isn't a flat polish — it's a modulated surface
 that responds to where the light is coming from.

 Her pose has been called the most romantic of the neoclassical age. Looking down, vulnerable, turning
 away — she isn't a goddess in glory. She's a young woman caught off guard. The shift from the antique
 to the modern, here, is the shift from cosmic order to private feeling.

 When you've finished, find the Titian Concert on the wall — a small painting of three musicians, one of
 them turning to look out at us. Play the next track.
