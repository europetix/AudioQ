 Sala di Saturno — The Room of Raphael
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In the Sala di Saturno, the first Planet Room you enter on the modern visitor route. The ceiling carries an
    elaborate Baroque fresco of Saturn surrounded by allegorical figures.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A grand reception room with a Pietro da Cortona ceiling fresco (begun 1646, completed by Ciro Ferri in the
    1660s) showing Saturn — old age, time — with allegorical figures of Fame and Eternity. Walls hung with
    Renaissance and early Baroque paintings, with a heavy concentration of Raphael.
    LISTEN FOR
    Why this room is called Saturn, why it contains so many Raphaels, and the three Raphaels that each get their
    own track here.

 A heads-up before you go looking for this room. As of the latest museum updates, the Sala di Saturno
 has been temporarily closed for maintenance, so you may find it roped off when you arrive. If it is, the
 four Raphael masterpieces on the next tracks — the Madonna della Seggiola, Pope Leo the Tenth, the
 Madonna del Granduca, and the Madonna del Baldacchino — may have been moved to another room or
 taken off view for a while; glance at the signage or ask a gallery attendant where they've gone. The
 tracks will be ready whenever you find the paintings, or for when the room reopens. It's worth checking
 the Uffizi Galleries website before your visit.

 You've just stepped into one of the great rooms of European art. Take a breath before you start looking.

 First, the ceiling. It's by Pietro da Cortona, begun in sixteen forty-six and completed by his pupil Ciro
 Ferri in the sixteen sixties. Saturn — the old god, time, the slowest planet — stands at the centre, with
 allegories of Fame and Eternity around him. The frescoes in all five Planet Rooms together form a
 single programme written by Francesco Rondinelli, the Grand Duke's librarian. The programme follows
 a young prince through the stages of his education: tempted by the pleasures of Venus, instructed by
 Apollo, hardened by Mars, crowned by Jupiter, and finally in old age in this room — Saturn — joining
 the realm of eternity. Under the Medici, the visitor entered at Venus and worked through to Saturn as a
 kind of moral ascent. The modern museum reverses that route, so we end at Venus.

 The walls of this room are dominated by Raphael — four major works, the largest concentration of his
 Florentine and Roman portraits anywhere. They're here because Vittoria della Rovere, the
 seventeenth-century Medici grand duchess, brought them from her family's collection in Urbino.
 Without her inheritance, this room would not exist as it is.

 Four Raphaels get their own track. Start with the Madonna della Seggiola — a small round painting, a
 Virgin embracing the Christ Child, the most reproduced Madonna in Western art. Find it and play the
 next track.
