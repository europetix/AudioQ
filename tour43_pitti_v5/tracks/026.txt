 Raphael — La Gravida (The Pregnant Woman)
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of Raphael's La Gravida, in the Sala dell'Iliade. The painting is small — about sixty-five by fifty
    centimetres — and shows a seated young woman in profile-three-quarter view.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    An oil-on-panel portrait, painted around 1506. The sitter is a young woman, dressed in a dark gown with a
    white linen chemise visible at the neck. Her right hand rests on the curve of her pregnant abdomen. She turns
    her head almost to face the viewer, with a calm, direct look.
    LISTEN FOR
    Who she might be, why she has no name, and what makes this small painting one of the most quietly radical
    portraits of the Italian Renaissance.

 Move close. She rewards close attention.

 We don't know her name. The painting has been called La Gravida — the pregnant woman — for
 centuries, and we have no record of who commissioned it or who the sitter was. Best scholarship places
 it around fifteen-oh-six, when Raphael was twenty-three years old and spending time in Florence
 between his early career in Umbria and his summons to Rome by Pope Julius the Second. She would
 have been a Florentine woman, probably from a prosperous family — the cloth of her gown, the white
 chemise, the simple gold ring on her index finger all suggest moderate wealth without courtly
 ostentation.

 What makes the painting unusual for its moment is exactly what makes it ordinary now. Renaissance
 portraits of women were almost always about something other than the woman herself. A bride at her
 wedding. A widow in mourning. A donor on the side of an altarpiece. Raphael has stripped all of that
 away. No symbols, no inscription, no allegory. He's painted a specific person at a specific moment in
 her body — late second or early third trimester — and let her look at us.

 Look at her hand on her abdomen. It's not demonstrative. She's not pointing. The hand is just resting
 there the way a pregnant woman's hand often does, half-conscious of the weight. Her expression is calm
 and faintly amused. She knows we're looking at her and she's looking back, and the painting trusts us to
 handle that.

 Raphael painted three or four small portraits of Florentine women in these years, all of them carrying
 this same direct quality. La Gravida is the one that survives best because she's the least adorned and the
 most herself.

 When you've looked enough, find Andrea del Sarto's Assumption of the Virgin — a large altarpiece on
 the wall showing the Virgin rising into heaven surrounded by apostles. Play the next track.
