 Peter Paul Rubens — The Four Philosophers
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of Rubens's Four Philosophers, in the Sala di Marte. The painting is medium-sized — about a metre
    and a half wide — and shows four men gathered around a table, with a marble bust above them.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A 1611-1612 group portrait. Four men in dark scholarly clothing sit and stand around a table covered in a red
    cloth. One holds a book open. A vase of tulips and a small Roman bust sit on a shelf above them. Behind the
    group, an open window shows a glimpse of Roman ruins. The lighting is warm and intimate.
    LISTEN FOR
    Who the four men actually are, why Rubens included a self-portrait, and what the tulips on the shelf mean.

 This is a family painting, more than it is a philosophy painting. Rubens painted it in Antwerp in sixteen
 eleven, two years after he'd come home from a long stay in Italy, and the four men in it are tied together
 by close personal bonds.

 On the right, holding the open book, is Justus Lipsius — the great Flemish humanist and Latin scholar,
 the most influential teacher of his generation. Lipsius had taught both of the two men closest to him in
 the painting. To his right, in profile, is Philip Rubens — Peter Paul's older brother, a brilliant Latin
 scholar himself, who had died in Italy in sixteen eleven, the year this was painted. Lipsius himself had
 died in sixteen-oh-six. The painting is partly a memorial.

 On the left, half-turned away, is Joannes Woverius, another former student of Lipsius. And standing at
 the back, looking out at us — the only figure not part of the seated conversation — is Rubens himself.
 He's painted his own face into the group, slightly removed, as a witness rather than a participant. He
 wasn't a scholar. He wanted to be near them.

 Look at the small details. The marble bust on the shelf above is Seneca — the Roman Stoic philosopher
 Lipsius spent his life editing. The open book is one of Lipsius's editions of Seneca's letters. The two
 tulips in the vase are an early sign of the tulip mania that would grip the Netherlands within twenty
 years; they're also flowers that bloom and die quickly, the standard memento mori of Northern still-life
 painting.

 The painting is grief made visible, but warm, not bitter. Four men in a room together, two of them
 already dead when the paint was wet.

 When you've looked enough, find the much larger Rubens on the opposite wall — the Consequences of
 War — a violent, swirling allegorical scene. Play the next track.
