 Filippo Lippi — Madonna and Child with the Birth
 of the Virgin (Tondo Bartolini)
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of Filippo Lippi's Tondo Bartolini, in the Sala di Prometeo. The painting is round, about a metre and
    a third in diameter, and crowded with figures and architectural detail.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A circa-1452 oil and tempera tondo. In the foreground, the Virgin sits holding the Christ Child, who reaches
    eagerly for a pomegranate. Behind her, in deep architectural perspective, smaller scenes from the legendary
    life of the Virgin: the meeting of her parents Joachim and Anne at the Golden Gate of Jerusalem on the right,
    the birth of the Virgin herself in the background interior on the left.
    LISTEN FOR
    Why a single round painting contains multiple stories, who Filippo Lippi was, and what to look at in the
    architecture.

 Step in close. There's more going on in this round panel than first meets the eye.

 Filippo Lippi painted this around fourteen fifty-two for the Bartolini family of Florence. He was a
 Carmelite friar by training — though by this point in his life he had abandoned the monastic discipline
 almost entirely and was living with a former nun named Lucrezia Buti, with whom he eventually had a
 son, Filippino, who would become one of the most important Florentine painters of the next generation.
 Lippi the elder was one of the great storytellers of the early Renaissance.

 Look at the structure of the painting. The Virgin and Child in the foreground are the ostensible subject,
 but step into the round frame and you realise the painting contains at least three separate stories. In the
 deep background on the left, behind a series of open archways, you can see the birth of the Virgin
 herself — Saint Anne reclining on a bed, women bringing her food, a servant washing the baby. On the
 right, in a separate architectural space, Joachim and Anne meet at the Golden Gate of Jerusalem — the
 moment tradition held the Virgin was conceived. So what you're looking at is, in time, three generations
 layered into one frame: the Virgin's grandparents meeting, the Virgin being born, and the Virgin herself
 now seated with her son.

 Now look at the architecture. Lippi painted this in the decades immediately after Brunelleschi had
 revolutionised Florentine thinking about perspective. The arches receding into the deep background of
 the painting are a perspectival exercise — straight lines converging on a single vanishing point — but
 Lippi has bent the exercise toward storytelling. Each archway is a stage. Each stage holds an episode.
 The deeper you look into the painting, the further back in time you go.

 Look at the Christ Child reaching for the pomegranate. The pomegranate, with its many seeds inside a
 single fruit, was a traditional symbol of the Church and of resurrection — but the gesture here is the
 gesture of any baby reaching for something bright.

 When you've finished, walk through into the next small rooms, the Sala dell'Iride and the Sala del
 Bagno di Napoleone. Play the next track when you arrive.
