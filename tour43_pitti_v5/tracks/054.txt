 Giovanni Fattori — La Rotonda di Palmieri
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of Fattori's La Rotonda di Palmieri, in the Macchiaioli rooms, 8 to 16. The painting is tiny, twelve
    centimetres tall by thirty-five centimetres wide, and may take you a moment to find.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    An 1866 oil on wood panel. A circular wooden platform extends out toward the sea. Six or seven seated
    women in long dresses and hats are arranged in a row under a yellow striped awning. To their right, the calm
    blue Mediterranean. To their left, a thin slice of Tuscan coast. The whole composition fits into a panel the size
    of a thin notebook.
    LISTEN FOR
    What this small panel actually shows, who the women are, and why some Italian critics consider it the single
    greatest small painting of the nineteenth century.

 If you have to step very close, do. The panel is the size of a slim paperback book.

 Fattori painted this in eighteen sixty-six, at the Bagni Palmieri — a seaside establishment on the
 Livornese coast where respectable middle-class women went to take the sea air. The 'rotunda' of the title
 is the circular wooden platform jutting out over the water. The yellow striped awning above it is for
 shade. Underneath the awning, in a row, sit seven women.

 The conventions of nineteenth-century seaside bathing meant that respectable women could not wear
 bathing costumes — they were entirely covered in long dresses, jackets, hats, and shawls, and what they
 actually did at the seaside was sit in the shade and breathe in the salt air. That's what's happening in this
 painting. Seven women on holiday, doing what their society permitted them to do.

 Fattori painted from memory and from preliminary studies in a sketchbook he carried with him. The
 figures are tiny — none of them larger than a fingernail — and yet each one is an individual. Some lean
 forward. Some lean back. One is in profile, head turned toward the sea. Another is in three-quarter view,
 talking to a neighbour. You can't see their faces in detail. But you can read each posture, each angle of
 head, each configuration of body in a chair.

 Look at the colour. The yellow of the awning, the pale Mediterranean blue, the soft greys of the
 women's clothing, the strip of beige coast on the left. The whole panel is built from broad strokes of
 pure colour, sitting side by side without blending. This is the macchia at its purest — and it's eight years
 before Monet's Impression, Sunrise. Fattori was thirty-eight years old.

 Italian critics have argued for a hundred and fifty years about which of Fattori's small panels is the
 greatest. This one is usually near the top of the list. Some put it at the top.

 When you've finished, walk on through the remaining rooms, seventeen through thirty. Play the next
 track when you arrive.
