 Raphael — Madonna dell'Impannata
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of Raphael's Madonna dell'Impannata, in the Sala di Ulisse. The painting is medium-sized — about a
    metre and a half tall — and shows five figures grouped around the Christ Child.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    An oil-on-panel painting from around 1513-1514. The Virgin sits holding the Christ Child, who twists in her
    lap to look at the infant John the Baptist on the right. Saint Elizabeth — John's mother — stands behind, and
    Saint Anne — the Virgin's mother — looks on from the left. Behind the group, a window covered with oiled
    paper (the impannata) catches a softer, diffused light.
    LISTEN FOR
    What an impannata actually is, the five-figure composition Raphael is building, and why this painting is
    partly a Raphael and partly a workshop production.

 The painting's name is its background. An impannata, in Italian, is the oiled-paper windowpane that
 filled the role glass would later play in Italian windows. Cheaper than glass, more rigid than cloth, it
 diffused the light through a yellowish, milky surface and made rooms warmer in winter. Raphael
 painted an impannata across the window in the background of this painting, and the gentle
 yellow-cream light of that window falls evenly across all five figures.

 He painted this around fifteen-thirteen, in Rome, during his years as court painter to Pope Leo the
 Tenth. By this point his workshop was producing paintings on an industrial scale — commissions
 arriving faster than Raphael could complete them personally, and a team of skilled younger painters
 working alongside him on backgrounds, drapery, and secondary figures while Raphael handled the
 faces. This painting shows that division clearly. The faces — especially the Virgin and the Christ Child
 — are Raphael himself. The hands are mostly Raphael. The drapery of Saint Anne and the architectural
 backdrop were probably painted by Giulio Romano, his most talented assistant.

 Look at the composition. Five figures fit into a tight pyramidal space. The Christ Child at the centre is
 turning in his mother's lap to reach toward the infant John on the right — the cousin who would one day
 prepare the way for him. The two children making eye contact is the moment the picture turns on.
 Everything else — the three older women — is framing.

 The painting came to the Pitti through the della Rovere inheritance, the same path that brought the great
 Raphaels into the Saturn Room. Cardinal Bibbiena, Raphael's close friend, may have commissioned it
 for his own household — the painting was certainly in his estate at his death in fifteen twenty.
