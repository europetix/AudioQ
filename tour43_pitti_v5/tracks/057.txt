 Museum of Costume and Fashion — The Palazzina
 della Meridiana
    WHERE YOU ARE
    At the entrance to the Museum of Costume and Fashion, in the Palazzina della Meridiana — a separate small
    palace attached to the southern wing of the Pitti, with its own ceremonial entrance.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A small palace begun in 1776 for Grand Duke Pietro Leopoldo and finished in 1840. The museum reopened
    at the end of 2023 after four years of renovation, with a new arrangement of the collections. In the vestibule,
    look up: a 1693 ceiling fresco by Anton Domenico Gabbiani — the Allegory of Time and the Arts — and on
    the floor, the bronze meridian line installed by Vincenzo Viviani in 1699.
    LISTEN FOR
    What the Palazzina della Meridiana is, what the meridian line in the floor actually does, and how the museum
    is currently organised.

 Two things to know before you go in. First, this building is itself a Florentine monument. Second, the
 museum reopened at the end of twenty twenty-three with a new arrangement after four years of
 renovation, so any older guidebook you have is out of date.

 The Palazzina della Meridiana — the small Palace of the Meridian — was begun in seventeen
 seventy-six for Grand Duke Pietro Leopoldo and finished in eighteen-forty. The building served, across
 its history, as a residence for the Lorraine, Savoy, and assorted regents.

 The meridian in the building's name is literal. Stop in the entrance vestibule and look down at the floor.
 A long thin bronze line runs across the marble, marked with metal points at intervals — the days of the
 year. Above your head the ceiling has a pinhole. At solar noon on any given day, a spot of sunlight falls
 through the pinhole and lands on the bronze line at exactly the right point for that day. The installation
 was the work of Vincenzo Viviani in sixteen ninety-nine — Galileo's last pupil, carrying his teacher's
 scientific legacy forward.

 Look up. The vestibule ceiling shows Gabbiani's Allegory of Time and the Arts from sixteen
 ninety-three — an appropriate fresco for a room with a meridian line. Time in the floor, time on the
 ceiling.

 Inside the museum, the collection of over fifteen thousand pieces is shown in rotation, with displays
 changing every two to three years. The permanent core is the sixteenth-century funeral garments of
 three Medici. One W-track on those. Play the next track when you find them in their dedicated case.
