 Giovanni Fattori — Self-Portrait, 1854
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of Giovanni Fattori's Self-Portrait of 1854, in the portraits room, Room 8, at the start of the
    Macchiaioli wing. The painting is small, a half-length portrait of a young man with dark hair and beard.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    An oil on canvas from 1854. Giovanni Fattori, twenty-eight years old, painted himself in three-quarter view
    against a plain dark background. He wears a dark coat over a white collar. His expression is direct, slightly
    wary, mature beyond his age. The brushwork is already loose for the period.
    LISTEN FOR
    Who Fattori was, what he was about to do to Italian painting, and what's worth seeing in this small portrait of
    a young man.

 Lean in. It's a small canvas, and it gives back what you bring to it.

 Giovanni Fattori was twenty-eight when he painted this. He'd been born in Livorno on the Tuscan coast,
 the son of a working-class family, and had moved to Florence in his teens to study at the Accademia di
 Belle Arti. By eighteen fifty-four he was still a student painter, still working in the academic style —
 historical scenes, careful compositions, conventional finish. The self-portrait in front of you was made
 in that conventional manner. Tight brushwork. Restrained colour. A young man being serious about
 himself.

 But look at his eyes. There's something watchful in them, almost suspicious. Fattori would become,
 within a decade, the central figure of the most important Italian painting movement of the nineteenth
 century — the Macchiaioli — and he would do it by rejecting almost everything the Accademia di Belle
 Arti had taught him. He would start painting outdoors, directly from observation. He would use patches
 of pure colour — macchie — instead of careful tonal blending. He would paint Tuscan peasants,
 soldiers, landscapes, and small ordinary moments instead of grand historical themes.

 By the eighteen sixties, Fattori would be painting the Battle of Magenta — a major work elsewhere in
 these Macchiaioli rooms — in a style that anticipated Impressionism by ten years. By the eighteen
 eighties he would paint Lo Staffato and the Rotonda di Palmieri, two of the small masterpieces of the
 entire nineteenth century, both of which are also ahead of you.

 Look one more time at the face of the young man in this self-portrait. He doesn't yet know any of this.
 He's a student painter with a serious face, painting himself the way Italian painters had painted
 themselves for centuries.

 When you've looked enough, walk on through the Macchiaioli rooms, eight through sixteen, with Room
 eleven, the Diego Martelli room, at their heart. This is the Macchiaioli core. Play the next track when
 you reach the first of them.
