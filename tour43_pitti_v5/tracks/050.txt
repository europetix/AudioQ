 Rooms 5–7 — Romanticism, History, and into the
 Macchiaioli
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In Rooms 5, 6, and 7 of the Modern Art Gallery, the rooms of Romanticism and history painting, covering
    Italian painting from the 1820s to the 1850s.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Three rooms: Francesco Hayez and the Romantic historical genre; a room of Florentine and Neapolitan
    painters turning toward a plainer realism; and the room of Antonio Ciseri and the commemorative portrait.
    The track then leads on into the Macchiaioli rooms, 8 to 16.
    LISTEN FOR
    What Italian Romanticism looked like, the turn toward realism, and the Fattori self-portrait in the first
    Macchiaioli room, Room 8, that gets its own track.

 Three rooms of Romanticism and history painting now, rooms five through seven, each a step toward
 what's coming. You're in the eighteen twenties to the eighteen fifties, the moment when European
 Romanticism reached Italy and began to transform Italian painting.

 Look for Francesco Hayez in the room of Romantic historical genre. He was the great Italian Romantic
 painter of the period, trained in Venice, settled in Milan, painter of huge dramatic historical scenes and
 tear-filled romantic narratives. His most famous painting, The Kiss, is in Milan, but the Pitti owns
 several smaller Hayez works, including portraits and historical scenes. His style is what Italian
 audiences in the Risorgimento generation wanted: rich colour, literary subject, intense human emotion.

 The two rooms that follow turn from drama toward observation. One gathers Florentine and Neapolitan
 painters feeling their way toward a plainer realism, an inquiry into the real, as the room is themed. The
 last of the three belongs to Antonio Ciseri and the commemorative portrait: grave, polished, dignified
 likenesses of the men the new Italy wanted to remember.

 Past these rooms, and past the grand Ballroom, the Sala da Ballo, in the corner, you cross into the heart
 of the gallery: the Macchiaioli and mid-nineteenth-century rooms, eight through sixteen. The first of
 them is a room of portraits from the years when Florence was briefly the capital of Italy. Look there for
 the small self-portrait by Giovanni Fattori from eighteen fifty-four. Fattori would soon become the most
 important Italian painter of the second half of the nineteenth century, the central figure of the
 Macchiaioli, but this is him as a twenty-eight-year-old. Find it and play the next track.
