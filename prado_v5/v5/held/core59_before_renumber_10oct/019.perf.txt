[curious] In 1549, the emperor Charles the Fifth ordered a bronze of himself. What he got has a secret: the armour comes off.

First, where you are. This is Floor 1, the main floor, and you're in Room 1, the round room by the Goya entrance. From here the long Central Gallery runs the length of the building, like a spine. The great Spanish rooms, El Greco, Ribera and Velázquez, open off one side of it. We'll start with Titian, just beside this room, then work our way round to Velázquez.

Now the emperor. The sculptor was Leone Leoni, and the figure and its armour were cast in Milan. His son Pompeo later finished the work in Spain. At the emperor's feet lies Fury, the image of disorder and rebellion, among a pile of weapons. The Latin words say it plainly: Fury, tamed by Caesar's virtue.

[conspiratorial] And here's the secret. The armour is a separate bronze shell, held on with large screws. Take it off, and underneath stands the emperor as a nude hero, in the classical manner. Look closely at how he's shown today, and see which emperor you've got.

The museum has been moving its Leoni bronzes. If this group isn't here in Room 1, you'll find the Leoni bronzes in the Central Gallery, Room 27, later on this route.

From the round Room 1, step into Room 40, by the Goya entrance lift, and walk on through Room 41 to Room 42, for Titian's Andrians. On the way, in Room 41, there's a short Extra on Titian's self-portrait: play it there if you have time.
