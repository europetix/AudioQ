[warmly] In 1644 the king rode out to war in Aragon. Among those who went with him was a court buffoon known as El Primo. Velázquez went too, and painted his portrait there.

The court called people like him men of pleasure. Jesters, dwarfs, people whose bodies or minds set them apart, kept to amuse the royal family. Velázquez painted them again and again, and he gave them something the court rarely did. He looked at them as people.

El Primo sits on the ground in a red coat, legs straight out, fists resting on his thighs. For a long time this painting was called Sebastián de Morra. Only recently has the sitter been identified as El Primo. [pause] What hasn't changed is his gaze. Few portraits by Velázquez connect so directly with whoever stands in front of them.

Francisco Lezcano, the boy from Vallecas, came from the Basque Country and served the little Prince Baltasar Carlos. He sits beneath a crag in the Guadarrama hills, holding what looks like a pack of cards, as if inviting you into the game. The hat under his foot shows how small he was. Juan Calabazas, known as Calabacillas, crouches beside the gourd that gave him his name, his face almost out of focus. It may be Velázquez's most radical portrait.

[curious] Then Pablo de Valladolid, all in black, caught in the middle of a speech. There's no floor and no wall, nothing but a faint shadow, and yet he stands in real space. Over two centuries later, Manet called it possibly the most astonishing piece of painting ever made, and based his famous Fifer on it.

Next door is Room 15A. Look for a dim workshop of women spinning wool, with a bright room and a tapestry behind them. That's The Spinners.
