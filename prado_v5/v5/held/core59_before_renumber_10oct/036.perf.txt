[curious] The most famous portrait of the Emperor Charles the Fifth shows no battle at all. No enemy, no army, no dead. Just a man in armour, riding alone towards evening.

The battle was real. On 24 April 1547, at Mühlberg, Charles defeated the Protestant princes of the Schmalkaldic League. The next year Titian came north to Augsburg to paint him. The writer Aretino suggested showing the beaten enemy under the horse's hooves. It was turned down. Most of Charles's own soldiers were Protestants, and he was trying to reconcile the two faiths. So Titian painted a Christian knight, with a lance that recalls Saint George, and the emperors of ancient Rome.

[reverent] Look at the armour, trimmed with gold, with an image of the Virgin and Child on the breastplate. It was made for him by the armourer Desiderius Helmschmid, and it's still kept in the Royal Armoury here in Madrid. There's even a wheel-lock pistol on the saddle, then a modern weapon.

The painting belonged to Charles's sister, Mary of Hungary. She is the thread that ties this room together. Look at the two dark canvases of figures in eternal torment. The giant Tityus lies chained while an eagle tears at his liver. Sisyphus, a king who defied the gods, staggers under his rock. Mary chose these punished rebels for the great hall of her palace at Binche, hung high between the windows. They were a warning to the German princes who had defied her brother. The other two of the four, Ixion and Tantalus, were lost in the Alcázar fire of 1734. This Tityus is a later version by Titian himself.

The Leoni bronze in this room, by Leone and Pompeo Leoni, is her nephew, the future Philip the Second, aged twenty-four. Mary ordered it too. Look for the eagle's head on the pommel of his sword.

[quietly] One family's power, in three moods. The calm rider, the threat, and the heir.

Walk one room along the gallery to Room 26. Look for a sleeping young hunter stretched across the lap of Venus, and a little Cupid holding back a dog.
