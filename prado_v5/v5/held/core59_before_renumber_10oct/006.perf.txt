[curious] The young man in this portrait is wearing gloves, fine grey kidskin. Those gloves are the whole argument of the painting.

Albrecht Dürer painted himself in 1498, and he tells you so. The inscription says he painted it after his own likeness, at twenty-six. In his day, a painter in Germany was a craftsman, someone who worked with his hands. So Dürer covers his painting hands in gloves, a sign of high rank. He dresses as a gentleman, in a black-and-white doublet and a striped cap, with a cloak tied by a blue-and-white cord. The message is clear. Painting is a liberal art, a work of the mind, and the painter belongs with the learned and the well-born.

The portrait had a long journey to Madrid. The city council of Nuremberg gave it to Charles the First of England. After Charles was executed, his collection was sold, and the painting was bought for Don Luis de Haro, who gave it to Philip the Fourth.

[pause] Now turn to the two tall panels, Adam and Eve. Dürer painted them in 1507, after his second trip to Italy. He wanted to join the Italian ideal of the perfect body to the German eye for precise detail. These are the first life-size nudes in all of northern painting. Notice how he tells them apart by colour. Adam is tanned, Eve is a pinkish white. Adam holds a branch with an apple. Eve takes the apple from the serpent.

Look for the little tablet beside Eve. Its inscription dates the work to 1507 years after the Virgin gave birth. The museum reads it as a reminder of Mary as the new Eve, who undoes the sin these two are about to commit.

[quietly] These panels came to Philip the Fourth as a gift from Queen Christina of Sweden. A self-made gentleman, and the first man and woman, all in one room.

Before you go, there's a short Extra on Baldung Grien's Ages of Woman and Death in this room. Next door is Room 56B: the early Italian Renaissance. Look for 56B at the doorway, and a large golden Annunciation.
