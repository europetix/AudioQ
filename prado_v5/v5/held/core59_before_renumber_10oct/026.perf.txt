[curious] People have wanted this man to be somebody famous. Miguel de Cervantes, perhaps, the author of Don Quixote. Or the courtier Antonio Pérez. The truth is, nobody knows who he is.

The painter, at least, we know rather better. Domenikos Theotokopoulos was born on Crete in 1541, and Spain simply called him El Greco, the Greek. His colour owes much to Venice, and his heroic figures to Michelangelo in Rome. Then he came to Spain, settled in Toledo, and died there. This is one of the first portraits he painted in Spain.

[pause] As for the sitter, the suggestion the museum finds most convincing is Juan de Silva, Marquis of Montemayor, a military commander who also held the post of chief notary. And that would explain the hand. Spread flat on his chest, it looks like a man taking an oath. For a notary, swearing to the truth was the job.

[quietly] Now spend a moment with him. Almost everything is black, the doublet and the background. Against it, three things stand out: the pale face, the white ruff, and that hand, its long fingers spread on his heart. Look just below it for the gilded hilt of his sword, glinting against the black. A gentleman's sword, and a gentleman's word.

His eyes meet yours, calm and steady. He isn't performing for anyone, he's simply there, quietly promising something.

The portrait came to the royal collection as a gift. The widow of the Duke of Arco gave it, and the duke had owned six El Greco portraits of gentlemen.

[warmly] We may never learn his name. But four centuries later, he's still keeping his promise.
