[curious] Rembrandt painted this canvas in 1634, when he was about twenty-eight. And people have argued ever since about who she is. A widowed queen, about to drink her husband's ashes? Or a heroine on her way to kill a general?

These top-floor rooms hold Flemish and Dutch painting. For historical reasons, many of the great Dutch names never reached the Spanish royal collection. The museum has described this as the only fully accepted Rembrandt in a Spanish public collection.

For a long time she was called Artemisia, the queen who mixed her husband's ashes into her wine. [pause] The museum now reads her differently. It sees Judith, the widow who went into the enemy camp, sat at the banquet of the general Holofernes, and came home with his head.

So look for clues. A girl kneels with her back to us, offering a cup made from a nautilus shell on a golden stem. Behind, in the shadows, an old servant in a white cap holds a cloth. [conspiratorial] Look again. It's a half-open sack, with a tasselled cord hanging down. In the story, that's what Judith's maid carries the head home in.

X-rays show that Rembrandt changed his mind. Another figure once stood between the two women, perhaps holding a mirror. And the black background was added much later, by a restorer.

[wry] Now the twist. For the Dutch, Judith was a patriotic heroine, a symbol of their stand against Spain. Yet the painting turns up in the collection of a Spanish minister, the Marquis of Ensenada, listed simply as a Judith. Later it hangs in the palace of King Charles the Third.

[warmly] So enjoy the light on her pearls, the gold chain, the soft ermine at her shoulders. A rebels' heroine, glowing in the king's house.

Walk on through Rooms 77, 78 and 79 to the round, darkened Room 79B. That's the Dauphin's Treasure, a ring of glittering cups and vases in one long curved case. On the way, Rooms 77, 78 and 79 each have a short Extra: play them there if you have time.
