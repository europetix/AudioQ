[quietly] Everyone in this painting has just noticed someone come in. The princess turns her eyes. The painter pauses, brush raised. Even the man on the steps at the back has stopped. [pause] And the people they're looking at are standing more or less where you are.

The date is 1656. The place, according to his early biographer Palomino, is a room in the old royal palace of Madrid, the very room you see. At the centre stands the Infanta Margarita, the king's daughter. Two maids of honour attend her. María Agustina Sarmiento kneels to offer her a little red jug, and Isabel de Velasco bows. These girls are the meninas of the title. Old palace inventories called the picture something else, the family of Philip the Fourth.

On the right are two court dwarfs, Mari Bárbola and Nicolasito Pertusato, who is prodding a large, sleepy mastiff with his foot. Behind them, the veiled Marcela de Ulloa talks with an attendant. And framed in the bright doorway at the back is José Nieto, the palace chamberlain.

[curious] Now find the mirror on the back wall. Two soft faces glow in it, Philip the Fourth and Queen Mariana, the Infanta's parents. They're watching the scene, and you're standing in their place. On the left, Velázquez works at a vast canvas that we only see from behind. Is he painting the king and queen? Or this very picture? Nobody knows for certain.

On his chest is the red cross of the Order of Santiago. The story goes that it was added later, once he had been made a knight.

Now step close. The princess's dress falls apart into quick dabs of grey and white. Step back, and it turns to silk, and the whole room fills with air.

[warmly] Palomino called it the most illustrious of all his works. Not bad for a picture of a little girl who has been asked to stand still.

Leave Room 12 through the wide doorway into the Central Gallery. In Room 27, look for Titian's emperor on horseback, in gleaming armour, his lance lowered.
