[lightly] After firing squads, deathbeds and Goya's dark walls, the Prado lets you out into the sun.

In the summer of 1909, Joaquín Sorolla went home to Valencia for three months, from late June to late September. He spent the season painting by the sea. He signed this canvas with the next year's date, but the museum thinks he most likely painted it that summer.

Notice first what's missing. There's no sky and no horizon. Sorolla brings you right down to the water's edge, so close that the three boys fill the picture. They're not a detail in a seascape. They're the subject.

Now look at the water. What caught Sorolla was the movement of the sea itself, and he turns it into pure painting. Broad strokes of turquoise, blue, violet and mauve slide over each other as the wave runs up the sand.

[curious] Then follow the light from front to back. On the nearest boy, the highlights are matte, thick dabs of white paint. On the boy furthest away, half in the sea, they're bright and glassy. The deeper the boys lie in the water, the more they shine. Look at the shadows. They aren't brown or black. They're violet. And the skin runs from white with mauve reflections to a deep reddish bronze.

Sorolla wasn't the first to paint children by the sea. But unlike Mariano Fortuny before him, he cared above all about water in motion. Ten years later Sorolla gave this picture away himself, and in time it came to the Prado.

[warmly] On this floor, Spain's nineteenth century opened with Goya's night of executions. It closes here, with a summer by the sea.
