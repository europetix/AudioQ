[amused] One hundred and seventy-nine rubies and five diamonds. That's what a palace clerk counted on this little cup in 1746. Count them today and you'll find a hundred and seventy-seven rubies, and just two diamonds. Somewhere along the way, a few stones went missing.

You're standing in the Dauphin's Treasure. These vessels belonged to Louis, the Grand Dauphin, heir to the throne of France. When he died, they passed to his son, Philip the Fifth, the first Bourbon king of Spain. In the nineteenth century they came to the Prado. Now the whole treasure is shown together in this round room, in a single curved case about forty metres long.

[pause] Now find the mermaid. She's small, about seventeen centimetres high. [warmly] She has a golden body, a headdress of enamelled feathers, and not one tail but two, curling away in coloured enamel. With both arms she lifts a bowl of carved agate above her head. Below, four little enamelled fish, perhaps dolphins, hold everything up on an agate base.

What was it for? The museum suggests it may have been a salt cellar. Imagine the salt arriving at a royal table, carried by a mermaid. Who made it is a puzzle too. It has been called Florentine, but it was probably the work of a French goldsmith, made under the spell of the great Cellini.

It has had adventures. In 1813 the treasure was stolen, and it came back in 1816. [quietly] And this cup has lost one piece for good: a little dolphin that once sat between the mermaid's tails.

So lean in close. More than four centuries old, no taller than your hand, and still a few rubies short.
