[reverent] This saint doesn't look at you. He doesn't need to. He sits face-on, perfectly still, wrapped in more gold than you can take in at once.

These rooms around Room 51 hold some of the museum's oldest paintings, from medieval Spain. In the fifteenth century, Spanish painters looked hard at Flanders, at the oil paint and close detail you've just seen. They married it to the gold and glory of the medieval altar. The museum calls this panel one of the most representative images of Spanish Gothic painting.

Bartolomé Bermejo painted it between 1474 and 1477, as the centrepiece of a great altarpiece for the church of Saint Dominic of Silos in Daroca, in Aragon. Its carved frame survives too, and it's also in the Prado. Imagine it in place, with real wooden pinnacles and tracery around this painted throne. Bermejo was playing with you. Which gold is carved, and which is only paint?

[curious] Now go closer. The cope is gold brocade, woven with a pattern of pine cones or artichokes. Its border is a row of tiny saints, each standing under its own little canopy. The mitre is so exact that Bermejo may have copied a real one he had seen. The knot on the crozier looks like a monstrance made for Girona Cathedral.

Then look at the sides of the throne. Small women in bright colours sit in its niches. They are the Virtues, faith, hope and charity among them. They surround the saint like a guard of honour, a sign of his moral perfection.

[pause] Face and pose are rigid and ancient. The fabric, metal and jewels are as real as anything in Flanders. That tension is the whole charm of Spanish painting in these years.
