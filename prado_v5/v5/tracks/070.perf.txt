[curious] Between Goya the tapestry designer and Goya the witness of war, take a breath with two young men who have stood together for about two thousand years.

They were carved around 10 BC, in the age of the emperor Augustus. The museum gives them to a pupil of the sculptor Pasiteles. Like many Roman sculptors, he borrowed from the Greeks. He took two figures from different centuries of Greek art and blended them so smoothly that you can't see the join.

Their later journey reads like a roll call of great collectors. The Ludovisi family in Rome. Queen Christina of Sweden. Then the Spanish crown, and the palace of La Granja de San Ildefonso, where they belonged to the collection of Queen Isabel Farnese. That palace gave the group its name.

But who are they? [wry] The royal inventories called them Castor and Pollux, the heavenly twins. The museum's title calls them Orestes and Pylades, the most loyal pair of friends in Greek legend. Many viewers simply see what's in front of you: friendship, and a brotherly arm around the shoulder.

Now look closely, because there's a confession to make. The original head was replaced in modern times with the head from an ancient bust of Antinous. The arms are modern too, though they keep roughly the old position.

Follow the torch in the right hand. It points down towards a small altar carved with the skull of an ox. Then step back and see the gentle S-curve running through the two bodies. The group was made to be seen from the front, just where you're standing.

[warmly] The kings who hired Goya didn't only collect paintings. They collected the ancient world.

Now follow the Goya signs to Room 67, near the foot of the stairs by the Murillo entrance. It's the last of the Goya rooms on this floor, and it holds the Black Paintings.
