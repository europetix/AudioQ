[amused] Down in the grass at the bottom of this picture lies a sheet of music. It's real, and you could still sing it. It's a round by Adriaen Willaert, a Flemish musician at the court of Ferrara. The words say, more or less, that whoever drinks and doesn't drink again doesn't know what drinking is.

Titian was the painter of the Habsburgs. The emperor Charles the Fifth made him a knight in 1533, and he worked for the family for over forty years. Yet he refused, again and again, to come and settle at the Spanish court. He stayed in Venice. He'd already said no to the Duke of Ferrara, who collected pagan scenes like this one for a private room.

The island is Andros, so loved by the wine god Bacchus that a stream there runs with wine. In the background an old man lies on a bed of branches. He's probably the river itself. [curious] But where's Bacchus? Look out to sea. A ship is sailing away, and we're meant to imagine the god and his followers on board. The party carries on without him.

Now find the sleeping woman in the lower right corner, a splendid nude, bathed in light. She isn't Ariadne, as some have claimed. Titian seems to have worked from a living model. And just beside her, a little boy is cheerfully relieving himself. For Titian's audience, he stood for laughter.

The picture was copied again and again by later painters. Its signature is easy to miss: Ticianus, low in the centre.

[warmly] You passed the man himself in Room 41. In his self-portrait he's in his seventies, seen in profile, which was rare. Otherwise, Titian painted profiles only of sitters who had already died. He holds a brush and wears the golden chain of his knighthood. Painter and knight, as his medal by Leone Leoni put it.
