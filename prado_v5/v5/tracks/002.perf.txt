[curious] There's a man in a boat in the middle of this painting, and he's about the size of your thumbnail. Yet the whole picture turns on him, and on the passenger he's carrying.

Welcome to the painters of the Low Countries. Around 1500, artists in Flanders began letting the landscape take over the picture. Joachim Patinir was one of the first to make landscape the star, and this panel, painted around 1520, shows how far he could take it. It was probably owned by Philip the Second.

The boatman is Charon, who in Greek myth ferries the dead across the river Styx. But Patinir mixes myth with Christian belief. On the left bank, angels walk with souls through a soft green paradise. On the right, the three-headed dog Cerberus guards the way into hell. The river runs between them like a road with two exits.

[pause] Now find the passenger. Charon has reached the middle of the river, the point where the choice is made. The soul sits in strict profile, and the museum says he's facing the easy road, the one that leads to perdition. The decision, it seems, is already taken.

[quietly] It's a reminder to prepare for death, painted only a few years after Luther shook the Church in 1517. Patinir borrowed ideas from Bosch, whom you'll meet in Room 56A. But X-rays show he took things out as he worked. A giant demon by the riverbank was painted out. Devils and damned souls were thinned out and made smaller. Little by little, the landscape won.

So the real drama here is in the scenery. Still water, blue mountains fading into the distance, and one small soul who has to choose.
