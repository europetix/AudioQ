[curious] In the spring of 1800, at the palace of Aranjuez, the royal family of Spain sat for Goya one by one. He painted ten small oil studies of them from life, and five still survive. Then he put them all into one picture, fourteen figures in all, counting himself.

This is the first of three Goyas on our route. Here, the court painter at his peak. Upstairs on Floor 2, the young tapestry designer. Down on Floor 0, the deaf old man of the war and the Black Paintings. Born in a village near Zaragoza, he'd been made First Court Painter just months before this portrait.

Look at the centre. It's Queen María Luisa who stands in the middle, not the king. Her pose copies the little Infanta Margarita in Las Meninas, which you saw in Room 12. King Charles the Fourth stands a step forward, heavy with orders, the Golden Fleece among them. On the left, in blue, is Prince Fernando, the future king.

[wry] Some historians read the queen's pose as a cruel satire of an ageing woman. The museum sees something else, the triumph of a fertile queen surrounded by her heirs. Goya gives you both at once. Dazzling silk and diamonds, and faces that are utterly real.

Now find the painter. At the back on the left, in the half-light beside a huge canvas, stands Goya himself, his eyes level with the king's. It's the trick Velázquez played in Las Meninas, borrowed by a man who knew that picture well.

[quietly] One last detail. Next to Fernando, a young woman turns her face away from us. She's probably his future bride, who hadn't yet been chosen. A royal portrait with a blank space for the future.

The museum plans to rehang Rooms 32 to 38, with no date announced. If this room is closed or changed, follow the Goya signs or ask a member of staff.

Leave Room 32 by the far side into Room 34, then walk through 35, 36 and 37 to Room 38: two paintings of one woman on a green couch. Room 36 has a short Extra on Goya's self-portrait, if you have time.
