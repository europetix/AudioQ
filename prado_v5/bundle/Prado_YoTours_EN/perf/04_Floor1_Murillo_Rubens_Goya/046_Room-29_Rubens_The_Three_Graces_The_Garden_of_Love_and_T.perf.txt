[warmly] In 1626, Rubens lost his first wife. At the end of 1630 he married again, a young woman called Helena Fourment. From then on, as one catalogue entry puts it, he lived in a state of infatuation. This room is full of it.

Begin with The Three Graces, a painting Rubens made for himself. It was still in his house when he died. There's no known sketch for it, because Rubens made sketches for clients and assistants, never for himself. The Graces are Brightness, Joy and Bloom, goddesses of generosity, of giving and giving back. The one on the left may well have Helena's face.

[quietly] Look at how they touch, hands on shoulders, arms around waists, the soft weight of real bodies. Above them hangs a garland of red and white roses. The museum's own text has a lovely line for it. Thanks is what Rubens is saying to life.

The Garden of Love is the same happiness, shared out among friends. Elegant couples gather in a garden, while chubby cupids, some with bird wings and some with insect wings, push them together. A statue of Venus presides from her grotto. The little boy flying overhead with a torch is Hymen, god of marriage. And the woman in blue bears a striking resemblance to Helena. After the king's death, it was recorded hanging in the very room where Philip the Fourth had died.

[amused] The very long canvas is The Judgement of Paris, ordered by the king himself around 1638. Paris must decide which goddess is the most beautiful. He chooses Venus, she promises him Helen of Troy, and the Trojan War follows. The king's brother, who handled the order in Flanders, thought it one of Rubens's best. He also worried, in his letters, that the goddesses were rather too naked. [wry] The king seems not to have minded.

Through to the octagonal Room 32, the first of the Goya rooms. Look for 32 above the doorway, and Goya's great portrait of the family of King Charles the Fourth.
