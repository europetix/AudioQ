[quietly] An angel, hardly more than a child, is holding up the body of Christ. He can barely manage the weight. And he's crying.

Antonello da Messina painted this small panel around 1475, just after a trip to Venice. There he saw the Pietàs of Giovanni Bellini, with the dead Christ held up by angels, and he borrowed the idea. Then he took it home to Sicily.

[pause] Look past the figures, into the background. That harbour town is Messina, Antonello's own city. He paints it with tiny, northern precision, the same patience he gives to every strand of Christ's hair. But the body itself is pure Italy. It has real weight and volume, a heavy body that sags against the angel's arm.

Now look at the angel's face, pressed close to Christ's. An inventory written in 1633 already described him as weeping. That's what people have noticed first, for almost four centuries.

[amused] And yet, for most of that time, nobody knew who painted it. A Spanish countess received it as a work by Dürer. It ended up in a convent in Galicia, in the north of Spain. When Napoleon's armies invaded, the nuns hid their treasures in private houses. When it finally reached the Prado in 1965, it was first given to a Venetian painter, Cima da Conegliano. Only two years later was it confirmed as Antonello.

[warmly] So this little panel waited a very long time to be recognised. Northern detail, Italian weight, and a Sicilian town on the horizon. It was always exactly what it is.
