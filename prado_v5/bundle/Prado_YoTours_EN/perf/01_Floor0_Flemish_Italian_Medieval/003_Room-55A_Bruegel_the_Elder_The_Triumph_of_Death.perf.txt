[quietly] Nobody gets out of this painting alive. Not the soldier, not the priest, not the lovers. That's the whole point.

Pieter Bruegel the Elder painted it around 1562. An army of skeletons sweeps across the earth. Behind them the land is bare and burning. In front, Death rides a rust-red horse and drives the living into an enormous coffin, with no way out. Every rank of society is here. Some people fight back. Others simply give in. Neither power nor piety saves anyone.

The idea is an old one. In the medieval dance of death, Death leads everyone away in one long line, the rich and the poor together. Bruegel joined that northern tradition to Italian paintings of the triumph of death. The crowds and the moral sting owe a lot to Bosch, next door.

[pause] Now listen with your eyes, because this picture is full of music. At the lower left, a skeleton riding a cart piled with skulls plays a hurdy-gurdy. Above it, two skulls haul on ropes to toll bells hung from a dead tree. Three skeletons blow trumpets to announce their mistress. It's a whole band, playing for a funeral.

Then look to the lower right. A young man plays the lute and sings to his companion. She sings from a little music book and beats time. Just behind them, a skeleton has picked up a bowed fiddle and joined in. They are the only two people in the picture who haven't noticed.

[wry] The painting passed through Italian and Spanish noble collections before a Spanish queen, Isabel Farnese, bought it in the eighteenth century. She hung it in her palace at La Granja. A queen chose this for her walls.

[quietly] And the lovers keep singing.
