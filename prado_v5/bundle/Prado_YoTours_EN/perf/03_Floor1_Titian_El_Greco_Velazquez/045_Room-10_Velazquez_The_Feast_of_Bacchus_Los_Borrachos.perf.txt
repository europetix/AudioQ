[amused] Hardly anyone in Spain calls this painting by its proper title. For generations it has simply been Los Borrachos, the drunks. Look at the faces on the right and you'll see why.

Velázquez painted it for the king, and he was paid for it in the summer of 1629. He was about thirty, five years into his work for Philip the Fourth. He had just met Rubens, who was visiting Madrid, and he was about to leave for Italy.

In the centre sits Bacchus, the god of wine, pale and half naked, lit like a statue. He is placing a crown on the head of a young man who kneels before him. Behind the god, a nude figure raises a fine crystal glass. These belong to the world of myth. [pause] Then the temperature changes. On the right, a beggar and four men in dark brown capes crowd in, with weathered faces and lively eyes. One of them grins straight out at you, bowl in hand.

[curious] For a long time this was read as a joke at the expense of the ancient gods. More recent scholars see something kinder. Wine cheers people up, and it can stir the imagination. So look closely at the crown. It isn't made of vine leaves. It's ivy, the plant of poets. The god may be crowning a poet.

Also in this room is the man who paid for all this. It's a small portrait of the young Philip the Fourth in armour, a crimson sash across his chest. A technical study suggests it was once part of a larger canvas. X-rays show how carefully Velázquez built up the king's face.

[wry] So here is the new court painter, polishing the royal face on one wall, and on the other, letting a few weathered drinkers steal the show from a god.

Before you go, there's a short Extra on Velázquez's early Adoration of the Magi in this room. Next door is Room 11. Look for a blacksmith's forge, where a young god crowned with laurel has just walked in with bad news. That's Vulcan's Forge.
