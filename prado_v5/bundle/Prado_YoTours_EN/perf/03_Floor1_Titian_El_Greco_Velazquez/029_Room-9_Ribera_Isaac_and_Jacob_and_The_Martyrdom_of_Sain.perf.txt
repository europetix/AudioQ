[curious] Jusepe de Ribera was born near Valencia and died in Naples, where people called him Lo Spagnoletto, the little Spaniard. He never let anyone forget it. On the big square martyrdom in this room, look low on the right. He signed himself Jusepe de Ribera, español.

Start with the long, low painting. It's a story from Genesis, and it's a trick. Old Isaac is blind and close to death, and he means to bless his elder son, Esau. But his wife Rebecca prefers the younger twin, Jacob. She has dressed Jacob in his brother's clothes and covered his arm with a skin, to feel like Esau's hairy arm.

[conspiratorial] Now look at Rebecca. With one hand she pushes Jacob towards his father, and she looks straight out at you. The museum calls us her silent accomplices. Far away on the left, through an opening, you can see Esau coming home from the hunt, with no idea what's happening. On the table at the right sits the dish of game Isaac asked for. For a blind man, everything here is about touch.

[pause] Now turn to the martyrdom, two years later. For centuries it was thought to show Saint Bartholomew. In 1953 an American scholar showed that it is Saint Philip, about to be raised on a cross.

Two men haul on a rope to lift the beam, while a third grips the saint's leg. Their effort is enormous, and Philip is resigned. Around them, onlookers watch, some with pity, some with indifference. We look up from below, into a vast, beautiful blue sky. [quietly] And nothing happens there. The heavens do not open, and there's no chorus of angels. For Ribera, this is something that happens on earth, among ordinary people.

It may well have been commissioned by the viceroy of Naples, probably as a gift for Philip the Fourth. Saint Philip was the patron saint of them both. By 1666 it hung in a main hall of the royal palace in Madrid, where the king received his audiences.
