[curious] When El Greco arrived in Spain, this was his first commission. Everything depended on it. And he painted it for a place where nobody would ever see it up close.

The canvas once crowned the main altarpiece of the convent church of Santo Domingo el Antiguo, in Toledo. It hung high above the altar, with a painting of the Holy Face set even higher. The museum points out that it had to work at a great height. That's partly why the figures are so massive, and why the perspective works the way it does.

[pause] Now look at the subject. We usually picture the Trinity in glory, enthroned in heaven. El Greco gives us grief instead. God the Father, old and bearded, sits on a throne of clouds and holds the dead body of his son. Above them hovers the white dove of the Holy Spirit. It's the Trinity, and it's a lament.

He borrowed the idea from a composition by Albrecht Dürer. And the body of Christ owes its heavy, heroic weight to Michelangelo. But the colour is El Greco's own Venetian lesson. [reverent] Christ is drained almost to grey, a cadaver among ashen clouds. Around him, the angels blaze in blue, carmine red, green, purple and yellow, under a golden sky. Life all around, and death at the centre.

Look at the Father's face, and at his hands. This is not a judge. It's a parent holding a child who has died.

In 1832 King Ferdinand the Seventh bought it for the royal collection.

[warmly] And here's the thread to follow. El Greco's first Spanish commission was for Santo Domingo el Antiguo. Some thirty-five years later, his very last painting was made for the same convent, to mark his family's grave. That's where we go now.
