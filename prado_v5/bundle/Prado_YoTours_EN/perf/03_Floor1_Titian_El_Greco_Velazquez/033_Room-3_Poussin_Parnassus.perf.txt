[curious] Every poet dreams of a welcome like this one. On the mountain of Parnassus, home of Apollo and the Muses, a poet is being received among the gods. Apollo offers him a drink of nectar, and a Muse sets a laurel wreath on his head.

Nicolas Poussin was a Norman who made his life in Rome, and he died there. He painted this around 1630, and he did it with a famous model in mind. The composition is based on Raphael's fresco of Parnassus, in the Vatican.

So who is the poet? Probably Homer, the first of them all. The woman crowning him is Calliope, the Muse of epic poetry. [pause] But there may be a second guest of honour, invisible this time. The painting may pay homage to the Italian poet Giovanni Battista Marino, who had been Poussin's patron. A thank-you, perhaps, in the language Marino loved best.

Now look at the bottom of the picture. The nude woman reclining in the foreground is Castalia, the sacred spring of the mountain. Little putti pass her water round to the poets. This was the water of inspiration, and whoever drank it could write.

[warmly] See how calmly it's all arranged. Figures sit and stand along the slope in a gentle rhythm, nobody shouting, nobody falling. The colours are clear and cool. After Titian's wild party back in Room 42, this is a different idea of pleasure, a pleasure of the mind.

Its first known owners were the Mattei family in Rome. By 1727 it belonged to Philip the Fifth, at his palace of La Granja.

[reverent] Nectar from Apollo, water from the spring. Poussin's point is simple: poetry is a gift from the gods, and then you still have to drink it.
