[wry] There's someone in this painting who isn't interested in the god at all. Look at the old woman on the right. While a god pours down from the sky, she's holding out her skirt to catch the gold.

In the myth, Danaë was a princess locked away from every man, until Jupiter reached her as a shower of golden rain. In Titian's first version, made in Rome for a cardinal, a little Cupid stood at her feet. When he painted her again for Philip the Second, he swapped the Cupid for this old servant. Youth beside old age.

The canvas in front of you is a later version, from the 1560s. [curious] And the museum thinks it was Velázquez who found it, on his first trip to Italy. By 1634 it had been bought, with seventeen other pictures, for the new Buen Retiro palace in Madrid.

Now step close. The brushstrokes are short and broken, and the paint is so thin in places that you can see the weave of the canvas through it. Even the little dog may be a hint at desire.

[lightly] Then turn to the two Venuses in this room. In each, the goddess reclines while a young organist plays and gazes at her. So play spot the difference. In one, there's a little dog, and she wears a wedding ring. That picture was probably made for a marriage. Its organist plays with one hand only. In the other, a Cupid takes the dog's place, and the organist plays with both hands.

The dog version has a royal history of its own. It belonged to King Charles the First of England, and after his death Philip the Fourth bought it.

[amused] In the nineteenth century this Danaë was kept in a reserved room, away from general view. Now she shares a room with everyone.
