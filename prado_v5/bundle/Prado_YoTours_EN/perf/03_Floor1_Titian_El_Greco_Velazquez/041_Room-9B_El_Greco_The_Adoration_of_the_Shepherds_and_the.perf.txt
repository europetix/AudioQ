[quietly] El Greco painted this for his own family's grave. It's thought to be the last picture he made before he died. And then, a few years after his death, the nuns of the convent changed their minds.

The canvas was to hang above the family burial place in Santo Domingo el Antiguo, in Toledo, the same convent where his Spanish career began. But the nuns demanded that the remains be removed, and in 1618 the agreement was cancelled. The painting stayed in the church for centuries, until the Spanish state bought it for the Prado in 1954.

[pause] Look at the light. It pours from the baby himself, and washes over the Virgin, Joseph and three shepherds, and up to the angels, who carry a banner: Gloria in excelsis Deo. Even an ox kneels to look.

Around you hang four canvases from an earlier altarpiece, painted around 1600 for a college church in Madrid. In The Annunciation, a burning bush stands for Mary's purity, and angels make music. In The Crucifixion, probably made for the top of that altarpiece, three angels catch the blood of Christ. In The Resurrection, the soldiers reel in confusion, all except one, who sleeps through it.

[amused] Now play a game of faces. In Pentecost, flames settle on the Virgin and the apostles. The bald, bearded apostle on the right, looking straight out at you, has been identified as El Greco himself, or perhaps his friend Antonio de Covarrubias.

Then find the bright Adoration of the Magi by Juan Bautista Maíno. It was painted in exactly the same years, also for Toledo, by a younger artist who became a Dominican friar while working on it. His colours are clear and bright. The young Black king, Balthasar, wears a red cap with feathers. And the young man on the left, pointing at the Child, may be Maíno's own self-portrait.

[warmly] Perhaps two painters, one old, one young, both looking out at you from the same room.

Next door, Room 9: Ribera. Look for a very long, low canvas of a blind old man in bed, and a huge square martyrdom under a blue sky.
