[curious] A queen is dying. And what she says in the next few minutes will help decide who rules Spain.

This is Isabella the Catholic, in the autumn of 1504, in her bedchamber at Medina del Campo. Propped up against her pillows, she's dictating her will. She'll be dead within weeks.

Now meet the people around her, one by one. At the bedside, the scribe Gaspar de Gricio writes down every word. On the left sits her husband, King Ferdinand, turned away from a little private altar. Beside him stands their daughter Joanna, the heir. At the far end of the bed, Cardinal Cisneros leads the courtiers. And deep in the shadows behind the canopy, if you look hard, you'll find the Marquis and Marchioness of Moya.

[quietly] Look at the queen herself. Pale, and very still. Her veil is fastened with a brooch, the shell and cross of Saint James. Then look at the faces again. Each person reacts in a slightly different way to her words, because her words carry the future of the crown.

The painter was Eduardo Rosales, a young man from Madrid working in Rome. He signed the canvas there in 1864. He was in his late twenties. The state bought it the following year.

What made it special was the way it's painted. No shouting, no theatre. Soft, dim light, a restrained palette, air you can almost feel between the figures. The museum calls it a masterpiece of nineteenth-century history painting, and credits it with turning Spanish painters back to Velázquez and his quiet, atmospheric realism.

[warmly] Rosales died young, before he was forty. But this one picture changed what his generation thought a history painting could be.
