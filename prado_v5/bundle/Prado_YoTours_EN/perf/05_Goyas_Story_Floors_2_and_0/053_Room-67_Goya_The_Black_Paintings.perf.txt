[quietly] Nobody was meant to see these. Goya painted them on the walls of his own house.

We're meeting the end of his story before its cause. A few rooms on, in Room 64, is the war that changed him. This is where he ended up. A grave illness had left him permanently deaf in 1793. He'd lived through the French invasion and six years of war. Then in 1819 he bought a country house outside Madrid, known as the Quinta del Sordo, the House of the Deaf Man. On its walls, in his seventies, he painted what we now call the Black Paintings.

He worked straight onto the plaster, with oil in the mix. Saturn hung on the ground floor, across from a portrait of a woman, Leocadia Zorrilla. The old god devours his own child. The museum suggests he may stand for a very human feeling: the fear of losing one's power.

Now find the Witches' Sabbath, the long dark frieze. A great he-goat on the left. Before him, a crowd of witches and warlocks with animal faces. At the far right, a young woman sits apart, perhaps waiting to be initiated. When the wall was cut away, more than a metre and forty centimetres of the scene was cut off.

The two men fighting with clubs now seem sunk in the ground up to their knees. [curious] But X-rays and old photographs show that originally they stood on a grassy meadow. And the Drowning Dog is only a head, peering over a great empty field of colour at something we can't see.

In 1873 Baron Émile d'Erlanger bought the house and had the murals transferred to canvas. They lost a great deal of paint. He gave them to the state, and they've been here ever since.

[reverent] Later artists saw the origins of modern art in these walls. An old man, alone, painting with absolute freedom.

Now walk back through Rooms 66 and 65 to Room 64. Look for two huge canvases of the same size: a street fight, and a firing squad at night.
