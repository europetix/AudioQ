[lightly] By 1786 Goya was no longer the beginner. That summer he was named Painter to the King. And for the prince's dining room at El Pardo he produced the largest cartoon of the whole set. This one, more than six metres wide.

The subject is summer. Tradition offered an easy answer: Ceres, goddess of the harvest, crowned with ears of wheat. Goya didn't want a goddess. He gave the season to the people who actually worked it.

Look at them. Harvesters have dropped down beside a heap of freshly cut sheaves, recovering from the heat. It's siesta time, and you can almost feel it. On the right, one man is still working. [amused] And on the left, a little group is trying to get one man drunk. The museum takes him for the village fool, from his clothes and the way he stands.

Now turn to Winter, from the same set, hanging in this room. Snow drives across the whole scene. Three peasants trudge home in their cloaks, cold and hungry, with nothing in their hands. Beside them come two better-dressed men, like stewards of some great house. One carries a shotgun. The other leads a mule loaded with an enormous gutted pig.

[quietly] And watch the peasants' dog. It's as thin and humble as its owners, and it's the first to sense the clash between the two groups.

That's the Goya to keep an eye on. Even in a design for a royal dining room, meant to look pretty over dinner, he slips in hunger and inequality. These cartoons sat in royal storerooms for most of a century, and came to the Prado in 1870.

Now take the stairs in the corner of this room, the ones you came up, and go down two floors, past Floor 1, to Floor 0. The lift by Room 86 goes there too. At the bottom, by the Murillo entrance, follow the sign to Room 71 and look for a marble of two young men side by side.
