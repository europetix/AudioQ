We know almost nothing about the woman who painted this. Not her teacher, not her exact birth, not even whether she kept painting in later life. What survives is the work, and a signature. It's on the front, low in the centre: Clara P., and the year 1611.

Clara Peeters probably worked in Antwerp, and she was a pioneer. She was one of the first painters of her time to specialise in still life, and one of very few women painting for a living. The Prado owns four of her pictures. When it finally gave a whole exhibition to a woman painter, the first in its history, it chose her.

Now look at what she's laid out. On the rim of a wicker basket perches a sparrowhawk, painted about life-size. It's a female, the larger of the two. Around her lies the catch. A mallard in the basket, a woodcock, a hen, two plucked young pigeons on a red clay plate. Tiny finches hang by their necks from a twig. Find the bullfinch on the right.

Then the luxury. A stack of white plates and bowls sits on a blue and white dish. This is kraak porcelain, the ware shipped from China. Its blue has faded with time. And there are four seashells, each one painted so exactly that experts can name the species.

Peeters had a private joke. In at least eight of her paintings she hid tiny reflections of herself, on the side of a goblet or the lid of a jug. There's no shiny goblet on this ledge, so you won't find her here. Her signature will have to do.

By 1666 this panel was very likely in the Spanish royal collection in Madrid. A woman's still life in the king's palace, three and a half centuries before the museum gave her a show of her own.
