At first glance, this looks like a carved wooden altarpiece, the kind with painted statues standing in a gilded box. It isn't. It's flat. Every figure is paint.

Rogier van der Weyden made it before 1443 for the Great Crossbowmen's guild of Leuven, for their chapel outside the city walls. Look up into the top corners, in the gold tracery. Two tiny crossbows hang there, the guild's signature.

Rogier squeezes about ten near life-size figures into that shallow box. There's no distance and no landscape, nowhere for your eye to escape. Joseph of Arimathea and Nicodemus take the weight of the body. A young man on the ladder helps from above. On the right, Mary Magdalene bends over, wringing her hands. Look at the faces. Almost everyone is crying, and the tears are painted one by one.

Now find the centre. Christ's pale body hangs in a long, slack curve. Directly below him, his mother has fainted, and her body falls in almost the same line. Her collapse mirrors his death. The museum points out that she is given the same prominence as her son. Their grief is one shape.

Down at the foot of the cross lie a skull and a bone. Tradition held that Adam was buried on this spot, so the death of Christ falls right where the first sin is laid to rest.

This blend of real detail and an unreal, golden setting struck painters of the time with enormous force. It left a deep mark on Flemish painting. Mary of Hungary acquired it for her palace chapel at Binche. It came to Spain, and Philip the Second later sent it to the Escorial, where it stayed for more than three and a half centuries.

Take a moment with it before you move on.
