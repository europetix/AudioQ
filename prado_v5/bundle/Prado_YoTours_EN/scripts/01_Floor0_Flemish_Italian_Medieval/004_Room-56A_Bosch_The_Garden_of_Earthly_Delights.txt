Philip the Second ruled a vast empire from a granite monastery-palace, the Escorial. He was famously serious and devout. And he could not get enough of Hieronymus Bosch.

He kept one Bosch in his private apartment, and he kept buying more. In 1591 he bought this one at a sale, and two years later he sent it to the Escorial. That passion is why this room exists. It's usually busy here, so step back a few paces, or come round to one side.

Bosch painted it in the 1490s, most likely for the Nassau family in Brussels. In 1568 the Duke of Alba seized it from William of Orange, the leader of the Dutch revolt.

Read it from left to right. On the left is paradise. God presents Eve to Adam. Bosch first painted the creation of Eve, then changed his mind. In the centre, the same landscape and the same high horizon carry on, so at first it looks like paradise continued. The museum calls it a false paradise, given over to lust. Hundreds of naked figures play among giant red and blue fruit. Riders on fantastic animals circle a pool of bathing women. And at the edges, two owls stare straight out at you.

On the right is the reckoning, often called the musical hell. Instruments have become instruments of torture. A bird-headed creature, perched on what looks like a child's potty chair, devours the greedy. And inside the hollow body of the tree-man, there's a tavern.

When the wings are closed, all that colour disappears. On the outside, in shades of grey, is the world on the third day of creation, before any of this began.

It's a pessimistic picture about how fragile and fleeting pleasure is. Five centuries on, the pleasure of looking at it hasn't faded at all.

Stay in Room 56A for two more by Bosch. Look for the Haywain, with its huge cart of hay, and a panel painted like a great round eye.
