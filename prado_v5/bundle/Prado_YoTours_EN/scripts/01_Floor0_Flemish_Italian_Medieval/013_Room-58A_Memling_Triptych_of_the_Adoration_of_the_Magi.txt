After the grief of the last room, this is a deep breath. Nobody faints here. Nobody weeps. Hans Memling gives you order, calm and jewel-bright colour.

He painted this triptych around 1470. And he had a model. The centre panel and the right wing follow an altarpiece by Rogier van der Weyden, the painter of the Descent from the Cross you've just seen. Memling took Rogier's design and smoothed away the drama.

Start in the centre. The Virgin sits with the Child in front of a great column, part of a ruined building with broken arches. The first king kneels and kisses the Child's foot. The second kneels on the left, in an ermine cape and gold brocade, holding out his gift in a metal vessel. The third stands on the right, an African king. The museum explains the tradition behind him. Each king stood for one of the three continents known at the time, so the whole world comes to the stable. Behind them, attendants carry banners, one with a crescent and a star from the East.

Now the left wing, the Nativity. Mary kneels before the naked Child, who lies on her cloak. And here's a lovely touch. Joseph comes in holding a candle, and he cups his hand around the flame to keep it from blowing out.

On the right wing, Mary hands her son to the old priest Simeon in the Temple. Look for the sword hilt at Simeon's robe. It recalls his prophecy that a sword would pierce Mary's soul.

The triptych belonged to the Spanish royal collection and hung for a time in a castle oratory near Toledo, then at Aranjuez. It was a picture to pray with, quietly, up close.
