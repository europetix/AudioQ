Around 1420, a young painter in Florence called Guido di Pietro became a Dominican friar. Today we know him as Fra Angelico. And this altarpiece, painted a few years later for the Dominican convent at Fiesole, is one of his first masterpieces.

This is where the Italian rooms begin. In Florence in these years, painters and architects like Masaccio and Brunelleschi were rethinking how to build space and light. They looked back to ancient Rome and out at the real world. That rebirth is what we call the Renaissance.

The subject is the Annunciation. Under a light, arched porch, the angel Gabriel bows towards Mary, and she folds her arms in answer. Look at the slender columns and the shallow room behind her. Angelico is using the new architecture to hold the scene together.

Now look to the left, into the garden. An angel is driving Adam and Eve out of paradise. That's the key to the whole picture. The museum explains that it pairs the loss of paradise with the salvation of humanity. The first sin happens on the left, and the remedy begins on the right.

For a long time, critics dismissed this panel as a late copy, largely by assistants. Today it is seen as the opposite, an early triumph. Even the frame was new. Its plain square shape follows a recommendation Brunelleschi made in 1425, and it is one of the first altarpieces to do so.

Along the bottom, five little scenes tell more of Mary's life. In the wedding scene, look for a trumpeter playing a long straight trumpet for the celebration. And in the garden, flowers and birds are painted one by one, with real care.

The altarpiece stayed in its convent for almost two centuries. Then it was bought and given to a powerful Spanish duke, and it reached the Prado in 1861.
