Before the war paintings and the Black Paintings, there was this Goya. Young, ambitious, and painting for the walls of royal palaces.

From 1775 he worked for the Royal Tapestry Factory in Madrid. His job was to paint cartoons. Not jokes, but full-size designs in oil, which the weavers copied thread by thread into wool. These rooms on Floor 2 bring his cartoons together.

This one went to the factory in the summer of 1777. It belonged to a set of ten country scenes for the dining room of the Prince and Princess of Asturias, at the palace of El Pardo. And you've already met that couple. They were the future Charles the Fourth and María Luisa, standing in their jewels in Room 32, more than twenty years later.

The documents show that Goya invented this composition himself. He may have started from a French painting of an old myth, the god Vertumnus courting Pomona. Goya kept the couple and dropped the gods. Instead you get a fashionable young woman and a majo, a young man in the popular dress of Madrid, gallantly keeping off the sun.

Now notice where you seem to be standing. Slightly below them. The tapestry was meant to hang above a window, so Goya painted it to be seen from underneath. The figures make a neat pyramid in the foreground, a lesson he'd learned from Italian painting. And see how the parasol's shade falls softly across her face.

If you have a moment, The Pottery Vendor hangs in Room 93, a few rooms along. Goya wrote proudly to a friend that the king and the royal couple had seen his new pictures, and this was almost certainly one of them.

Now go back past the stairs to Room 85 for the biggest cartoon of all: a harvest scene more than six metres wide.
