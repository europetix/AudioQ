During the war, Goya stayed in Madrid and swore an oath of loyalty to Joseph Bonaparte, the king Napoleon had given Spain. Six years later, with the French gone, he was painting the day Madrid rose up against them.

On the 2nd of May 1808, the people of Madrid fought French troops in the streets. In 1814 the Regency Council, which governed Spain while the king was away, commissioned two great canvases from Goya, this one and its partner here in this room. For a long time people believed they were made to be shown in the streets. The museum says that's wrong. The bills for the frames were charged to the king's own household. These were pictures for the palace.

Now let Goya throw you into the middle of it. The riders in turbans are Mamelukes, horsemen of Napoleon's Imperial Guard. A rider is dragged from his saddle. Among the French you'll also find a dragoon and a dead grenadier.

And look at the Spaniards. They wear the clothes of different regions, as if the whole country had come to fight in one Madrid street. There's no general and no hero on a white horse. Just people with knives against cavalry.

The canvas very nearly didn't survive. In 1938, a truck carrying this painting and its partner crashed at Benicarló, on the Mediterranean coast. The left side lost patches of paint, and the first repair simply filled them with red ink. A careful recent restoration brought back Goya's original brightness, filling the losses invisibly with the help of old photographs.

So stand back and take in the whole thing. A furious tangle of bodies, painted for a king's palace, now hanging where anyone can see it.
