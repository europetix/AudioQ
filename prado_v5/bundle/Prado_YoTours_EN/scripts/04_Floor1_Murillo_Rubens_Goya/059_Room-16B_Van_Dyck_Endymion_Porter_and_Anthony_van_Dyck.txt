Anthony van Dyck painted kings, queens and courtiers. But this is the only portrait we know in which he himself stands beside another person.

The other man is Endymion Porter, his friend and protector in London. Porter was secretary to the Duke of Buckingham, a diplomat at the English court, and the man who bought paintings for King Charles the First. Van Dyck, a Fleming from Antwerp, became Charles's court painter, and Porter's support may well have helped. Nobody knows why this picture was made. One idea is that it was a thank-you.

Here's a small coincidence. Van Dyck and Velázquez were born in the same year, 1599. Both grew up to paint kings. And both knew exactly where a painter stood in the order of a royal court.

So read this picture like a code of manners. Porter faces you squarely, in shining white satin. That frontal pose is the place of honour. Van Dyck, in black, turns in profile, and he's placed a little lower, so that he doesn't outrank a gentleman of the court. Every rule is respected.

And yet look at their hands. Both rest on the same rock, a symbol of how firm their friendship was. The oval around them draws them in, closer than protocol would normally allow. Look, too, at the light on that satin, and the ease of the two faces.

The painting later belonged to Queen Isabel Farnese, the same queen who bought Murillo's Holy Family next door.

Equal in friendship, careful in rank. Van Dyck manages both on a single canvas.
