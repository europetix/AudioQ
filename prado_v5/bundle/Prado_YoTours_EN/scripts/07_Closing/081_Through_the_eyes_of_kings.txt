This collection was never meant for the public. For three hundred years these pictures hung in palaces, chosen by kings and queens for their own walls. The collection grew fast under Charles the Fifth, and the Habsburgs and Bourbons after him kept it growing. The original building next door, by Juan de Villanueva, was designed for natural history. Then, in November 1819, King Ferdinand the Seventh, encouraged by his queen, opened it to the public as a royal museum. The first catalogue listed 311 paintings.

So for most of today, you've been looking through the eyes of kings. Titian's emperor riding out in armour. Velázquez's little princess, with the king and queen caught in the mirror behind her. A royal family lined up for Goya. Even the cheerful tapestry designs upstairs were made for a prince's dining room.

But you've also watched the painters start to look back. Velázquez put himself in the picture, brush in hand. Goya did the same, in the shadows behind his king. Then he painted a war from the side of the people who were shot. And at the end, in a house outside Madrid, he covered his own walls with paintings no king had asked for, made for himself alone.

By the nineteenth century, it wasn't a king ordering the great canvases. It was the state, buying pictures for the nation.

That's the real journey of this building. A private royal eye, slowly turned outward, until the collection belonged to everyone who walks in through these doors.
