Adonis is asleep. He has no idea that this is his last afternoon.

The story comes from Ovid. Venus, goddess of love, fell for the young hunter and begged him to keep away from wild boar. Their tusks, she warned him, strike like lightning. He didn't listen. His hounds would track down a boar, and the boar would kill him.

Veronese paints the calm before. Adonis lies across her lap in the shade, and Venus cools him with a little flag-shaped fan. The figures are life-size, close enough to touch.

But look at her face. Her eyes are wide and her lips slightly parted. She has just noticed Cupid, on the right, struggling to hold back one of the hounds. Cupid has dropped his bow and is gripping his quiver between his legs. The hunt is already calling. And she can't stop it.

Now the colour, Venice at its richest. Venus wears a purple-blue silk scattered with flowers in an Ottoman style. Adonis glows in pure orange, made partly with realgar, the only pure orange pigment of the day. It contains arsenic. Beautiful and poisonous at once, rather like the story.

The painting reached Spain thanks to Velázquez himself. He acquired it on his second journey to Italy, and it entered the royal collection. Its partner, a scene of Cephalus and Procris, is now in Strasbourg.

Before you go, there's a short Extra on Guido Reni's Hippomenes and Atalanta and its neighbours in this room. One more room along the gallery is Room 25. Look for a very long, low canvas of a tiled hall, with Christ kneeling at the right-hand end to wash a disciple's feet.
