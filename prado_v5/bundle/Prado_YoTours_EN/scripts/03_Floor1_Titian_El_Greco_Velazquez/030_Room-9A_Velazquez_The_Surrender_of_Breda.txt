In the summer of 1625, after a long siege, the Dutch city of Breda surrendered to the Spanish army. About ten years later, the king wanted the moment on his wall. What Velázquez gave him looks less like a victory than the beginning of a friendship.

This is your first Velázquez, so a word about the man. He was born in Seville in 1599, and in his twenties he came to Madrid to paint the young king, Philip the Fourth. He stayed at court for the rest of his life. The rooms ahead follow him all the way to Las Meninas.

The picture was made for the Hall of Realms, in the king's new Buen Retiro palace on the edge of Madrid. Its walls showed the victories of Philip's reign, painted by the best artists at court, so Velázquez was among rivals. His scene is the handover of the keys. The Dutch governor, Justin of Nassau, offers them to Ambrosio Spinola, the Genoese general who led the Spanish troops.

Now look at the two men in the centre. Nassau bends forward with the key. Spinola, the winner, does not snatch it. He lays a hand on his enemy's shoulder, almost like a friend. The museum says the gesture seems more like the announcement of peace than the end of a war. Writers of the day praised Spinola's generosity to a respected enemy, and Velázquez paints exactly that.

Then look at the lances. On the right, the Spanish pikes stand straight and close against the sky. That's why Spaniards call this painting Las Lanzas. Behind the generals, smoke drifts over a wide, pale land, the only sign of the destruction.

Finally, find the sheet of paper lying on the ground in the lower right corner. It's blank. Velázquez didn't need to write his name. The painting is the signature.

Next door is Room 10A. Look for a long, low still life by Zurbarán: four vessels in a row on a dark ledge.
