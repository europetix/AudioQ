Six large canvases were ordered in Naples for the king of Spain's new palace in Madrid. Four went to Massimo Stanzione, and one went to Paolo Finoglia, though his is now lost. The last went to a woman, and this is hers.

Artemisia Gentileschi was born in Rome and died in Naples. Around 1635 she was working in Naples for the Spanish viceroy. The set was to tell the life of John the Baptist, for the Buen Retiro palace. Her part was the very beginning.

In the Gospel, John's parents are old. Elizabeth has long been unable to have children, and the baby is a miracle. But look how Artemisia tells it. The miracle stays in the background, where Elizabeth rests in her bed and old Zechariah sits in front of her. The real action is in the foreground, among the women.

Several of them gather round the naked newborn. They tend him and lean in close, attentive and practical. There are only capable hands, and the ordinary business of a birth. It's a holy scene, told as a room full of women at work.

Now look right down at the lower left edge of the canvas. There's a signature, at the very bottom of the original painting. Artemitia Gintiles, it reads, and then the letters wear away. Below it, the canvas was later extended, and painted by a less skilful hand.

She signed at the edge of a picture for a king, in a set where every other painter was a man. And she made sure her name was on it.
