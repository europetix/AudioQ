This painting was designed to be seen from the side. Stand straight in front of it and it looks oddly lopsided, as if the main event had slid off to one end.

Tintoretto painted it in Venice around 1548, when he was about thirty, for the church of San Marcuola. It hung on the right-hand wall near the altar, so the congregation saw it at an angle. He put the most important figures at the end closest to them.

The scene is from the Gospel of John. Before the Last Supper, Jesus kneels to wash the feet of his disciples, a lesson in humility and service. Peter can hardly bear to let his master do it.

So try it. Walk to the right-hand end and look back along the canvas. The tiled floor rushes away from you, the disciples fall into a long diagonal, and everything leads to an arch beyond a canal, the true vanishing point. From there, the museum says, the painting becomes extraordinarily coherent. The setting is borrowed from a famous stage design, the Tragic Scene, by the architect Serlio. Tintoretto turns the Gospel into theatre.

Look at the disciple being helped off with his stockings, and at the dog in the middle of the floor. And on the right, in another room, the Last Supper is already under way.

Then the painting travelled. It ended up with King Charles the First of England. After his execution his collection was sold off, and in 1654 an agent bought this for the Spanish courtier Luis de Haro. Haro gave it to Philip the Fourth, who hung it in the sacristy of the Escorial. It stayed there until 1936, when it came to the Prado.

Walk back along the gallery, through Room 26 to Room 27, then turn into Room 12 and cross it into Room 14. Look for a pale Christ on the cross against a black background.
