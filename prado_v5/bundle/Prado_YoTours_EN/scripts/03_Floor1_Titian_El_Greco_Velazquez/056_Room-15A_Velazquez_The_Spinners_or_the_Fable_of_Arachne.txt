For a long time, people thought this was a factory scene. A royal tapestry workshop, with spinners in front and elegant visitors at the back. Then a clue turned up in an old list of belongings.

In 1664, an inventory of the possessions of a man named Pedro de Arce included "a Fable of Arachne by Velázquez". Critics had already begun to suspect a myth. Now they had the title.

The fable goes like this. Arachne was a mortal weaver, so proud of her skill that she challenged the goddess Pallas Athena to a contest. She wove the love affairs of the gods, among them Jupiter, disguised as a bull, carrying off a princess. That gave the goddess her excuse. She turned the girl into a spider.

Now look into the bright room at the back. The figure in a helmet, raising her arm, is Pallas, arguing with Arachne. Behind them hangs the fatal tapestry. It copies Titian's painting of that very story, which Rubens had also copied here in Madrid. The whole myth is hidden in plain sight, at the back of a working room.

Some read it as Velázquez's case for his own art. Raw wool in front, a finished work of art behind, craft turning into form. It was painted in the same years as Las Meninas, and it's just as layered.

Then the spinning wheel, on the left. The spokes have melted into a blur, and your eye reads it at once as movement. In the eighteenth century the canvas was enlarged with strips on every side, which made the back room seem further away. Today's display hides them.

Also in this room, Velázquez's Mars, the god of war, sits on an unmade bed wearing nothing of his armour but his helmet, the rest dumped at his feet, looking thoroughly glum. After Vulcan's Forge, you may have a guess why.

Go back into Room 15 and straight on into Room 16, Murillo's room. Look for 16 at the doorway, and a tall Virgin in white and blue rising on a crescent moon.
