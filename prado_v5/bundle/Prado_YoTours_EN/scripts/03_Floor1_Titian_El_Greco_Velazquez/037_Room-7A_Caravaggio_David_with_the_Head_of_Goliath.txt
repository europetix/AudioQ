For years, the Prado's own catalogue didn't call this a Caravaggio. In 1872 it was listed as school of Caravaggio, the work of a follower. It took a restoration in the 1940s, and the Italian scholar Roberto Longhi, before the experts agreed. Even then, the museum admits, some agreed reluctantly.

Look at what the painter actually shows you. This is not the hero's victory pose. David isn't holding the head up for a crowd. He's straddling the giant's body, bending down, and tying the severed head by the hair with a rope, ready to carry it off. The famous moment is over. This is the dirty work that comes after.

The boy comes out of a shadowy background, caught in the light, with everything else falling away into dark. The colours are earthy, mostly ochres. And the brushwork is firm and sure.

Then science got involved. When the head of Goliath was X-rayed, it showed that Caravaggio first painted the giant just after death, wild-eyed, his mouth wide open in a scream. He painted over it. The face you see now is far more restrained. More recent tests found another change. At first, David's right ankle was wrapped in cloths. Caravaggio dropped that too.

The canvas has had its own adventures. At one point in the eighteenth century it was enlarged to nearly two metres tall. And X-rays found a number, 449, painted in white in the lower left margin. It was an old royal inventory number, covered up before the picture came to the Prado.

So the painting that was once only school of Caravaggio turned out to be the real thing. And the scream is still under there, just out of sight.
