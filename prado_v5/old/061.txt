TRACK 061 · 55A                                      W-FOCUS                               205 WORDS · ~2 MIN

 Patinir, Charon Crossing the Styx
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Step across to the wide panel of a vast, bird's-eye landscape split by a river, with a tiny boatman ferrying a
    soul down the middle — paradise on one bank, hell on the other.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A sweeping panoramic landscape seen from above. A river runs down the centre; on it, a small boat carries
    the ferryman Charon and a tiny naked soul. To the left lies a luminous paradise with angels; to the right, a
    dark, smoking hell-mouth guarded by a three-headed beast. Distant mountains fade into blue.
    LISTEN FOR
    Find the tiny soul in the boat — then notice how small he is against the huge world. Patinir makes landscape
    the real hero.

 Joachim Patinir holds a special place in art history: he is often called the first true landscape painter —
 the first to make the natural world, rather than the human figures, the real subject of a picture. This
 panel shows exactly that revolution.

 The ostensible story mixes classical myth and Christian belief: the ferryman Charon rowing a soul
 across the river of the underworld, who must choose between the paths to heaven and hell. But look how
 Patinir handles it. The figures — Charon, the little soul, the guardian beings — are tiny, almost lost.
 What dominates is the landscape itself: a vast, panoramic world seen as if from a great height, a
 serpentine river dividing a radiant paradise on one side from a smoking, fiery hell on the other, with
 blue mountains dissolving into the far distance.

 This sweeping "world landscape," with its high viewpoint and its hazy blue distances, was Patinir's
 invention, and it would shape European landscape painting for centuries. The soul in the boat, poised
 between salvation and damnation, drifts at the centre — the human drama reduced to a speck within the
 immensity of creation. Now, a darker, more terrifying northern panorama — Bruegel's Triumph of
 Death, just along here.
