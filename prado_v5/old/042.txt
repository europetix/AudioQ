 Artemisia Gentileschi, The Birth of Saint John the
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Look at the warm interior scene of women attending to a newborn baby — bathing and swaddling him —
    while an older man writes at the side.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A domestic interior crowded with women. One holds the newborn John the Baptist; others prepare to bathe
    and dress him, carrying cloths and a basin. At the right, the aged father Zacharias sits writing the child's name
    on a tablet. The figures are solid, the colours warm and earthy.
    LISTEN FOR
    Notice this is women's work and women's world — birth handled by capable hands. That perspective was
    rare, and it is Artemisia's.

 Among the hundreds of male painters in this museum, here is one of the most remarkable women in the
 history of art. Artemisia Gentileschi was a major Baroque painter — trained by her father, shaped by
 Caravaggio's drama, and celebrated across Italy at a time when professional women artists were almost
 unheard of. The Prado names her among the great seventeenth-century figures, and this scene comes
 from a series painted for the Spanish royal palace of the Buen Retiro.

 The subject is the birth of John the Baptist, the cousin of Christ. But notice how Artemisia tells it — not
 as a grand sacred event but as a real birth, handled almost entirely by women. They cluster around the
 newborn with practical competence, bathing him, preparing cloths, passing the baby between sure
 hands. It is a scene of female labour and care, observed with a directness and weight that feels distinct
 from how her male contemporaries painted such moments. At the edge, the old father Zacharias, struck
 mute, writes the child's name.

 Artemisia's figures are sculptural and grounded, her light Caravaggesque, her women never merely
 decorative. To stand before her here is to be reminded how much of the story this collection tells from
 one point of view — and how powerful the exceptions are. Now, two French masters who turned
 landscape into poetry, further along.
