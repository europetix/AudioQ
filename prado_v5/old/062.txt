 Bruegel the Elder, The Triumph of Death
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Step up to the wide, brownish panorama swarming with skeletons — armies of the dead overrunning a
    scorched landscape full of fleeing, dying people.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A vast, apocalyptic landscape under a smoky sky. Armies of skeletons sweep across the land, slaughtering
    people of every rank — kings, lovers, soldiers, peasants. The dead drive the living into a giant trap; fires burn,
    ships sink, gallows and wheels dot the horizon. No one escapes.
    LISTEN FOR
    Look for the king at lower left, his gold spilling out — death takes him exactly like everyone else. That is the
    point.

 This is one of the most terrifying images in the museum, and one of the hardest to shake. Pieter Bruegel
 the Elder, the great Flemish painter of peasant life and proverb, here paints the ultimate leveller: the
 triumph of Death over all humanity.

 Across a scorched, burning landscape, vast armies of skeletons sweep forward, harvesting the living
 without mercy. And Bruegel's grim point is that Death spares no one, of any station. Look closely and
 you find them all: a king, his crown still on, dying as his spilled gold proves worthless; cardinals and
 soldiers; a pair of lovers who play music, oblivious, as a skeleton fiddles along behind them. Some flee,
 some fight, some pray — and none escape. The skeletons herd the living into an enormous trap marked
 with the cross, while in the background ships sink, gallows stand, and fires consume the world. It is a
 medieval idea — the "Dance of Death," in which Death claims everyone equally — painted with
 Bruegel's astonishing eye for crowded, panoramic detail. You could spend an hour finding individual
 dramas in the chaos. Painted in a century of plague, war, and religious slaughter, it is both a moral
 warning and a howl of dread. After all this darkness, we close the floor with light and confidence — the
 German master Dürer, just ahead.
