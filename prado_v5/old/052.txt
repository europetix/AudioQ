 Antonello da Messina, The Dead Christ supported
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Move to the small panel of the dead Christ, upright, supported from behind by a weeping child-angel, with a
    distant landscape beyond.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    The body of the dead Christ is held upright, slumping slightly, the wounds visible, the head fallen. Behind, a
    small angel with tear-streaked face strains to support him, cheek pressed close. A low landscape with a town
    and a few bones stretches behind. The mood is intimate and grief-stricken.
    LISTEN FOR
    Look at the little angel's face — Antonello gives a child the unbearable task of holding up the dead God, and
    the child weeps.

 Antonello da Messina was a crucial bridge in the history of art: a Sicilian painter who mastered the new
 Flemish oil technique and carried it into Italy, helping to transform Venetian painting. This small,
 deeply moving panel shows everything that made him singular.

 The subject is the dead Christ, his body taken from the cross, here held upright by a single small angel.
 And it is the angel that breaks your heart. Antonello gives the task of supporting the dead body of God
 not to grown mourners but to a child — a little angel who strains under the weight, his face crumpled
 and wet with tears, his cheek pressed against the lifeless head. The grief is unbearably tender and
 human.

 Antonello renders Christ's body with that fusion of Flemish precision and Italian monumentality that
 was his gift — the pallor of death, the modelling of flesh, the unflinching realism of the wounds. Behind
 stretches a calm, detailed landscape, scattered with a few bones — a reminder of the place of the skull,
 Golgotha. The whole panel is small enough to hold, intimate as a private devotion. Now, the supreme
 harmony of the High Renaissance — Raphael, right in front of you.
