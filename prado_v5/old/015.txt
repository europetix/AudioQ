 Ribera and the Spanish realists
 Velázquez was not alone. The same decades that produced him produced a whole generation of Spanish
 painters who looked hard at the real world and refused to prettify it — and the first of them is here.
 Jusepe de Ribera was born near Valencia but spent his whole career in Naples, then a Spanish territory,
 where the Italians nicknamed him Lo Spagnoletto, "the little Spaniard."

 In Naples he fell under the spell of Caravaggio, and you will see it instantly: the harsh, raking light that
 gouges figures out of deep shadow, the unidealised bodies, the sense that holiness and suffering happen
 to ordinary, ageing flesh. Ribera pushed it even further than Caravaggio — into wrinkled skin, dirty
 feet, the textures of old age and pain rendered with almost unsettling honesty.

 But do not mistake him for a mere sensationalist. Beneath the brutality runs real tenderness and a deep
 human sympathy, especially for the old and the poor. His saints are not marble ideals; they are
 weathered men you might pass in the street, lit as if by a single candle and granted sudden dignity. This
 dark, candle-lit realism — the tenebroso style — runs straight through Spanish painting, and it sets the
 tone for Zurbarán just beyond. Start with a hushed, strange family drama from the Book of Genesis, just
 here.
