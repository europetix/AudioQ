 The Treasure of the Dauphin
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Step into the darkened, glittering room of jewelled vessels in glass cases, and find the cup carved as a golden
    mermaid, set with gems and crystal.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A vaulted, dimly-lit chamber displays dozens of luxurious vessels — cups, ewers, and bowls carved from
    rock crystal, agate, jasper and lapis, mounted in gold and set with rubies, emeralds, and pearls. Among them,
    a spectacular cup is shaped as a golden mermaid, fantastical and gleaming under the lights.
    LISTEN FOR
    These were never meant to be used — they are pure display, treasures made to dazzle a court. Let them
    simply astonish you.

 We end the tour not with a painting but with a hoard of jewels — the Treasure of the Dauphin, one of
 the most spectacular collections of decorative art in Europe. These glittering vessels came to Spain
 through royal inheritance: they belonged to the Grand Dauphin of France, son of Louis the Fourteenth
 and father of Philip the Fifth, the first Bourbon king of Spain. When the treasure passed south, it entered
 the Spanish crown.

 They are objects of pure, fantastical luxury, made for the most powerful court in the world. Vessels of
 rock crystal, agate, lapis lazuli, and jasper are carved into impossible shapes and mounted in gold,
 encrusted with rubies, emeralds, and pearls. The showpiece cup shaped as a golden mermaid is typical
 of their fantasy and craftsmanship — pieces never intended for use, only for display, to overwhelm a
 visitor with the wealth and taste of a king.

 It is a fitting place to end, because it returns us to where we began: the Prado as the treasure-house of
 kings, filled with the most beautiful things the most powerful people in the world could gather. From
 Bosch to Velázquez to these jewelled cups, everything here was, first, a royal possession — and is now
 yours to enjoy. One last word before you go, just beyond.
