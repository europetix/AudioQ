 Bosch, The Haywain
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Cross to the three-panelled painting with a giant wagon piled high with hay at its centre, a crowd scrambling
    around it, beneath a small figure of Christ above.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A triptych. The centre shows an enormous hay wagon rolling forward, mobbed by people who fight, claw,
    and are crushed trying to grab handfuls of hay. Demons pull it toward a burning hell on the right wing.
    Above, Christ looks down from a cloud. The left wing shows the Fall of the angels and Adam and Eve.
    LISTEN FOR
    Watch the crowd grabbing at the hay and trampling each other for it — Bosch's hay is worthless, and that is
    the whole point.

 If the Garden is a riddle, The Haywain is a sermon — and a brilliantly savage one. It is built around an
 old Flemish proverb: "The world is a haystack, and each man grabs what he can." Hay, in other words,
 stands for worldly goods — money, power, pleasure — which are ultimately as worthless and fleeting
 as dry grass.

 In the central panel, a colossal wagon piled with hay trundles across the land, and humanity goes mad
 around it. People claw, fight, trample, and murder each other to snatch handfuls of the stuff — peasants
 and nobles, even a pope and an emperor ride along behind, all in the grip of the same greed. Demons,
 half-animal and grotesque, are hauling the whole wagon steadily toward the fires of hell on the right
 wing. Above it all, ignored, Christ looks down from heaven, arms spread, while the crowd below
 notices nothing but the hay.

 It is a ferocious moral cartoon: human greed marching itself straight into damnation, blind to the
 salvation hovering overhead. Bosch delivers it with the same teeming invention as the Garden, but here
 the meaning is unmistakable. Before we leave Bosch, two smaller panels turn that same moralist's eye
 on everyday human folly, right this way.
