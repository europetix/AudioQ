 Rembrandt, Judith at the Banquet of Holofernes
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Find the warm, shadowy portrait of a richly-dressed young woman receiving a cup, an old servant woman
    looming in the darkness just behind her.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A young woman in luxurious gold-trimmed clothing and pearls sits half-turned, reaching to take a goblet. She
    glances aside, thoughtful. Behind her, half-swallowed by shadow, an old serving woman leans in.
    Rembrandt's warm light glows on the fabrics and the young face; everything else dissolves into rich brown
    darkness.
    LISTEN FOR
    Look at the light on her skin and pearls against the dark — this glowing intimacy is the Rembrandt magic,
    even in one painting.

 This is the only painting by Rembrandt in the entire Prado, which makes it something of a pilgrimage
 piece — and it shows, in a single work, why he is among the most beloved painters who ever lived. The
 subject has been debated — the museum calls it Judith, the biblical heroine who saved her people by
 beheading the enemy general Holofernes, shown here at his banquet before the deadly act. A young
 woman in sumptuous dress and pearls reaches to take a cup, her face caught in a thoughtful, slightly
 apprehensive glance; behind her, an old servant looms out of the darkness, watching. It is an early
 Rembrandt, from his ambitious youth in Amsterdam, when he was making his name with exactly this
 kind of richly costumed, dramatically lit history piece.

 What you feel instantly is the Rembrandt warmth — that famous golden light that seems to come from
 inside the painting, glowing on the young woman's skin, her pearls, the gold of her gown, while
 everything around dissolves into deep, soft brown shadow. There is psychological inwardness here too:
 she is thinking, hesitating, alive behind the eyes. No other painter handled light and human feeling quite
 like this. Treasure it; it is the Prado's single Rembrandt. Now, a Flemish picture about looking itself —
 The Sense of Sight, waiting just over there.
