 Fra Angelico, The Annunciation
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Step up to the panel of the angel Gabriel kneeling before the Virgin Mary beneath a delicate pink portico,
    with a small garden scene at the left.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Under an airy portico of slender columns, the angel Gabriel, in rose and gold wings, bows toward the seated
    Virgin, who folds her arms in acceptance. Golden rays cross from a small God-figure above. At the far left,
    Adam and Eve are expelled from a flowering garden. Below runs a row of small scenes.
    LISTEN FOR
    Look left to the tiny Adam and Eve being driven out — their fall is the reason the angel kneels here at all.

 This is one of the loveliest paintings in the museum, and one of the most important early Renaissance
 works in Spain. Fra Angelico was a Dominican friar as well as a painter — a genuinely holy man, later
 beatified — and his art has a sweetness and serenity that feels lit from within.

 The subject is the Annunciation: the moment the angel Gabriel tells the Virgin Mary she will bear the
 son of God. Fra Angelico stages it under a graceful pink-and-grey portico, all slender Renaissance
 columns and clear light — the new architecture of the age, beautifully observed. The angel kneels in
 shimmering wings; Mary bows her head and crosses her arms in humble acceptance; golden rays carry
 the divine message across the space between them.

 But look to the left, into the garden. There, in miniature, Adam and Eve are being expelled from
 Paradise — and that is the whole point. The Fall of Man in the garden is the reason the Annunciation
 must happen: Mary's "yes" begins the redemption of Adam's sin. Fra Angelico ties the beginning and
 the remedy of human sorrow into a single panel. The colours are tender, the gold still present but now
 wedded to real space and light. Beneath, a predella of small scenes tells more of Mary's story. Now, a
 colder, sharper Italian genius — Mantegna, close by.
