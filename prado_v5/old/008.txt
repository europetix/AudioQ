 Velázquez, The Surrender of Breda (Las Lanzas)
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Step up to the wide battlefield canvas where two generals meet at the centre, one handing the other a large
    key, beneath a famous forest of tall upright lances.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Two armies on a smoking plain. At centre, the defeated Dutch commander offers a golden key to the
    victorious Spanish general, who lays a hand gently on his shoulder. Behind the Spanish ranks rises a dense
    thicket of vertical lances against the sky — the detail that gives the picture its nickname, Las Lanzas.
    LISTEN FOR
    Watch the hand on the shoulder. Velázquez paints the winner stopping the loser from kneeling — victory
    without humiliation.

 This is Velázquez as a painter of history, and it may be the most humane war picture ever made. The
 scene is the surrender of the Dutch city of Breda to Spanish forces in 1625 — a propaganda
 commission, painted for a hall built to glorify the king's triumphs. You would expect chest-beating.
 Instead, Velázquez gives you grace.

 The moment is the handover of the city's key. The Dutch general Justin of Nassau steps forward to
 surrender it; the Spanish commander Spinola, rather than seizing it like a conqueror, reaches out and
 lays a hand on his beaten opponent's shoulder — his face not triumphant but kind, almost consoling.
 The story has Spinola forbidding his men to humiliate the defeated, and Velázquez paints exactly that
 decency: the magnanimity of the strong toward the fallen.

 Now read the composition. On the right, the Spanish side bristles with those tall, ordered lances rising
 into the sky — disciplined, unshakeable. On the Dutch left, a few ragged pikes lean at broken angles, an
 army in disarray. Velázquez tells you who won and who lost with nothing but the geometry of spears.
 Behind, the flooded Dutch landscape melts into smoke and distance with a feeling for atmosphere that
 looks forward two hundred years.

 It is worth knowing Spain would lose the Netherlands anyway, which lends the painting's generosity a
 faint melancholy: made to celebrate a victory that would not last. What endures is the gesture — the
 hand on the shoulder. Hold it, and turn now to the same king — and the court around him — rendered
 larger than life on horseback, in the next room.
