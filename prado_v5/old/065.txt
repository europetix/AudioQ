 Goya II: war, dread, and the nation
 We met the first Goya upstairs — the brilliant, sly court painter. Now we meet the second, and he is a
 different man entirely. Between the royal portraits and these rooms, Goya's world fell apart. He went
 profoundly deaf after a terrible illness. And then, in 1808, Napoleon's armies invaded Spain, and the
 country dissolved into six years of savage guerrilla war and atrocity that Goya witnessed first-hand.
 Something broke open in him. The painter of silk and lace became the first great artist of modern horror
 — of war not as glory but as butchery, of human cruelty stripped of all comfort. He stopped flattering
 and started bearing witness.

 These rooms hold the result: the two enormous paintings he made to commemorate the uprising against
 the French, and — close by — the most disturbing works he ever produced, the Black Paintings he
 made in private at the end of his life, expecting no one to see them. This is Goya the prophet of the
 modern: unflinching, despairing, and centuries ahead of his time. Steel yourself. Begin with the painting
 that changed how the world pictures war forever — The 3rd of May 1808, right in front of you.
