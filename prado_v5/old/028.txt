TRACK 028 · CENTRAL                                 W-FOCUS                              214 WORDS · ~2 MIN

 Titian, Charles V at Mühlberg
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Come round to the large equestrian portrait of an armoured man on a dark horse, lance in hand, riding out
    alone against a wide evening sky.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A man in gleaming half-armour rides a powerful dark horse with red trappings, a long lance couched in his
    hand. He is alone — no army, no enemy — moving through a darkening landscape under a sky streaked with
    gold and grey. His expression is grave and inward rather than triumphant.
    LISTEN FOR
    Notice he is utterly alone, and not shouting victory. Titian invents a new image of power: solitary, sober,
    almost melancholy.

 This single painting more or less invented the modern image of the ruler on horseback, and its influence
 runs straight through Velázquez and Goya in the rooms you have just left. It commemorates the
 Emperor Charles the Fifth's victory at the Battle of Mühlberg in 1547 — but look how strangely
 subdued Titian makes a triumph.

 There is no battle. There are no troops, no fallen enemies, no banners of conquest. Charles rides out
 entirely alone across an empty, dusky plain, his lance lowered, his face thoughtful and tired rather than
 exultant. Titian gives us power as a burden as much as a glory — the loneliness of the man who carries
 an empire. The armour gleams with that miraculous Venetian handling of light on metal, and the whole
 scene is wrapped in a sky of smouldering gold and shadow that feels almost elegiac. Charles was the
 most powerful man in the Western world, ruler of an empire "on which the sun never set," and within a
 decade of this portrait he would abdicate and retire to a monastery. Titian seems to have sensed that
 weariness already. Remember this lonely rider; you will see his descendants all over this museum. Now,
 Titian in a wholly different key — pure pagan joy, over this way.
