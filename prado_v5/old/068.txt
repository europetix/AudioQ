 Goya, The Black Paintings (including Saturn)
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Enter the dim room hung with large, dark murals. Stop before the most violent: a wild-eyed giant devouring
    the bloody body of a smaller human figure.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A series of fourteen large, dark mural-paintings line the room. The most infamous shows a huge, crazed
    Saturn, eyes bulging, cramming the headless, bloodied body of one of his own children into his mouth. Others
    nearby show a terrifying witches' sabbath, a ghostly procession, and figures sunk in dread, all in blacks,
    browns, and bone-white.
    LISTEN FOR
    Remember — Goya painted these on his own walls, for no one. This is a private mind, with no audience to
    please.

 This room holds the most disturbing works in the museum, and among the strangest in the history of art.
 At the end of his life, old, deaf, and disillusioned, Goya bought a country house outside Madrid — the
 "Quinta del Sordo," the House of the Deaf Man — and covered its walls, in private, with fourteen
 nightmarish murals. He never titled them, never exhibited them, never explained them. They were
 painted for himself alone. We call them the Black Paintings.

 The most famous is Saturn Devouring His Son — the ancient god who, fearing a prophecy that his
 children would overthrow him, ate them one by one. Goya's Saturn is no dignified deity but a wild,
 hollow-eyed, almost insane creature, looming out of the dark to cram a small, bloodied, headless body
 into his mouth. It is an image of raw, primal horror — and perhaps of time devouring us all, or power
 destroying its own, or simple madness.

 Around the room hang the others: a satanic witches' sabbath, a grim pilgrimage of crazed faces, figures
 drowning in despair. There is no story, no comfort, no faith — only dread, painted in blacks and
 bone-white. After Goya's death the murals were carefully transferred from the walls to canvas and
 brought here. They feel less like nineteenth-century paintings than like something from our own age.
 Now we turn to the calmer painters who came after Goya — Spain's nineteenth century, up ahead.
