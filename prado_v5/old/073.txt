 Fortuny, The Painter's Children in the Japanese
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Stand before the small, brilliant painting of two young children playing on the floor of a sunlit room filled
    with Japanese screens, fabrics, and objects.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A luminous interior crammed with fashionable Japanese screens, textiles, and curios. On a richly patterned
    floor, the painter's two small children play — one sprawled among cushions, the other nearby. Sunlight pours
    across the colourful clutter; the brushwork is quick, glittering, and astonishingly free.
    LISTEN FOR
    Look how loosely it's painted — flecks and dashes of pure colour. Fortuny astonishes with sheer brushwork
    and light.

 After the solemn history pictures, here is pure painterly fireworks. Mariano Fortuny was the most
 brilliant Spanish virtuoso of the later nineteenth century — wildly admired and hugely successful across
 Europe for his glittering technique — and he died tragically young, at just thirty-six, with this very
 painting left unfinished.

 It shows an intimate, modern subject: his own two small children playing on the floor of a room
 decorated in the height of 1870s fashion — a "Japanese room," filled with the screens, fabrics, and
 curios that were all the rage as Europe fell in love with Japanese art. But the real subject is light and
 brushwork. Fortuny renders the whole glittering scene in quick, sparkling touches of bright colour,
 capturing sunlight glancing off silk and lacquer with breathtaking freedom. Get close and it dissolves
 into flecks and dashes; step back and it shimmers into life.

 This loose, light-filled, modern manner points straight toward Impressionism and toward the next
 generation of Spanish painters. That it was left unfinished only adds to its freshness — you can see the
 master's hand moving. Fortuny brought a new lightness and modernity to Spanish art. Now, that love of
 light reaches its glorious peak — Sorolla, round the corner ahead.
