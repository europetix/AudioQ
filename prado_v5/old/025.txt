 Goya, The Family of Charles IV
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Stand back from the large group portrait of a royal family arranged in a row, glittering with medals and silks,
    with the painter just visible at his easel in the shadows behind.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Thirteen members of the Spanish royal family stand in a line in a dim palace room, dressed in shimmering
    silk and heavy with jewels and sashes. The queen stands at the centre, the king beside her. Children and
    relatives fan out on either side. At the back left, in shadow, Goya himself paints at a large canvas.
    LISTEN FOR
    Find the queen at the dead centre, not the king — and find Goya in the shadows behind. He learned that trick
    from Las Meninas.

 This is one of the boldest portraits ever made, because of how little it flatters. Goya was First Court
 Painter; this was the official portrait of the reigning royal family; and he has painted them with an
 honesty that borders on the merciless.

 Look at the arrangement. The composition openly echoes Las Meninas upstairs — Goya even places
 himself, like Velázquez, at a great canvas in the shadows at the back, watching. But where Velázquez
 wove a magical puzzle, Goya gives you a frieze of stiff, blinking, over-dressed people. And crucially, at
 the dead centre — the place of power — stands not the king but Queen María Luisa, widely believed to
 be the real force in the marriage and the kingdom. Charles the Fourth, amiable and a little vacant, is
 shunted to her side.

 Then look at the faces, one by one. There is no idealising here. The features are heavy, the expressions
 glazed or anxious, the whole family rendered with every double chin and dull stare intact. The jewels
 and sashes glitter magnificently — Goya's brush is breathtaking on silk and gold lace — but the people
 inside the finery look ordinary, even unfortunate. One French critic later cruelly called them "the corner
 baker and his wife after winning the lottery."

 How did Goya get away with it? Probably because the sitters simply could not see themselves as we do,
 dazzled by the splendour of the surfaces. That gap — gorgeous paint, unsparing truth — is the essence
 of Goya. Now, for the painting that made him notorious, turn this way.
