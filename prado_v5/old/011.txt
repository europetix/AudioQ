TRACK 011 · 12                                     W-FOCUS                              294 WORDS · ~2 MIN

 Velázquez, Las Hilanderas (The Spinners)
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Cross to the painting of women working in a dim tapestry workshop in the foreground, with a brightly-lit
    room of elegant ladies beyond.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Two spaces in one. In front, a shadowy workshop: women spinning wool, one at a whirring wheel, balls of
    yarn on the floor. Beyond, up a step and bathed in light, finely-dressed women stand before a tapestry, and
    one figure wears a helmet. A cat dozes in the foreground.
    LISTEN FOR
    Look at the spinning wheel on the left — Velázquez paints its spokes as a blur, perhaps the first time motion
    itself was painted.

 For a long time people thought this was simply a charming scene of a royal tapestry factory — working
 women in front, well-dressed visitors admiring the goods behind. It is far stranger and grander.
 Velázquez has smuggled a Greek myth into a Madrid workshop.

 The story is the fable of Arachne, the mortal weaver so proud of her skill that she dared challenge the
 goddess Athena to a contest — and was destroyed for her arrogance. Look into the bright back room:
 that tapestry on the wall reproduces Titian's Rape of Europa — the image Arachne supposedly wove —
 and the woman in the helmet is Athena herself, caught punishing the upstart. So the humble spinners in
 front are not just factory hands; they are the goddess and the doomed girl, myth unfolding in a
 back-street workroom.

 Why hide it like this? Many read it as Velázquez musing on his own art — weaving and painting as
 rival crafts, the divine and the everyday tangled together, genius that risks punishment for its pride. It is
 the same mind that made Las Meninas, fascinated by layers of reality and the picture within the picture.
 And then that spinning wheel. Velázquez paints the spokes not as solid lines but as a transparent grey
 blur; your eye reads it instantly as a wheel turning too fast to see. It is one of the first times in Western
 art that a painter rendered movement itself rather than a frozen object — three centuries before the
 camera, the blur of speed.

 Step close to dissolve it into magic, step back to let it spin. Then meet the young Velázquez, fresh and
 bold, in the rowdiest picture in these rooms — The Triumph of Bacchus, right in front of you.
