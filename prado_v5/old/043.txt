 Poussin, Parnassus
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Come round to the painting of the god Apollo enthroned on a hillside among the Muses and poets, with a
    reclining river-god and a fountain below.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    On the slopes of Mount Parnassus, the laurel-crowned Apollo sits enthroned among the nine Muses and a
    gathering of poets. A winged Cupid offers laurel; a reclining nude personifies the sacred spring. Figures
    recline and converse on the grass in a balanced, frieze-like arrangement under a clear sky.
    LISTEN FOR
    Notice the calm, ordered arrangement — Poussin builds the scene like architecture, every figure placed with
    cool deliberation.

 After the heat of the Baroque, Nicolas Poussin offers cool, clear, classical order. He was a Frenchman
 who spent his life in Rome, the great intellectual of seventeenth-century painting, and where Rubens
 floods you with feeling, Poussin appeals to the mind — to balance, harmony, and thought. Parnassus
 shows the mountain sacred to poetry, where Apollo, god of the arts, presides over the nine Muses.
 Around him gather the great poets, being crowned with laurel; below reclines a figure personifying the
 spring whose waters grant inspiration. It is, in effect, a painting about art and inspiration itself — a
 hymn to poetry, painted by the most poetic of the classical painters. Look at how Poussin organises it.
 Every figure is placed with deliberate, almost architectural care, spread across the hillside in a
 measured, frieze-like rhythm. Nothing is accidental; the whole composition feels balanced and serene,
 the colours clear and harmonious. Poussin studied Raphael and ancient sculpture deeply, and you can
 feel that classical discipline in every pose. This is painting as intellect and order — the great
 counterweight to Baroque excess, and the fountainhead of the French classical tradition that would
 shape European art for two centuries. Now, Poussin's friend and the supreme poet of light and landscape
 — Claude Lorrain, on the wall ahead.
