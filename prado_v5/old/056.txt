 Philip II's Flemish obsession
 We come now to one of the strangest and most wonderful corners of the museum — and we have a
 single king to thank for it. Philip the Second, the most powerful and most devoutly austere monarch in
 Europe, had a private passion that sits oddly with his grim reputation: he was obsessed with the bizarre,
 teeming, often nightmarish paintings of Hieronymus Bosch.

 He hunted Bosch's works down, hung them in his monastery-palace of the Escorial, and meditated on
 them. Thanks to that royal fixation, the Prado holds the greatest collection of Bosch on earth —
 including the single most famous puzzle-painting in all of art. Around Bosch sit the other northern
 masters the Spanish kings gathered: the gentle Memling, the landscape pioneer Patinir, the savage
 Bruegel, and the German genius Dürer.

 These are the painters of the Low Countries and Germany — meticulous, inventive, unsettling,
 profoundly imaginative. Where Italy gave the Spanish kings beauty and harmony, the north gave them
 dreams, devils, and a microscopic eye for the real world. Brace yourself, and step before the most
 astonishing painting in the museum — Bosch's Garden of Earthly Delights, waiting just over there.
