 Tiepolo, The Immaculate Conception
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Stand before the tall, bright altarpiece of the Virgin Mary rising on a globe and crescent moon amid clouds,
    light, and fluttering cherubs.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    The Virgin, in white and blue, stands atop a globe wreathed in cloud, a crescent moon and a serpent beneath
    her feet, a crown of stars about her head. Cherubs bear lilies and a palm; a dove descends in radiant light. The
    whole composition soars upward in airy, luminous space.
    LISTEN FOR
    Compare this to Murillo's version upstairs — Tiepolo is brighter, airier, more theatrical. This is the Baroque's

 We end this floor with a burst of light from the last phase of the grand Italian tradition. Giambattista
 Tiepolo was the supreme decorator of eighteenth-century Europe, the master of vast, airy ceilings that
 seem to open onto heaven itself — and he spent his final years in Madrid, working for the Spanish
 crown, where he died.

 This altarpiece, painted for a church near Madrid, takes the same subject as Murillo's beloved version on
 the floor above: the Immaculate Conception, the Virgin conceived without sin. But the temperature is
 different. Where Murillo is soft, warm, and tender, Tiepolo is bright, cool, and theatrical — the Virgin
 rising on a cloud-wreathed globe, the moon and the conquered serpent beneath her, cherubs wheeling
 around her in a dazzle of light and pale colour. Everything soars; the eye is swept upward into luminous,
 weightless space.

 It is the Baroque's farewell — the last great flowering of that soaring, heaven-storming style before the
 cooler Neoclassical age took hold. Tiepolo brought the whole Venetian tradition full circle, here in
 Spain, at its very end.

 That completes the great main floor. Now we descend to the ground floor, and back in time — to the
 gold-ground altarpieces and the dawn of the Renaissance, just beyond.
