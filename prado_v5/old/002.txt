 El Greco, the Greek of Toledo
 Before we reach Velázquez, pause here, because this room holds the painter who opened the door. His
 name was Doménikos Theotokópoulos — a Greek, born on Crete, trained among the icon-painters of
 the Orthodox church, then sharpened in Venice in Titian's shadow and in Rome. When the work dried
 up in Italy, he gambled everything on Spain, hoping for royal commissions at the Escorial. The king did
 not warm to him. So he settled in Toledo instead, and there, far from court fashion, he became
 something no one had seen before — and earned the name that stuck: El Greco, "the Greek." Look
 around and you will feel why he unsettled people. His figures stretch upward like flames. His colours
 are acidic, otherworldly — cold greens, electric blues, a silvery grey that seems lit from within. His
 space refuses to behave. For centuries critics called him mad, or guessed his eyes were defective. We
 see it differently now: a man painting the spiritual world as though it were more real than the physical
 one, four hundred years before modern art caught up with him.

 He never did get his royal commissions, and the Spanish school that follows — Velázquez, Zurbarán,
 Ribera — owes him less in style than in permission: the proof that a painter working in Spain could be
 strange, intense, and utterly his own. Start with his stillest, most human work, a little further on.
