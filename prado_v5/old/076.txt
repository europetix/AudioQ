 Up to the top floor: the young Goya
 We climb now to the top floor — and to a delicious surprise. After the firing squads and the Black
 Paintings downstairs, the Goya up here is almost unrecognisable: young, cheerful, sun-filled, and
 charming. These rooms hold his tapestry cartoons — freshly rehung across Room 85 and Rooms 90 to
 94, with the eighteenth-century court painters between them — and they show where his extraordinary
 journey began.

 In his early years at court, before fame and before deafness, Goya's job was to design "cartoons" —
 full-size painted models that weavers at the royal tapestry factory would copy into wool to hang on
 palace walls. The subjects were light and pleasant, meant to delight the king: picnics, games, parasols,
 dances, ordinary Spaniards at play in the countryside. They are full of bright colour, fresh air, and good
 humour.

 It is worth coming up here precisely for the contrast. Standing among these sunny scenes, knowing what
 Goya would later become, you feel the whole arc of his life in a single building — from this Rococo
 brightness to the abyss of Saturn. Around him, this floor also holds a jewel-box of smaller Dutch and
 Flemish pictures and a literal royal treasure. Begin with one of his loveliest, warmest cartoons — The
 Threshing Ground, over this way.
