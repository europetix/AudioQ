 Welcome to the Prado
 Welcome to the Museo del Prado. Before you take a step, here's the idea that unlocks this building —
 keep it in your pocket and the rest makes sense.

 Most great museums were assembled to teach the public the story of art: school by school, century by
 century, the gaps dutifully filled. The Prado was never that. It began as something far more intimate —
 the private walls of the kings of Spain. For four centuries, Habsburg and Bourbon monarchs bought
 what they loved, hung it where they slept and prayed, and answered to no curator at all. What opened to
 the public in 1819, in a building Juan de Villanueva had built for a natural-history collection, was not a
 survey of anything. It was a royal eye, turned inside out for us to look through. Hold on to that, because
 it explains both the glories and the gaps. It is why the Prado owns more Velázquez than anywhere on
 earth, more Goya than you can hold in memory, one of the richest gatherings of Titian, and the wildest
 dreams Bosch ever set down — because Philip the Second and Fourth could not live without them. And
 it is why you will hunt in vain for much Impressionism, or for a tidy march through every movement.
 This is not the history of art; it is the appetite of a dynasty — magnificently lopsided.

 So, how we will walk it. The collection spreads over three floors, and the room numbers leap and double
 back; ignore them and follow me instead. We begin one floor up, in the molten core — the Spanish
 Golden Age, and the painter Spain measures every other against: Velázquez. From there we come down
 to the ground floor for medieval gold, the Flemish masters, Bosch's paradise and hell, and Goya at his
 darkest. Then we climb to the top for Goya's last whispers and a king's private treasure. You came in
 beneath one of three names — Velázquez, Goya, or the Jerónimos cloister. The Prado rewards the slow
 looker, so slow down. Head up, and let us go and meet Velázquez.
