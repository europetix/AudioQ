 Leone & Pompeo Leoni, Charles V and the Fury
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Turn to the life-size bronze statues of armoured monarchs — one figure of an emperor mastering a crouching
    figure, another of a standing king.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Gleaming bronze sculptures of the Habsburg rulers. One shows Charles the Fifth, heroically nude or
    armoured, dominating a cowering figure that personifies Fury or rebellion. Nearby stands a portrait of his
    son, the dark, sober King Philip the Second. The metal is worked to a high, living polish.
    LISTEN FOR
    Look at the emperor subduing the chained figure — power as the conquest of chaos itself, cast in eternal
    bronze.

 A pause from paint, for the Prado is not only a picture gallery. Among the royal treasures are these
 magnificent bronzes by the father-and-son sculptors Leone and Pompeo Leoni, the Habsburgs' favoured
 image-makers in metal — and they now stand here in the Central Gallery itself, newly placed beside the
 painted Habsburgs they were made to accompany.

 The masterpiece is Charles V and the Fury: the emperor stands in triumph over a writhing, chained
 figure that personifies Fury, war, and disorder. It is propaganda in bronze — the ruler as the force that
 masters chaos and brings peace — and ingeniously made, with removable armour so the emperor could
 be shown clothed or heroically nude. Beside it, the portrait of Philip the Second gives you the son:
 colder, more austere, the king who built the Escorial and ruled half the world from a desk. These
 bronzes show the other face of royal collecting — the same monarchs who gathered Titian wanted
 themselves immortalised in the noblest, most durable of materials. Now back to painting, and to the
 Flemish titan who rivalled Titian himself — Rubens, up ahead.
