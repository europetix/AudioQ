TRACK 013 · 10–11                                  W-FOCUS                              266 WORDS · ~2 MIN

 Velázquez, Apollo at the Forge of Vulcan
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Step across to the painting of a blacksmith's workshop where a glowing, laurel-crowned young god has just
    appeared among startled, muscular ironworkers.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Inside a forge. On the left, a radiant young Apollo, haloed and crowned with laurel, has just stepped in. The
    smiths freeze mid-work around the anvil — hammers paused, a half-forged piece of armour glowing orange.
    At the centre the older smith Vulcan turns toward the god, his face caught between confusion and dawning
    anger.
    LISTEN FOR
    Watch the faces ripple outward from the news — surprise, disbelief, fury — passing man to man like a
    current.

 Velázquez painted this on his first trip to Italy, around 1630, a young master testing himself against
 everything Rome and Venice had to teach. Beneath the mythology, it is a painting about a single
 devastating piece of gossip.

 The story: Apollo, god of the sun and of truth, has come to Vulcan's workshop to deliver bad news —
 Vulcan's wife, Venus, is betraying him with Mars. Watch what Velázquez does with that moment: he
 paints the news travelling. Apollo speaks; Vulcan, at the anvil, turns and we catch the exact instant
 comprehension and humiliation cross his face. Around them the other smiths react in a chain — one
 stares, one frowns, one hasn't quite understood yet — surprise rippling outward through the room like a
 stone dropped in water. It is superb storytelling: a whole emotional event caught in mid-air. This is also
 Velázquez studying the human body as never before. Those smiths are real, muscular, sweating
 workmen, their anatomy observed in firelight — the reward of looking hard at Italian art and at live
 models. The hot metal glows convincingly; the light divides between the cool radiance of the god and
 the warm orange of the forge.

 Notice how much looser and more silvery the paint has become since Los Borrachos, only a year or two
 earlier. Italy has begun to open his hand, and from here it is a short road to the miraculous touch of Las
 Meninas. Now, for something completely different in mood — Velázquez at his most stripped-down
 and devout — turn to the hushed, dark Christ Crucified, just through here.
