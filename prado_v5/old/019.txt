TRACK 019 · 10A                                      W-FOCUS                                235 WORDS · ~2 MIN

 Zurbarán, Agnus Dei
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Walk over to the small, dark painting of a single white lamb lying on a grey ledge, its legs bound, against a
    plain black background.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A young ram or lamb lies on its side on a grey stone slab, its four legs tied together with cord, against an
    empty black ground. Its fleece is painted with melting softness; its head rests gently. There is no halo, no
    inscription, no setting — only the bound animal in a pool of light.
    LISTEN FOR
    Notice there is no halo, no cross — nothing to tell you this is religious. The stillness and the binding do all the
    work.

 The title means "Lamb of God," and that is the whole painting: the ancient Christian symbol of Christ as
 the sacrificial lamb, "who takes away the sins of the world." But Zurbarán does something daring — he
 gives you no halo, no banner, no inscription, none of the usual signposts. He simply paints a real lamb,
 its legs bound, lying on a slab, waiting.

 And that plainness is what makes it devastating. Because Zurbarán painted the animal so truthfully —
 the soft, slightly dirty wool, the delicate hooves, the resigned tilt of the head — you respond to it first as
 a living creature, bound and helpless. Only then does the symbolism arrive: this is sacrifice itself, still
 and unprotesting. The painting works on you the way a parable does, through the thing itself rather than
 through explanation.

 Look at the fleece. Zurbarán renders it with a tenderness bordering on love — every curl catching the
 light against the surrounding dark. The lamb lies in the same kind of austere, isolating space as the pots
 next door; Zurbarán's whole art lives in that single beam of light falling on a humble subject against
 blackness.

 It is one of the most moving small pictures in the Prado, and its power is entirely in its restraint. Now we
 move from Zurbarán's hush to the warmth and sweetness of the last great Sevillian master — Murillo,
 coming up.
