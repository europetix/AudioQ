    WHERE YOU ARE
    Look for two smaller works near the great triptychs — a round-cornered panel of a surgeon operating on a
    seated man's head, and a flat tabletop painted with one enormous staring eye.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    In The Stone of Madness, a quack surgeon in a funnel hat cuts into the scalp of a foolish-looking patient to
    remove the "stone" of his stupidity, while a monk and a nun look on. The Table of the Seven Deadly Sins is
    painted as a great eye of God, with seven scenes of sin ringed around the iris.
    LISTEN FOR
    Find the inscription at the centre of the eye — a warning that God sees everything, with the sins acted out
    around the pupil.

 These two smaller panels show Bosch the moralist working close to home — not the cosmic visions of
 the triptychs, but sharp, funny, biting little sermons on human folly.

 The Stone of Madness mocks the gullible and the frauds who prey on them in equal measure. A
 swindling "doctor," wearing an upturned funnel for a hat — the old shorthand for a fool — pretends to
 cure stupidity by surgically removing a stone from the patient's head. It is a visual pun: to "have a stone
 in the head" was to be an idiot, and the quack is plainly no wiser than his victim. Bosch stages the whole
 farce in a wide, calm summer landscape, which only sharpens the cruelty of the joke. The Table of the
 Seven Deadly Sins is cleverer still. Painted flat, to lie on a tabletop, it is built as an enormous eye — the
 eye of God — with the risen Christ at the pupil and the warning, "Beware, beware, God sees." Around
 the iris run seven vivid little scenes — wrath, pride, lust, sloth, gluttony, greed, envy — each a glimpse
 of ordinary people behaving badly. King Philip the Second loved it, and kept it in his private rooms at
 the Escorial. Now, a gentler northern voice — the serene Memling, a few steps on.
