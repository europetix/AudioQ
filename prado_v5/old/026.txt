 Goya, The Naked Maja and The Clothed Maja
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Look at the two paintings hung together: the same young woman reclining on the same couch, in the same
    pose — naked in one, fully clothed in the other.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Two canvases of identical size, side by side. In both, a young woman reclines on a green velvet couch piled
    with white pillows, arms raised behind her head, looking directly out. In one she is entirely nude; in the other
    she wears a fitted white dress with a rose sash and little slippers. Same pose, same gaze.
    LISTEN FOR
    Look at her eyes in both — she meets your gaze head-on, unashamed. That directness was the real scandal,
    more than the nudity.

 Here is the painting that nearly destroyed Goya, hung — as it often is — beside its twin. Together they
 are among the most famous and most mysterious works in Spanish art.

 The Naked Maja was revolutionary and dangerous. Spain, under the eye of the Inquisition, had almost
 no tradition of the female nude; the few that existed were safely mythological — Venuses and
 goddesses. Goya's maja is no goddess. She is a real, contemporary Spanish woman, reclining on a
 everyday couch, and — most shocking of all — she looks straight out at the viewer with calm, frank
 self-possession. It is one of the first unapologetically secular, full-frontal female nudes in Western art,
 and the Inquisition did indeed summon Goya to explain himself.

 Then there is the puzzle of the pair. The two paintings show the identical woman in the identical pose,
 one nude and one dressed. We still do not know exactly why, or who she is. The likeliest story is that
 both were painted for Manuel Godoy, the powerful prime minister and royal favourite, perhaps to hang
 one over the other with a hidden mechanism, the clothed version concealing the nude. Her identity has
 been argued over for two centuries — the Duchess of Alba? Godoy's mistress Pepita Tudó? — without
 resolution.

 Compare them closely. The clothed Maja, oddly, can feel the more charged of the two: the dress clings,
 the pose is the same, and that level gaze never wavers. Goya is toying with the whole game of looking
 and concealment. With that, we have reached the end of Floor One's Spanish heart. Follow me now into
 the Italian and Flemish masters who taught these Spaniards how to paint, a little further on.
