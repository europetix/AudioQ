 Goya, The 3rd of May 1808
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Look at the large night scene of a firing squad: a line of soldiers aiming their rifles at a group of terrified
    civilians, one in a white shirt flinging his arms wide.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A dark hillside at night, lit by a single great lantern on the ground. On the right, a faceless line of French
    soldiers level their rifles. On the left, a crowd of Spanish civilians awaits death; at the centre, a man in a white
    shirt throws his arms up in a blazing pose of terror and defiance. Bodies lie bleeding below.
    LISTEN FOR
    Look at the soldiers — Goya hides their faces. They are a machine. Then the white-shirted man: arms wide,
    like a crucifixion.

 This is one of the most important paintings ever made, the work that taught the modern world how to
 picture the horror of war. Goya painted it in 1814, to commemorate the night of the 3rd of May 1808,
 when French troops executed hundreds of Madrid citizens in reprisal for the previous day's uprising.
 Everything about it was revolutionary. Goya gives us no glory, no heroism in the old sense — only
 terror and slaughter. The French firing squad is a faceless, anonymous machine, a row of identical backs
 and rifles, mechanical and merciless. Facing them, the victims are all individual, all human, all afraid.
 And at the centre blazes the white-shirted man, lit by the harsh lantern on the ground, flinging his arms
 wide in a final gesture of terror and defiance — a pose that unmistakably echoes the crucifixion, even to
 a mark on his raised hand. He is every innocent victim of every massacre.

 There is no idealising here, no consolation. Just a lantern, a wall, a firing squad, and the unbearable
 instant before death. Goya invented the visual language of modern atrocity — and Picasso, painting
 Guernica a century later, looked straight back at this canvas. Now, the painting Goya made as its
 companion, the uprising that caused it — The 2nd of May, just through here.
