 Claude Lorrain, The Embarkation of Saint Paula
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Walk over to the luminous harbour scene: grand classical buildings framing a glowing sky and water, with
    tiny figures gathered on the quay below.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A serene seaport at golden hour. Tall classical palaces and a lighthouse frame the harbour on either side; ships
    ride at anchor on shimmering water. The sun hangs low and radiant over the sea, flooding everything with
    gold. Small figures — Saint Paula embarking on a voyage — cluster on the quay.
    LISTEN FOR
    Look straight into the sun on the water — Claude paints light itself as the true subject; the saint is almost an
    excuse.

 Claude Lorrain — known in Spain simply as "Lorena" — was, with his friend Poussin, the other great
 French master working in Rome, and he very nearly invented the idealised landscape. People came to
 call a certain kind of golden, harmonious view "Claudian" after him; he shaped how Europe saw nature
 for two hundred years.

 The ostensible subject is a holy story — Saint Paula, a noble Roman widow, setting sail for the Holy
 Land. But look how small the saint and her companions are, tucked among the figures on the quay. The
 real subject is the harbour itself, and above all the light: Claude has placed the low sun directly over the
 sea so that its radiance floods the whole canvas, glittering on the water, glowing through the hazy air,
 gilding the grand classical buildings that frame the scene like a stage set.

 This is light as poetry, calm and golden and infinitely peaceful. Claude built these idealised ports out of
 his imagination, perfectly balanced, bathed in that particular honeyed glow of early morning or late
 afternoon. Turner worshipped him; generations of travellers learned to see landscape through his eyes.
 Now, one last Italian flourish before we descend — the airy ceiling-master Tiepolo, waiting just over
 there.
