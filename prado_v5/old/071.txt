 Rosales, Queen Isabella the Catholic Dictating her
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Come round to the large, softly-lit scene of a dying queen propped in a great bed, dictating to a scribe while
    courtiers and clergy stand gravely around her.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    In a dim, richly-furnished chamber, the dying Queen Isabella lies propped against pillows in a canopied bed,
    lifting one hand as she dictates her last will. A secretary writes at a table; courtiers, a cardinal, and the king
    stand in solemn attendance. Warm light falls across the bed; the mood is hushed and grave.
    LISTEN FOR
    Notice the restraint — no melodrama, just sober dignity. Rosales paints a great death as a still, solemn human
    moment.

 This is one of the finest Spanish history paintings of the nineteenth century, and a good example of how
 the age tried to dramatise its own past. Eduardo Rosales chose a founding moment of the Spanish
 nation: the deathbed of Queen Isabella the First — "Isabella the Catholic" — who, with her husband
 Ferdinand, united Spain, completed the reconquest of Granada, and sponsored Columbus's voyage.
 Rosales shows her final act: dictating her last will and testament from her deathbed, surrounded by the
 court. But what is striking is his restraint. He could have made it bombastic; instead the mood is hushed
 and dignified — a great queen meeting death with composure, the room silent with respect. The dying
 woman lifts her hand to speak; the secretary records her words; the courtiers stand in grave attendance.
 Look at the handling of light and paint: warm, soft, and free, owing something to Velázquez and to the
 Venetians, far looser and more atmospheric than the hard finish of academic history painting. Rosales
 was admired precisely for this painterly quality and his refusal of cheap theatrics. The result feels less
 like a staged tableau than like a solemn, real moment of national history. Now, history painting in a far
 more savage key — Gisbert's execution scene, in the gallery ahead.
