 Ribera, Isaac and Jacob
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Come round to the dim, horizontal painting of an old blind man propped in bed, reaching to bless a young
    man who leans in, with a woman watching from the shadows.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A darkened room. The aged, blind patriarch Isaac lies in bed, one hand outstretched onto the head of the
    kneeling young Jacob. Jacob, dressed in rough skins, leans in to receive the blessing. At the right edge, half in
    shadow, Rebecca watches the deception she has arranged. A heavy curtain hangs above.
    LISTEN FOR
    Watch the blind man's hand and face — Ribera makes you feel a man reading the world through touch alone.

 This is one of the great con-tricks of the Bible, and Ribera tells it with breathtaking restraint. The old
 patriarch Isaac, blind with age, means to give his deathbed blessing — and with it the birthright — to his
 elder son, Esau. But his wife Rebecca favours the younger twin, Jacob, and has dressed him in goatskins
 so that his smooth arms will feel like his hairy brother's. The blind old man, fooled by touch, blesses the
 wrong son. The whole future of Israel turns on this small domestic lie.

 Ribera stages it almost entirely through hands and faces. Isaac's outstretched hand is the heart of the
 picture — the hand of a man who can no longer see, trusting his fingers and being betrayed by them. His
 upturned, sightless face is rendered with that famous Riberan honesty: every wrinkle, the slack skin, the
 vulnerability of great age. Jacob's expression is harder to read — is that guilt, or only concentration?
 And in the shadows, Rebecca, the silent architect of it all, watches her plan succeed. Notice the light: it
 falls from the left in a single warm beam, pulling the three faces and the crucial hands out of a brown
 gloom and leaving everything else to darkness. That is Caravaggio's lesson, learned and made tender.
 There is no melodrama here, only a hushed, almost unbearable intimacy.

 Now turn to Ribera at his most physically extreme — a martyrdom that is also, strangely, full of
 compassion, waiting just over there.
