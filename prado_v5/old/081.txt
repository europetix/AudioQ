 Jan Brueghel & Rubens, The Sense of Sight
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Step across to the painting of a grand gallery crammed floor-to-ceiling with pictures, sculptures, instruments
    and treasures, where a woman and a cupid examine a painting.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    An opulent collector's gallery overflowing with art: paintings stacked to the ceiling, sculptures, globes,
    scientific instruments, jewels, and curiosities. At the centre, the figure of Sight — a woman — examines a
    painting held up by a small Cupid. Light streams through a window onto the glittering profusion of objects.
    LISTEN FOR
    Try to count the paintings-within-the-painting — it's a gallery inside a gallery, a celebration of looking and
    collecting itself.

 This glittering little painting is a collaboration between two Flemish masters — Jan Brueghel the Elder,
 who painted the exquisite details, and Peter Paul Rubens, who added the figures — and it is part of a
 famous series on the Five Senses. This one represents Sight, and it is, fittingly, a feast for the eyes. The
 conceit is brilliant: to show the sense of sight, the artists paint an enormous collector's gallery
 overflowing with every kind of beautiful, visible thing. Paintings hang frame-to-frame up to the ceiling;
 sculptures, globes, telescopes, jewels, coins, and curiosities crowd every surface. At the centre, a female
 figure personifying Sight, attended by a little Cupid, contemplates a picture. It is a painting about
 looking — and about the joy of collecting, of surrounding oneself with visual treasures. It is also a kind
 of game, full of paintings-within-the-painting (several of them real works from the same archducal
 collection), instruments of optics, and references to vision and art. Brueghel's microscopic precision
 makes every tiny object gleam. There could hardly be a more fitting picture to find in a great museum:
 an image of the sheer pleasure of seeing, made for people who, like the Spanish kings, loved to gather
 beauty around them. Now, a rare master who was a woman — Clara Peeters, this way now.
