 El Greco, The Nobleman with his Hand on his
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Stand before the small, dark portrait of a man in black, one hand laid flat on his chest. He is roughly life-size
    from the waist up.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A Spanish gentleman, perhaps mid-thirties, against a plain black ground. A black doublet; a wide ruff of
    white lace at the throat; a gold-hilted sword just visible at his side. His right hand rests on his heart, fingers
    slightly splayed, a thin gold ring on one. He looks directly out at you.
    LISTEN FOR
    Watch the hand — the gesture is the whole painting. Then hold his gaze; he has been holding yours for four
    hundred years.

 This is the most famous portrait in Spanish art before Velázquez, and almost nothing about the sitter is
 certain. Tradition named him a nobleman of Toledo, but in truth we do not know who he was. What we
 have instead is rarer than a name: a presence. El Greco has stripped the picture to essentials — black
 clothes, black ground, a face, a ruff, a hand — and somehow that bareness makes the man more alive,
 not less.

 Everything happens in two places. First the face: lean, grave, intelligent, the dark eyes meeting yours
 with a directness that feels almost private, as if he is discreetly taking your measure. Second the hand —
 and the hand is why this picture stays with you. Fingers spread on the heart, it has been read a hundred
 ways: an oath, a vow of loyalty, the simple sign for "I give you my word." El Greco paints it so tenderly
 — pale skin, tapering fingers, the white cuff against the black — that it becomes the emotional centre of
 the work.

 And notice what he is already doing with paint: that ruff is not drawn line by line but conjured in quick
 strokes of white that only resolve into lace when you step back — the loose, suggestive touch Velázquez
 will carry to its summit, two rooms from here. El Greco is the threshold. Hold the gentleman's gaze a
 moment longer, then let us go and meet the master he made possible.
