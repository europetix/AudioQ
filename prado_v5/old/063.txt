 Dürer, Self-portrait
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Walk over to the portrait of an elegant young man with long curling hair, in fine striped clothing and gloves,
    posed at a window with a mountain view behind.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A confident young man sits at a sill, turned three-quarters toward us. He has long, carefully curled reddish
    hair, a neat beard, and wears refined striped clothing, a tasselled cap, and pale gloves. Through a window
    behind him, a distant alpine landscape appears. An inscription records his name and age.
    LISTEN FOR
    Notice he looks like a nobleman, not a tradesman — Dürer is making a bold claim about the dignity of the
    artist.

 We end the floor with one of the proudest, most self-aware images in the museum. Albrecht Dürer was
 the greatest German artist of the Renaissance, and this self-portrait, painted when he was just
 twenty-six, is a manifesto in paint.

 Look at how he presents himself. This is no humble craftsman in a workshop apron. Dürer shows
 himself as a refined, fashionable gentleman — elegant striped clothing, fine gloves, beautifully curled
 hair, the air of a nobleman or scholar. Through the window behind him stretches an alpine landscape,
 recalling his formative journey across the Alps to Italy, where he had absorbed the new Renaissance
 idea that the artist was not a mere artisan but a thinker, a creator, a person of standing. That is the
 painting's real argument. In northern Europe, painters were still largely seen as tradesmen; Dürer, with
 this image, stakes a claim for the dignity and intellectual status of the artist — the same battle Velázquez
 would fight a century later for his cross of Santiago. The technique is dazzlingly precise: every curl of
 hair, every thread of the costume, rendered with that famous German exactitude. An inscription on the
 wall proudly records that he painted it himself, from life, at twenty-six. Now, Dürer's vision of humanity
 at its origin — Adam and Eve, over this way.
