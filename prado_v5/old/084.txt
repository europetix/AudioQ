 Through the Eyes of Kings
 And there it is — six centuries, three floors, one royal obsession. Before you step back into the Madrid
 light, let us gather up what you have just walked through, because it was never random; it has a shape.
 You began with Velázquez, the watchful genius who turned court painting into something close to
 sorcery — and you leave having met the whole family he belongs to. El Greco, who made strangeness
 holy. Goya, who dragged painting out of the palace and into the nightmare of history. Titian and
 Rubens, who taught Spanish kings how colour could rule a room. Bosch, whose hell still grins at us five
 hundred years on. They were not gathered to illustrate a textbook. They were gathered because a
 handful of monarchs could not bear to be without them.

 Notice what the Prado refused to give you: no obligation to admire everything, no dutiful parade of
 -isms, no breadth for its own sake. What it gave you instead was depth — the chance to stand before Las
 Meninas, then Las Hilanderas, then the small, unbearable Christ Crucified, and watch a single mind turn
 one problem over and over until it cracked open. That is the gift of a collection built by passion rather
 than committee: it goes deep wherever it goes at all.

 And if the Prado has lit something in you, you are standing in the luckiest few hundred metres in
 Europe. Straight across the boulevard, the Thyssen-Bornemisza picks up almost exactly where the
 Prado stops — the Impressionists, the moderns, the centuries the kings ignored. A little further, the
 Reina Sofía guards Picasso's Guernica — the direct descendant of that Goya firing squad you stood in
 front of upstairs. Together the three are called the Golden Triangle, and you can walk it in an afternoon.
 Or simply slip into the Retiro gardens behind the museum, find a bench under the trees, and let it all
 settle.

 However you leave, you carry something no postcard holds: for an hour or two, you looked — really
 looked — through the eyes of kings. Travel well.
