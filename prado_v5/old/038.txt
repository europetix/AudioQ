 Rubens, The Garden of Love
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Cross to the painting of elegant couples in silks gathered in a lush garden before a grand fountain, with plump
    cupids fluttering and nudging among them.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    In a sumptuous garden beside a grotto and a fountain of the goddess Venus, several richly-dressed couples sit,
    flirt, and converse. Chubby winged cupids hover and gently push the figures together. The women's satin
    gowns shimmer; the whole scene glows with warmth, leisure, and amorous ease.
    LISTEN FOR
    Watch the little cupids nudging the couples — Rubens makes love itself the matchmaker, gently herding
    everyone together.

 Here is Rubens painting the good life — and, slyly, his own happiness. Painted in his later years, after
 his marriage to the young Helena Fourment, The Garden of Love is a vision of refined, joyful courtship:
 elegant couples gathered in a luxuriant garden, talking, flirting, dressed in gleaming silks. It is not quite
 a mythology and not quite a real scene, but something dreamier — an ideal of love and sociability. A
 fountain crowned by Venus, goddess of love, presides over the gathering, and everywhere little winged
 cupids flutter, nudging the couples closer, scattering their gentle encouragement. The mood is warm,
 sensuous, and wholly content. Several of the women are thought to carry the features of Helena, so this
 is in part Rubens's hymn to his own late-found domestic joy.

 Look at the satins — the shimmering, rustling fabrics are some of the most gorgeous passages of cloth
 in painting. The whole canvas glows with that Rubensian sense of abundance and well-being, of life
 lived generously. It influenced a century of French painters who turned it into the elegant "fête galante."
 Before we leave Rubens, one last picture — one of his most beautiful and most talked-about
 mythologies, just along here.
