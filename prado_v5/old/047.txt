 This is a masterpiece of the Spanish Middle Ages, and one of the most sheerly spectacular objects in the
 museum. Bartolomé Bermejo was the finest of the Hispano-Flemish painters, and here he pulls out
 every stop to overwhelm you with sacred magnificence.

 The subject is Saint Dominic of Silos, an eleventh-century Spanish abbot, shown enthroned as a bishop
 in glory. But the real subject is splendour itself. Bermejo seats the saint on a towering gold throne
 encrusted with jewels and crowded with tiny sculpted figures of the Virtues, set into delicate Gothic
 niches. The cope is a sea of gold brocade; the mitre and staff blaze. Bermejo had absorbed the Flemish
 oil technique — the ability to render every texture and reflection with microscopic care — and he uses it
 to make the gold and gems seem to throw off real light.

 The effect is almost overwhelming, deliberately so: this was painted as the centre of an altarpiece, meant
 to stop a worshipper in their tracks with a glimpse of heavenly glory. There is little movement, little
 drama — just a frontal, hieratic image of holiness made of light and gold. It is the medieval altarpiece at
 its absolute height. Now we step into Italy, and the gentle dawn of the Renaissance — Fra Angelico,
 right this way.
