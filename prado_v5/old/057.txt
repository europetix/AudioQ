 Bosch, The Garden of Earthly Delights
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Stand before the enormous three-panelled painting — paradise on the left, a vast crowded garden in the
    centre, and a dark, burning hell on the right.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A triptych. Left: Eden, where God presents Eve to Adam among strange beasts. Centre: a sprawling
    pink-and-green landscape swarming with hundreds of naked figures, giant fruit and birds, riding animals and
    embracing in endless play. Right: a black, fiery hell of torture, monstrous machines, and a man-tree with a
    hollow body.
    LISTEN FOR
    Don't try to "solve" it all at once. Move left to right — innocence, indulgence, punishment — then lose
    yourself in the details.

 Here it is — the most famous, most debated, most inexhaustible painting in the museum, and perhaps in
 all of Western art. Hieronymus Bosch painted The Garden of Earthly Delights around five hundred
 years ago, and we still cannot fully explain it. That is precisely its power.

 Read it left to right, like a story. On the left wing, Paradise: a serene Eden where God joins Adam and
 Eve, though even here strange, faintly sinister creatures lurk. In the centre — the vast, mesmerising
 heart of the work — hundreds of naked men and women frolic across a pink-and-green dreamscape,
 riding giant birds and beasts, climbing into enormous berries, embracing, playing, indulging every
 appetite in an endless, weightless carnival of pleasure. And on the right wing, the reckoning: a black,
 burning hell where those same pleasures turn to torment — bodies pierced by their own instruments, a
 monstrous bird-headed devil devouring sinners, the eerie "tree-man" with a hollow eggshell body
 staring out at us.

 Scholars argue endlessly about its meaning — a warning against lust and worldly pleasure, a lost
 paradise, an alchemical riddle. Bosch left no key. What is certain is the inexhaustible invention: every
 square inch teems with bizarre, precise, dreamlike detail you could study for hours. Lean in close and
 keep finding new things. When you can pull away, turn to a second Bosch triptych with a clearer
 message, this way now.
