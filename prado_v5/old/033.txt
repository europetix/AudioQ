 Veronese, Venus and Adonis
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Move to the painting of a young man asleep across the lap of a richly-dressed woman beneath a tree, with
    hunting dogs and a small winged Cupid nearby.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    In a shaded landscape, the beautiful Venus sits and cradles the sleeping young hunter Adonis, who lies back
    across her lap. A small winged Cupid restrains an eager hunting dog at the right. The fabrics shimmer —
    silver, rose, gold — and warm light filters through the trees.
    LISTEN FOR
    Notice she watches him sleep, tender and uneasy. Veronese paints the calm before a death she cannot prevent.

 Paolo Veronese was the third of the great Venetian painters, famous above all for opulence —
 shimmering silks, golden light, an air of sumptuous ease. Here he turns that gift to a tender and
 sorrowful myth: the love of the goddess Venus for the mortal hunter Adonis.

 The story ends in tragedy — Adonis will be killed by a wild boar on the hunt — and Veronese paints the
 still moment before. Adonis sleeps, exhausted, across Venus's lap; she gazes down at him, fanning him,
 her face touched with tenderness and a flicker of foreboding. At the side, Cupid holds back one of the
 hunting dogs straining to be off, a small reminder of the fatal hunt to come. The whole scene is bathed
 in a warm, golden Venetian light, the fabrics rendered with that famous tactile luxury. It is love and
 mortality wrapped in beauty — the goddess unable to hold back fate, the sleeping youth unaware. The
 Spanish kings adored these Venetian "poems" of myth and flesh. Beside the paintings stands a different
 kind of royal art — sculpture in bronze. Step back into the Central Gallery and find the Leoni, this way
 now.
