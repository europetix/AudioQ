 Botticelli, The Story of Nastagio degli Onesti
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Find the long horizontal panels telling a single story in stages — a young man in a pine wood, a fleeing
    woman pursued by a knight and hounds, then a banquet.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Across connected panels, a narrative unfolds in a pine forest by the sea: a young man recoils as a naked
    woman flees, chased down by a mounted knight and dogs; the violent vision repeats; finally a formal banquet
    is set among the trees. Elegant figures in fifteenth-century dress react in horror and wonder.
    LISTEN FOR
    Read it left to right like a comic strip — the same figures reappear as the grisly story plays out in stages.

 Here is Sandro Botticelli, the most poetic of Florentine painters, in storytelling mode — and telling a
 genuinely strange and chilling tale. These panels illustrate a story from Boccaccio's Decameron, and
 they were painted to celebrate a Florentine wedding.

 The tale: a young man named Nastagio, rejected by the woman he loves, wanders into a pine wood by
 the sea, where he witnesses a horrifying vision — a naked woman pursued and savaged by a knight and
 his hounds, over and over, as eternal punishment for having scorned the knight's love in life. Nastagio
 cleverly arranges for his own cold-hearted beloved to witness the same gruesome spectacle at a banquet
 in the wood — and, terrified, she finally agrees to marry him. The series ends with the wedding feast.
 Botticelli unfolds it like a strip of film, the same figures reappearing across the panels as the story
 advances. The pine wood, the elegant costumes, the lithe Botticelli figures with their flowing lines — all
 his trademark grace is here, applied to a tale that mixes courtship, cruelty, and the supernatural. It is a
 wedding gift with a distinctly dark moral about the cost of refusing love. Now, a small masterpiece of
 Italian feeling and precision — Antonello da Messina, over this way.
