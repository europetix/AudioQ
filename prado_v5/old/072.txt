TRACK 072 · 75                                      W-FOCUS                               215 WORDS · ~2 MIN

 Gisbert, The Execution of Torrijos and his
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Move to the large, bleak scene on a wide beach at dawn: a row of condemned men and their executioners,
    bodies already fallen on the cold sand.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A grey beach under a pale dawn sky. A line of condemned men is led to execution; some are already dead,
    sprawled on the sand, while a priest attends the living. Soldiers stand by. The mood is desolate, the light cold
    and even, the sea flat and indifferent behind.
    LISTEN FOR
    Notice there is no glory here, only cold dawn and death — and that bleakness is itself a fierce political
    statement.

 This grim, powerful painting is history with a sharp political edge. Antonio Gisbert painted it on
 commission from a liberal government, to honour a group of liberal martyrs — General Torrijos and his
 companions, who in 1831 tried to rise against the absolute rule of King Ferdinand the Seventh, were
 lured into a trap, and were shot without trial on a beach near Málaga.

 By the time Gisbert painted it, decades later, these men had become symbols of the long, bitter struggle
 for liberty and constitutional government in Spain — and the painting was meant as a monument to that
 cause. Gisbert refuses any heroics or consolation. He gives us a bleak, grey dawn on a cold beach; some
 of the condemned already lie dead on the sand, others await their turn as a priest moves among them.
 The light is flat and pitiless, the sea indifferent behind.

 That very bleakness is the point. By stripping the scene of glory and showing only the squalid reality of
 political murder, Gisbert turns the painting into an accusation — a protest against tyranny dressed as
 history. It hangs here as one of the great liberal images of nineteenth-century Spain. Now, a complete
 change of mood — the dazzling virtuosity of Fortuny, a little way along the gallery.
