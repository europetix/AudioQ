 Rubens, The Three Graces
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Step up to the painting of three nude women standing close together in a ring, arms draped over one another's
    shoulders, beneath a garland and a tree.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Three full-figured nude women — the mythological Graces — stand intertwined in a gentle circle, arms
    across each other's backs and shoulders, a flower garland looped above them. Behind, a soft landscape with a
    fountain and grazing deer. Their skin is luminous, pearly, touched with rose.
    LISTEN FOR
    Look how they touch — the easy, affectionate contact of the bodies. Rubens paints warmth, abundance, and
    real human softness.

 This was one of Rubens's most cherished paintings; he kept it in his own house until he died, and you
 can feel why. The Three Graces were the classical goddesses of charm, beauty, and joy, usually shown
 as an intertwined trio — but Rubens makes them gloriously, unmistakably real.

 These are not cold marble ideals. They are warm, soft, full-bodied women with dimpled flesh and rosy
 skin, standing close in a circle of easy intimacy, arms draped over one another, leaning in. Rubens loved
 this kind of generous, healthy body, and he painted it with sensuous tenderness — the pearly play of
 light across skin is among the finest passages of flesh-painting in European art. The model for at least
 one of the Graces is thought to be his own young second wife, Helena Fourment, whom he adored. The
 whole picture breathes affection, fertility, and well-being: the garland of flowers, the fountain, the deer
 in the meadow beyond. It is Rubens at his happiest and most personal — a private celebration of love
 and the body, made for himself rather than a patron. Now, Rubens at the opposite scale — a towering
 altarpiece blazing with drama, a few steps on.
