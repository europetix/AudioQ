 Van der Weyden, The Descent from the Cross
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Come round to the large panel where the dead Christ is lowered from the cross, his body and the swooning
    Virgin below echoing each other, all set in a shallow gold-edged box.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Ten near-life-size figures crowd a shallow gold-backed space, like a carved shrine. The dead Christ is
    lowered from the cross; directly below, the Virgin Mary collapses in a faint, her body curving in exactly the
    same line as her son's. Mourners weep around them, faces raw with grief. A skull lies at the foot.
    LISTEN FOR
    Trace the two curving bodies — Christ above, his fainting mother below. Their matching arcs make her grief
    physically mirror his death.

 This may be the greatest single painting on the ground floor, and one of the supreme masterpieces of all
 Northern European art. Rogier van der Weyden was the leading Flemish painter of the mid-fifteenth
 century, and this Descent from the Cross is his most famous work — an image of grief so powerful it
 has never been surpassed.

 Van der Weyden does something brilliant and strange: he compresses ten near-life-size figures into a
 shallow, gold-backed box, like the interior of a carved wooden shrine. There is no deep landscape, no
 distance — just this crowded, stage-like space that pushes all the emotion forward, right up against you.
 The dead Christ is being lowered from the cross, his pale body sagging. And directly beneath him, the
 Virgin Mary swoons in unbearable grief, fainting into the arms of the mourners.

 Now look at the two bodies — Christ's and his mother's — and you will see the masterstroke: they curve
 in almost exactly the same line, the same pose, the same fall. Van der Weyden makes the mother's
 collapse physically echo her son's death; her suffering mirrors his, body for body. Around them, the
 faces of the other mourners are studies in raw, specific grief — tears, swollen eyes, hands clutched in
 anguish. Every fabric, tear, and texture is rendered with that hyper-real Flemish precision. Philip the
 Second treasured it above almost anything. Spend a moment with it. Now we turn to the most
 extraordinary imagination in the whole museum — Bosch, coming up.
