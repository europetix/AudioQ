 Titian and the Venetian colourists
 You have now seen the Spanish school; here is the foreign root it grew from. Walk the museum's long
 Central Gallery and you walk through the single greatest passion of the Spanish kings: Venetian
 painting, and above all Titian.

 The Prado holds one of the finest gatherings of Titian anywhere on earth, and there is a reason. Titian
 was the favourite painter of the Emperor Charles the Fifth and his son Philip the Second — the most
 powerful men in Europe — and they bought everything he made. He never set foot in Spain, yet his
 work poured into the royal collection by the crate, and it taught every Spanish painter who came after,
 Velázquez above all, how colour and loose, sensuous brushwork could carry a picture. This is the
 "colourist tradition" the whole museum is built on: the belief, born in Venice, that painting is made of
 light and pigment and feeling rather than of careful drawing. Beside Titian you will meet his Venetian
 heirs, Tintoretto and Veronese, and the Flemish giant Rubens, who carried the same fire north. Begin
 with the painting that became the template for royal power itself, just along here.
