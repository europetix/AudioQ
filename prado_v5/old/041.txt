 Guido Reni, Hippomenes and Atalanta
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Step across to the dark, elegant painting of two near-nude runners frozen mid-stride — a woman bending to
    snatch something from the ground, a man racing on.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Against a shadowy ground, two beautiful athletic figures move in opposite directions, their pale bodies and
    fluttering drapery sharply lit. The young man, Hippomenes, runs on while glancing back; the young woman,
    Atalanta, stoops to seize a golden apple from the ground. Their gestures form a graceful, dance-like pattern.
    LISTEN FOR
    Watch the bodies make an almost choreographed shape — Reni turns a footrace into a moment of pure,
    balanced elegance.

 Guido Reni was, for centuries, considered one of the supreme painters of Italy — the master of grace,
 idealised beauty, and serene classical balance — and the Prado names him among the key figures of the
 seventeenth century. This is his most admired work here, and a perfect demonstration of his cool,
 elegant art.

 It illustrates a myth of love and cunning. Atalanta was a huntress so swift that she would only marry a
 man who could beat her in a footrace — and losers were put to death. The young Hippomenes won by a
 trick: he dropped three irresistible golden apples, a gift from Venus, and each time Atalanta paused to
 snatch one up, he gained ground and ultimately the race, and her hand.

 Reni catches the decisive instant: Hippomenes surges ahead while Atalanta bends to grab a gleaming
 apple. But what makes the painting unforgettable is its formal beauty — the two pale, idealised bodies
 set against deep shadow, their limbs and streaming drapery composing an almost abstract pattern of
 curves, like a frozen dance. The colour is restrained, the mood graceful and weightless. It is the opposite
 of Caravaggio's rough drama beside it — beauty as harmony rather than shock. Now, a rare and
 powerful woman's voice in this collection — Artemisia Gentileschi, just through here.
