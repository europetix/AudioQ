 The Velázquez suite
 You are about to enter the rooms that justify the journey to Madrid. No museum on earth holds
 Velázquez the way the Prado does — almost everything he painted after his Seville youth is gathered
 here, because he spent his life as court painter to a king who could simply keep the pictures. A word on
 the man. Diego Velázquez came up from Seville, a prodigy who painted water-sellers, cooks and
 kitchen pots with a gravity usually saved for saints. At twenty-four he was summoned to court, and
 there he stayed for life — painting Philip the Fourth, the royal children, the court dwarfs, the gods of
 myth, and finally the deepest puzzles a painter has ever set himself.

 He was strange for his time in barely sketching; he worked straight onto the canvas, building images
 from loose, confident touches that only resolve when you step back. Get close to any picture in these
 rooms and it dissolves into smears and dashes; walk backward and it snaps into breathing life. That trick
 — paint as suggestion, not description — is what makes painters fall silent in here. Manet called him
 "the painter of painters," and half of modern art, from Goya to the Impressionists, runs straight back to
 these walls.

 Take these galleries slowly. We start where everyone must: with one of the most analysed paintings in
 the history of Western art, up ahead.
