 Caravaggio, David with the Head of Goliath
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Find the dark canvas of a young man emerging from blackness, holding up by the hair the severed head of a
    bearded giant.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Out of deep shadow, a young David steps forward, a sword in one hand, lifting the heavy severed head of the
    giant Goliath by its hair. The boy's expression is not triumphant but pensive, almost sorrowful. A single harsh
    light rakes across the two faces; everything else is darkness.
    LISTEN FOR
    Look at David's face — not gloating, but troubled. Caravaggio drains the triumph out of victory and leaves
    only unease.

 Caravaggio changed European painting forever, and his shadow falls across half this museum — on
 Ribera, on Velázquez's early work, on Spanish realism itself. This is his only painting in the Prado, and
 it distils everything that made him revolutionary.

 The subject is the Old Testament hero David, who slew the giant Goliath with a slung stone and then cut
 off his head. But where another painter would give us a chest-thumping victor, Caravaggio gives us
 something far stranger and more modern. The young David holds up the dripping head, yes — but his
 face is pensive, even pitying, as if sickened by what he has done. Triumph curdles into doubt.
 Caravaggio refuses to let us simply enjoy the win.

 And then there is the light — his great invention. A single harsh beam slices in from one side, dragging
 the two faces and the boy's shoulder out of an enveloping blackness and leaving everything else in
 shadow. This is tenebrism, the violent contrast of light and dark, and it makes the scene feel real,
 immediate, and dangerous, like a moment glimpsed by torchlight. Every realist painter in this museum
 owes something to that beam of light. Now, a calmer Italian Baroque master, all grace and balance —
 Guido Reni, right in front of you.
