 Spain's turbulent nineteenth century
 After Goya, Spanish painting had a giant's shadow to step out of — and the nineteenth century was a
 turbulent age to do it in: a century of revolutions, civil wars, lost colonies, and constant political
 upheaval. These rooms gather the painters who navigated it, and they fall into two broad camps. First,
 the grand history painters. In an age hungry for national identity, huge canvases dramatising key
 moments of Spanish history won the official medals and filled the salons — scenes of queens and
 executions, triumph and tragedy, painted on a vast, theatrical scale. They are the visual equivalent of
 grand opera.

 Then, toward the century's end, something looser and brighter breaks through — a new love of light,
 colour, everyday life, and the open air, leading eventually to the dazzling sunshine of Sorolla. The
 century travels, in other words, from solemn history toward pure light.

 It is a less famous chapter than the Golden Age or Goya, but a rich one, and it completes the Prado's
 story of Spanish painting right up to the threshold of the modern age. Begin by meeting the giant whose
 shadow falls across this whole century — Goya himself, painted near the end of his life, waiting just
 over there.
