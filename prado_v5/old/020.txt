 Murillo, the gentle Sevillian
 If Zurbarán is the painter of austerity, Bartolomé Esteban Murillo is the painter of sweetness — and in
 his own day he was far more popular for it. Born in Seville, where he spent his whole life, Murillo
 became the most beloved Spanish painter in Europe for nearly two centuries after his death, prized for
 exactly the qualities the modern age later distrusted: tenderness, charm, soft light, and an easy emotional
 warmth.

 He painted two things supremely well. The first is religious devotion at its most approachable — gentle
 Virgins, dreamy Immaculate Conceptions, saints lit by a warm golden glow that seems to melt the edges
 of everything. The second, and quite different, is the street children of Seville: ragged urchins eating
 grapes, playing dice, grinning out at us — some of the first paintings in European art to treat ordinary
 poor children as worthy subjects in their own right.

 For a long time Murillo's very popularity counted against him; critics found him too sweet, too easy. But
 stand in front of the real thing and the technical mastery is undeniable — the melting light, the feathery
 brushwork, the genuine human feeling. He is the soft, glowing close of the Spanish Golden Age. Start
 with the painting that made his name across Europe, right this way.
