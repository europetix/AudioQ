 Velázquez, Las Meninas
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Stand well back from the enormous canvas — over three metres tall — of a little blonde princess surrounded
    by her court, the painter himself at a giant easel on the left.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A high, shadowy room. At the centre, the five-year-old Infanta Margarita in a silver gown, attended by two
    kneeling maids of honour. Around them: two dwarfs, a dog, a chaperone, a man paused in a lit doorway at the
    back, and Velázquez himself, brush in hand, before a canvas we cannot see. On the rear wall, a mirror.
    LISTEN FOR
    Find the mirror at the back. The two blurred faces in it are the king and queen — standing exactly where you
    are standing now.

 This is the painting people cross the world to stand before, and the strange thing is how ordinary its
 subject first seems: a snapshot of a palace afternoon. But give it a minute and the floor drops away,
 because Velázquez has done something no painter had dared — he has made you, the viewer, the secret
 subject of the picture.

 Here is the puzzle. Velázquez stands at a huge canvas, painting — but painting whom? Everyone in the
 room has turned to look out at a point in front of the canvas: the spot where you stand. Then you find the
 mirror on the back wall, and in it two soft, ghostly faces — King Philip the Fourth and Queen Mariana.
 They are the ones being painted. They are standing where you stand. For a vertiginous moment you
 occupy the royal couple's own position, looking back at the painter and the princess looking at you. It is
 a machine for thinking about looking — who sees and who is seen, where the real world ends and the
 painted one begins. Velázquez signs his ambition into it, too: he wears the red cross of the Order of
 Santiago, the noble honour he craved all his life and won only at the end. Legend says the king painted
 it on after his death.

 And technically it is his summit. Step close: the silver dress is a storm of grey and white slashes, the dog
 a few brown strokes, the whole thing apparently flung down at speed. Step back, and it is one of the
 most convincing rooms ever painted. Look as long as the crowd allows — then turn to Velázquez the
 painter of war, in the next room along.
