 Mantegna, The Death of the Virgin
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Turn now to the small, intense panel of apostles gathered around the deathbed of the Virgin Mary, with a
    wide view of a lake and town through an open window behind.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    In a tiled room, the apostles cluster around the Virgin laid out in death, holding candles and a censer, some
    grieving, one reading. Behind them, through a large open arch, stretches a precise, luminous landscape: a lake
    crossed by a long causeway, with towers in the distance.
    LISTEN FOR
    Look through the window at the real lake beyond — Mantegna paints a specific place, the lakes of his own
    city, with cool precision.

 Small in size but tremendous in power, this is a jewel by Andrea Mantegna, one of the most rigorous
 and influential minds of the early Renaissance. Where Fra Angelico is tender, Mantegna is hard,
 sculptural, and intellectually severe — every figure carved as if from stone, every line exact. The scene
 is the death — the "dormition," or falling-asleep — of the Virgin Mary, surrounded by the grieving
 apostles. Mantegna sets it in a precisely measured tiled room, the figures arranged with grave,
 monumental weight, each face a study in sorrow. There is no medieval softness here; these are solid,
 real bodies in real, mathematically constructed space. Mantegna was obsessed with perspective and
 antiquity, and you feel that disciplined intelligence everywhere.

 The unforgettable touch is the window. Behind the deathbed, a great arch opens onto a wide, luminous
 landscape — a real view of a lake crossed by a long bridge, the lagoons of Mantua where Mantegna
 worked. It is one of the first times a painter set a sacred scene against a recognisable, specific place,
 painted with crystalline clarity. That clear, hard light and exact landscape would influence Venetian
 painting for generations. Now, the great Florentine storyteller — Botticelli, just past the doorway.
