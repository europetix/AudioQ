 Murillo, The Immaculate Conception of Los
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Step up to the tall, glowing canvas of the Virgin Mary in white and blue, floating upward on a crescent moon
    amid a swirling cloud of cherubs.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A young Virgin Mary, in a white robe and billowing blue cloak, stands on a pale crescent moon, hands
    pressed to her breast, eyes lifted. She rises in a golden light through a swirling crowd of chubby cherubs,
    some carrying lilies and palms. Her loose hair and the cloak stream as if caught in an updraft.
    LISTEN FOR
    Feel the upward motion — everything streams skyward. Murillo turns a point of theology into pure,
    weightless ascent.

 This is the painting that fixed Murillo's fame, and it is the definitive image of a subject the Spanish were
 obsessed with: the Immaculate Conception — the doctrine that Mary herself was conceived free of
 original sin. It is an abstract theological idea, and Murillo's genius was to turn it into something you feel
 in your body: pure, joyful, upward flight.

 Look how everything rises. The Virgin stands on the crescent moon — the traditional attribute, drawn
 from the Book of Revelation — but she does not stand stiffly; she seems to float and lift, her cloak and
 hair streaming upward as if she were being drawn into heaven by light itself. Around and below her, a
 riot of cherubs tumbles through golden cloud, some bearing the lily of purity and the palm. Your eye is
 pulled irresistibly up toward the radiance above.

 This is Murillo's estilo vaporoso, his "vaporous style" — the soft, dissolving edges, the warm golden
 haze, the feathery touch that makes the whole thing feel weightless and dreamlike. It was endlessly
 copied and adored; for generations this was simply what the Immaculate Conception looked like. The
 painting takes its name from the Hospital de los Venerables in Seville, for which it was made. It later
 became one of the most famous spoils of the Napoleonic Wars, carried off to Paris before eventually
 returning to Spain. Now, for a warmer, more human Murillo — a gentle family at home — turn this
 way.
