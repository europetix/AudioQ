 The San Ildefonso Group (Roman sculpture)
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Stand before the life-size marble of two nude young men standing close together, one resting an arm gently
    across the other's shoulders, a small altar beside them.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A marble sculpture of two idealised nude youths standing side by side, almost the same height, one laying an
    arm tenderly over the other's shoulder. They hold attributes by a small altar. The carving is smooth and
    classical; the pose is still, intimate, and beautifully balanced.
    LISTEN FOR
    Notice the gentle arm across the shoulder — the tenderness between the two figures is what has fascinated
    viewers for centuries.

 The Prado is not only paintings — the Spanish kings also collected classical sculpture, and this is among
 the most admired pieces. The San Ildefonso Group is a Roman marble of around two thousand years
 old, named after the royal palace where it once stood.

 It shows two beautiful young men standing close together, idealised in the classical manner, one resting
 an arm affectionately across the other's shoulders. Exactly who they represent has been debated for
 centuries — perhaps the mythological twins Castor and Pollux, perhaps figures connected to sleep and
 death, perhaps Orestes and Pylades, the legendary devoted friends. The tender intimacy of the two
 figures has always been part of its appeal.

 The sculpture is a fine example of the Roman taste for combining and adapting earlier Greek styles —
 the bodies carved with that smooth, harmonious idealism the Renaissance and later ages so admired.
 Pieces like this shaped how European artists understood the human form; Velázquez, Goya, and the rest
 studied such antiquities closely. It reminds us that the royal collection reached back beyond the Middle
 Ages, all the way to the ancient world. Now we climb to the top floor — for Goya's brightest
 beginnings, a Flemish cabinet of wonders, and a king's treasure, further along.
