 Rubens, The Judgement of Paris
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Turn to the canvas of three naked goddesses standing in a landscape, presenting themselves to a seated young
    shepherd who holds a golden apple, while a winged boy and a peacock look on.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    On the right, the Trojan prince Paris sits with the god Mercury, holding the apple he must award to the most
    beautiful goddess. Before him stand three nudes seen front, side and back: Venus at the centre, Athena with
    her armour, Juno with her peacock. A small dark fury hovers in the sky above.
    LISTEN FOR
    Notice that the three goddesses are almost one body, turned three ways — Rubens showing you a single ideal
    of beauty from every angle at once.

 This was a personal commission from King Philip the Fourth, painted at the end of Rubens's life, and it
 is his ideal of beauty distilled into a single picture. The myth is the contest that started the Trojan War:
 Paris must hand the golden apple to Venus, Juno or Athena, and his choice of Venus — bribed with the
 promise of the most beautiful woman alive — will doom his city.

 Watch what Rubens does with the three figures. Rather than three different women, he gives you
 essentially one perfect body rotated in space — front, profile and back — so the painting becomes a
 kind of demonstration of the nude from every viewpoint at once. The flesh is warm, dimpled, alive; the
 brushwork loose and openly Venetian, the lessons of Titian in every stroke. The king's own brother,
 who handled the commission, fretted in his letters about the frankness of all that nudity. Rubens painted
 it exactly as he pleased.

 There is a shadow over the pleasure, though. Up in the dark sky a small fury hovers — the spirit of the
 war to come. Even at his most sensuous, Rubens lets you feel the cost hidden inside Paris's choice. Now
 we cross from Flemish warmth to Italian darkness — Caravaggio, over this way.
