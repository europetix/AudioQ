 Goya, The 2nd of May 1808
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Turn now to the violent, swirling scene of a street fight: civilians with knives hurling themselves at
    richly-dressed cavalrymen on rearing horses.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A chaotic mêlée in a Madrid square. Spanish civilians, armed only with knives and bare hands, surge against
    mounted soldiers — Napoleon's Egyptian Mameluke cavalry — dragging them from rearing horses. Bodies,
    blades, and animals tangle in a churning mass. Blood stains the ground; the violence is close and frenzied.
    LISTEN FOR
    Notice there's no hero, no centre — just a churning tangle of bodies. Goya paints the pure chaos of a riot.

 This is the companion to The 3rd of May, and it shows what came before — the spark. On the 2nd of
 May 1808, the people of Madrid rose up against the occupying French, and Goya paints the explosion of
 that fury in the city's streets.

 The scene is the famous charge of the Mamelukes — the exotic Egyptian cavalry in Napoleon's service,
 sent to crush the rising. Goya throws us straight into the chaos: ordinary citizens, armed with nothing
 but knives and their own rage, hurl themselves at the mounted soldiers, dragging them down from their
 rearing, panicking horses. There is no clear hero, no orderly composition — just a churning, frenzied
 knot of men, blades, and animals, blood already on the cobbles.

 Where a traditional battle painting would organise the action and elevate a leader, Goya gives us the
 raw, disorienting confusion of street violence — close, brutal, and without glory. It is the modern riot,
 the people as a desperate mob. And it sets up the tragedy of the next day: this fury is exactly what the
 firing squads of the 3rd of May will punish. Together the two canvases are a single statement about
 uprising and reprisal. Now, deeper into Goya's darkness — the Black Paintings, just here.
