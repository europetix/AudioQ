 Rubens, The Adoration of the Magi
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Stand back from the enormous, crowded altarpiece of the three kings bringing gifts to the infant Christ,
    torches and banners rising in the throng.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A vast, teeming scene. The Virgin holds the Christ child as the three Magi press forward to adore him, one
    kneeling, one in gold, one a richly-dressed African king. Around them surge attendants, soldiers, camels,
    torches and standards, all swept up in swirling Baroque movement and warm golden light.
    LISTEN FOR
    Step back to feel the sheer scale and surge — Rubens turns a tender Nativity into a roaring, jubilant
    procession.

 If The Three Graces was intimate, this is Rubens in full public voice — one of the grandest things he
 ever painted, and a wall of pure Baroque energy. The subject is the Adoration of the Magi: the three
 wise kings, having followed the star, arriving to lay their gifts before the newborn Christ. Rubens turns
 the scene into a vast, surging spectacle. Where a tamer painter might give us a simple stable, Rubens
 packs the canvas with a heaving crowd — kings and servants, soldiers and camels, torchbearers and
 waving banners — all caught in that characteristic swirling movement, diagonals sweeping the eye up
 and across. The light blazes warm and golden; the colours are rich and full-throated. One of the kings is
 shown as a magnificent African monarch, a reminder that the Magi traditionally represent all the
 peoples of the earth come to worship.

 Rubens actually painted this early in his career and then, years later, enlarged and reworked it himself
 — you are looking at a masterpiece revisited by its maker at the height of his powers. It is faith rendered
 as overwhelming abundance. Now, Rubens turns from heaven to earthly pleasure, close by.
