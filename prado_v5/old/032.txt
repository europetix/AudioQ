 Tintoretto, The Washing of the Feet
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Stand before the very wide canvas of a tiled hall where Christ kneels to wash a disciple's foot on the right,
    while other men sit and undress along a deep receding floor.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A long, theatrical hall in sharp perspective. On the right, Christ kneels with a basin to wash Saint Peter's foot.
    Across the tiled floor the other apostles sit, remove sandals, talk in groups. A table, a dog, and a grand
    Venetian architecture stretch into deep space behind.
    LISTEN FOR
    Stand to one side and look along the floor — the tiles rush back so steeply the whole room seems to tilt and
    deepen.

 Tintoretto was Titian's younger Venetian rival, and where Titian gives you warmth and colour,
 Tintoretto gives you drama, speed, and dizzying space. This large canvas shows the moment from the
 Last Supper when Christ kneels to wash the feet of his disciples — an act of startling humility, the
 master serving the servants.

 What grips you first is the staging. Tintoretto builds a vast tiled hall that plunges back into the distance
 in steep, almost vertiginous perspective; if you move to one side, the floor seems to swing and tilt
 beneath you. He scatters the apostles across this stage in casual, lifelike poses — one tugging off a
 sandal, others deep in conversation, a dog wandering through — so that the holy moment unfolds amid
 ordinary human bustle.

 The real subject is humility. Christ, who could command, instead kneels with a basin; Peter,
 embarrassed, draws back his foot. Tintoretto sets this humble act of service inside a grand, busy world,
 the divine gesture almost lost in the everyday — which is rather the point. The picture once hung in a
 Venetian church and entered the Spanish royal collection as a prize. Now, the third great Venetian, the
 master of sumptuous elegance — Veronese, waiting just over there.
