    WHAT YOU SEE
    Two men in rich seventeenth-century dress stand close together, half-length. On the left, the heavier-set

    himself. Their hands nearly meet over a stone the colour of the background. Both look calmly outward.
    LISTEN FOR
    Find the painter on the right — Van Dyck has placed himself beside his patron as an equal, almost a friend.
    That was a bold claim.

 A change of country and mood. Anthony van Dyck was Flemish, the most gifted pupil of Rubens, and
 the most glamorous portraitist of his age — the man who taught all of Europe's aristocracy how they
 wished to look. The Spanish kings collected him avidly alongside their own painters, which is why he
 hangs here among the Spanish school.

 This is a double portrait, and an unusual, revealing one: on the left, Van Dyck's English friend and
 patron Endymion Porter, a courtier of King Charles the First; on the right, Van Dyck himself. What
 makes it so remarkable is the relationship it claims. A painter in this period was, socially, a craftsman —
 a servant of his betters. Yet here Van Dyck places himself shoulder to shoulder with a gentleman of the
 court, dressed as finely, looking out with the same composure, the two of them linked almost as equals
 and companions.

 It is, in a sense, an argument about the dignity of the artist — the same battle Velázquez fought for the
 cross of Santiago. Van Dyck makes his case simply by where he stands.

 Look at the textures: the silken sheen of the clothes, the soft modelling of the faces, the effortless
 elegance that made Van Dyck so sought after. There is a cool, refined glamour here quite different from
 Spanish gravity. Now we turn to the second titan of Spanish art — Goya — beginning with the painter
 who served the crown, just ahead.
