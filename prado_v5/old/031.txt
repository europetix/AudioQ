 Titian, The Furias (Tityus and Sisyphus)
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Turn to the two huge, dark vertical canvases of muscular naked giants in torment — one sprawled beneath a
    vulture, the other crushed under an enormous boulder he carries uphill.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Two condemned giants of Greek myth, life-size and straining. Tityus lies chained in the underworld while a
    vulture tears at his liver, his body twisting in pain. Sisyphus staggers upward under a colossal rock lashed to
    his back, forever pushing it up a slope it will always roll back down. Fire and gloom surround them.
    LISTEN FOR
    These were painted to be hung high and looked up at — imagine them above you, the giants toppling out of
    the dark.

 Titian was the Habsburgs' favourite painter, and most of what he made for them was flattering
 portraiture. These two are something else entirely: a commission from Mary of Hungary, the emperor's
 sister, for a great hall — and a warning. They are two of the four "Furias," giants of antiquity punished
 eternally by the gods for crimes against divine order.

 The political message was pointed. Mary hung them to remind any restless subject what becomes of
 those who defy a rightful ruler: eternal, monumental suffering. But forget the politics for a moment and
 simply look at the bodies. This is Titian around sixty, painting enormous nudes in violent, twisting
 foreshortening — flesh built from rough, broken, almost furious brushwork that only resolves into
 muscle when you step back. He is competing head-on with Michelangelo's sculptural power and
 answering it with pure paint and shadow.

 They are grim, and meant to be. Hung high above eye level in their original room, these giants would
 have loomed down out of the murk like a nightmare of power and its consequences. Few painters have
 ever made damnation this physical. Now, from Titian's thunder to his great Venetian rival and his
 theatrical drama — Tintoretto, just here.
