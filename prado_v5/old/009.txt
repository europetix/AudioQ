 Velázquez, The Equestrian Portraits (Philip IV,
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Stay in the great gallery and turn to the large canvases of riders on horseback — the king, a small boy on a
    round-bellied pony, and a heavy man twisting in the saddle to meet your eye.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Three life-size riders against open sky. Philip the Fourth sits calm and upright on a stepping horse, seen in
    profile, master of all he surveys. His little son Baltasar Carlos charges almost out of the frame on a fat pony.
    The Count-Duke of Olivares wheels his mount and looks back over his shoulder, baton raised, the smoke of
    battle behind him.
    LISTEN FOR
    Compare the three poses — who faces straight ahead, who looks out at us, who turns away — and read the
    court's whole pecking order in the angles.

 These once hung together in the throne room of a vanished palace, the Hall of Realms, a chamber built
 for one purpose: to advertise the power of the Spanish crown. Velázquez designed the whole decorative
 scheme and painted the finest of these himself. Stand here and you are looking at the surviving
 fragments of a royal propaganda machine — except that the propaganda turned out to be art. Read them
 as Velázquez meant them read, by their poses. Philip the Fourth rides in severe profile, eyes front, not
 on us — serene, untroubled, a king by birthright who needs to prove nothing at all. His five-year-old
 heir, Baltasar Carlos, gallops head-on out of the canvas on a barrel-chested pony, all baby cheeks and
 boundless future — the boy who would die before he could inherit any of it. And then Olivares, the
 king's all-powerful favourite: he twists his horse into a dramatic, foreshortened turn and looks straight
 back at us, baton in hand, a battle smoking in the distance. He is not a king, and the picture knows it —
 so he must perform his authority, earn it with effort, where the king simply possesses it.

 That is the sly genius of the set. Velázquez lays out the entire hierarchy of a court using almost nothing
 but the direction each man turns his face. Look, too, at the land behind the king — the real foothills of
 the Guadarrama outside Madrid, painted with a freedom that melts into pure light when you step close.
 Now meet the other half of that same court — not its rulers, but the people kept to amuse them, painted
 with a tenderness that will surprise you, just along here.
