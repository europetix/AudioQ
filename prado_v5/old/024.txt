 Goya I: painter to the crown
 We now meet the other giant this museum is built around — and no museum holds him as the Prado
 does. Francisco de Goya spans the whole turn from the eighteenth century into the modern world, and
 his career is one of the strangest journeys in art: from cheerful designer of royal tapestries, to
 fashionable court portraitist, to the deaf, disillusioned prophet of the Black Paintings we will meet
 downstairs.

 These rooms hold the first Goya — the successful one. He clawed his way up from the provinces to
 become First Court Painter to King Charles the Fourth, painting the royal family, the aristocracy, and
 the glittering society of Madrid. He was brilliant at it: a portraitist of bravura skill and, increasingly,
 unsettling honesty.

 And that honesty is the thing to watch for. Goya flattered no one. Where earlier court painters smoothed
 and idealised, Goya looked at his royal sitters and painted exactly what he saw — vanity, weakness,
 dull-wittedness, the strain behind the finery — and somehow got away with it. The kings seem not to
 have noticed, or not to have minded.

 Keep that double game in mind as you look: official grandeur on the surface, sharp human truth
 underneath. It comes to a head in one of the most famous, and most understated yet devastating, royal
 group portraits ever painted, close by.
