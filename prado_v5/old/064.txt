 Dürer, Adam and Eve
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Find the two tall, narrow panels — a nude man on one, a nude woman on the other — each standing pale
    against a dark forest background.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Two matching panels. On one, Adam strides forward, an idealised male nude holding a branch; on the other,
    Eve, an idealised female nude, takes the apple from the serpent coiled in a tree. Their pale bodies glow
    against a black woodland. A small plaque hangs from a branch beside Adam.
    LISTEN FOR
    Look how perfect and balanced the two bodies are — Dürer is chasing the ideal human form, north of the
    Alps, from theory.

 These two panels are Dürer's great meditation on the perfect human body — and on the moment
 everything went wrong. Adam and Eve shows the first man and woman at the instant of the Fall, when
 Eve takes the forbidden fruit from the serpent and offers it to Adam.

 But Dürer's deeper subject is the ideal nude. He had become fascinated, during his Italian travels and his
 own theoretical studies, with the classical idea that the human body could be constructed to perfect
 proportions — and here he paints Adam and Eve as flawless, idealised figures, the man and woman as
 they were before sin, in their original perfection. He has separated them onto two tall, elegant panels,
 their pale, luminous bodies standing out against the darkness of the Garden's forest, posed with a
 graceful, almost dancing balance.

 It is fascinating to see a northern artist, famous for his microscopic realism, reaching instead for
 Italian-style ideal beauty — Dürer trying to out-classicise the classics, from first principles. Small
 details reward looking: the serpent, the apple, the animals of Eden hidden in the dark, the little tablet
 bearing his proud signature. It is the perfect human form, captured at the very moment of its loss. Now
 we climb back toward Goya — and into the darkest chapter of Spanish history, a little further on.
