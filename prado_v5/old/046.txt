 Down to the ground floor: Spanish medieval gold
 We leave Velázquez and Goya behind now and travel back in time — down to the ground floor, and
 back four hundred years before the Golden Age, to where European painting begins to stir. These rooms
 hold the oldest works in the museum: gold-ground altarpieces, jewel-like panels, the art of the late
 Middle Ages and the dawn of the Renaissance.

 Spain in the fifteenth century was a crossroads. Its painters absorbed the new oil techniques and
 meticulous realism flooding down from Flanders, fused them with the gold and pattern of medieval
 altarpieces, and produced the brilliant, slightly stiff, intensely detailed style we call Hispano-Flemish.
 Everything here glitters with gold leaf and burns with deep colour; figures are solemn, faces grave,
 every brocade and jewel rendered with loving precision.

 This is sacred art made for altars, meant to overwhelm a worshipper with the splendour of heaven. It
 asks a different kind of looking than the painterly Velázquez upstairs — slower, closer, attentive to
 detail and symbol. Begin with the most magnificent of the Spanish masters of this age, enthroned in
 gold, this way now.
