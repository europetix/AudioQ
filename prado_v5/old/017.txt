 Ribera, The Martyrdom of Saint Philip
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Stand before the large canvas of a near-naked man being hauled upward by ropes tied to his wrists, his body
    stretched against a pale, open sky.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Under a wide, bright sky, executioners haul a muscular, ageing man up onto a wooden cross-beam by ropes
    around his wrists. His arms strain overhead, his body half-lit, his face turned upward. Burly tormentors pull
    and fasten the ropes; a small crowd, including women and a child, looks on at the edges.
    LISTEN FOR
    Notice the sky — Ribera floods this martyrdom with daylight, not gloom. The cruelty happens in the open, in
    full view.

 For a long time this was thought to show Saint Bartholomew being flayed; we now believe it is the
 martyrdom of Saint Philip, hoisted to be crucified. Either way, it is Ribera confronting the thing Spanish
 Counter-Reformation art returned to again and again: the suffering body of the faithful, and what it asks
 of us to look at it.

 What is remarkable is the restraint. Ribera could have given us blood and horror; instead he catches the
 moment before — the straining of ropes, the heave of the executioners' shoulders, the saint's body
 pulled taut but not yet broken. The drama is all in the physical effort and the terrible ordinariness of it:
 men doing a job, hauling on ropes as if raising a sail.

 Look at the saint's body — the realism of an ageing man, the muscles under strain, lit by a clear daylight
 that makes everything visible and inescapable. And look at his face, turned up toward heaven not in
 agony but in a kind of exhausted acceptance. Around the edges, ordinary spectators watch; a mother
 shields a child. Ribera implicates the crowd — and us — in the act of looking.

 That bright, pitiless daylight is the opposite of the candle-lit gloom we usually expect from him, and it
 makes the scene more disturbing, not less. Now we move from Ribera's harsh truth to a painter of
 profound stillness — Zurbarán, just beyond.
