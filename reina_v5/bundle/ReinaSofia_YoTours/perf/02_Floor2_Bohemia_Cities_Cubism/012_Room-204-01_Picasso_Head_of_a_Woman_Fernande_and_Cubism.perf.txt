[curious] In 1912, the Paris dealer Ambroise Vollard sold a cast of this bronze head to a buyer in New York, the photographer Alfred Stieglitz. Stieglitz published photographs of it in the magazine Camera Work. The next year he put it in the Armory Show, the exhibition that brought modern art to America.

Picasso modelled the head in the autumn of 1909. The sitter was Fernande, who inspired a whole series of his Cubist portraits, in paint and in sculpture. He had been looking at objects from Africa, among them Nimba masks, and he found a new way to build a volume.

[pause] Walk slowly round it, if you can. Face, hair and neck are made of swelling forms that lock together. Where you'd expect a hollow, he often pushes the shape outward instead. Watch the neck as you move. It seems to twist. The museum says this head signalled the birth of Cubist sculpture.

Now find a painting with the same title, Woman's Head, Fernande. Here her face is cut into sharp geometric zones, laid out almost like a grid, in browns, greens, greys and blacks. That narrow palette is typical of Analytical Cubism. It keeps your eye on structure, not colour. As one critic put it, a Cubist picture has to be thought as much as seen.

[wry] Not everyone was convinced. French newspapers called Cubism cold, geometric, intellectual, monotonous, and foreign. Writers such as Guillaume Apollinaire defended it, and cheap little magazines carried it across borders. Camera Work was one of them. Some works in these Cubist rooms are on loan from the Telefónica Collection.

So the style Paris mocked as foreign went abroad anyway. Part of it travelled as this bronze head.
