[quietly] A few children stand in front of a house whose walls are full of bullet holes. The address is in the title: 10 Peironcely Street, in the Vallecas district of Madrid. Robert Capa took the picture after an air raid, in the first winter of the war, while this city was under siege.

The Spanish Civil War had more press coverage than any war before it. It became a key moment in the birth of modern photojournalism. Small, high-quality cameras like the Leica allowed a new way of photographing, and it was tested here first. The great war reporters came to Spain, Capa, Gerda Taro and David Seymour, known as Chim. All three supported the Republic.

[pause] Capa and Taro worked on the front line, at great personal risk. Their pictures went out through the international press and became landmarks of the genre.

The most famous is here. The museum's title for it is Death of a Loyalist Militiaman, near Espejo, on the Córdoba front, in early September 1936. A man falls backwards on a bare slope, at the moment he seems to be hit. The American magazine Life published it the following year.

[reverent] Take your time along these walls. Capa followed the war to Bilbao, to Barcelona, and to the farewell of the International Brigades. Next to him, the photographer Juan Miguel Pando Barrero shows the city from inside: people sheltering in the Sevilla metro station, a night bombing in the Plaza de Antón Martín. That square is not far from this museum.

These prints were made after Capa's death, and given to the museum by Cornell Capa. In them, the twentieth century learned to look at war up close.
