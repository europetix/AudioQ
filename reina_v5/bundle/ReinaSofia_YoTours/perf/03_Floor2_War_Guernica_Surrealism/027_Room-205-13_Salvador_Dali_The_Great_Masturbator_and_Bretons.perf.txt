[curious] In the late summer of 1929, a woman made a decision that changed Salvador Dalí's life. Her name was Gala. She was married to the French poet Paul Éluard. And when Éluard went back to Paris, she stayed behind with Dalí in Cadaqués.

After those few days together, Dalí painted this picture. The title is blunt, and so is the subject. Dalí himself wrote about it, and the museum treats it as the central image of his sexual obsessions.

Now look at the great pale head in the middle. Its eyes are closed. The Spanish critic Rafael Santos Torroella saw it as one of several self-portraits in the picture, with Dalí appearing in more than one scene at the same moment. For him, the whole canvas is deeply autobiographical, a record of the change that Gala brought into Dalí's life, in body and in spirit.

[pause] Then find the grasshopper, clinging to the head. Dalí had been terrified of these insects since he was a small child. Here he places his own terror right at the heart of the painting.

All of this belongs to a movement, and this room is built around its leader. In 1924, André Breton published the first Manifesto of Surrealism. He wanted art to come from the inner life, through techniques like automatic writing. Four years later, his book Surrealism and Painting gathered the painters he believed could do the same thing with images. Around you hang works by Miró, Picasso, Magritte, Max Ernst, Yves Tanguy and Picabia.

[wry] For Breton this was never just a style. Paintings like these were meant to unsettle the comfortable middle-class mind. They were tools for a revolution.
