[warmly] "The revolutionary posters were everywhere, flaming from the walls." That's George Orwell, remembering the streets of Barcelona in his book Homage to Catalonia.

During the Civil War, posters, newspapers and magazines were how ideas travelled. Painters, photographers, writers and designers threw themselves into making them. In the Republican zone there were so many, from so many groups, pasted one over another, that the walls themselves changed. Streets and squares became a stage. The room text calls them a "machine-speaker of unrest and propaganda". And at the newsstands, magazines did the same job with bold headlines and avant-garde layouts. It was a war of ideas.

One name keeps coming back on these walls. Josep Renau. You've just seen the man who taught him most, John Heartfield. Renau took the photomontage and made it Spanish. Look for his wide poster with the Catalan title Obrers, camperols, soldats, intel·lectuals. Workers, peasants, soldiers, intellectuals. Four groups, one cause, in one long image. Nearby, his poster for a Soviet film, The Kronstadt Sailors, and Arturo Ballester's poster named for a single date, the nineteenth of July 1936.

[conspiratorial] Now the turn. Renau wasn't only a designer. He was the Republic's director general of Fine Arts. In early 1937, the government asked Picasso to paint a large work for its pavilion at the Paris world's fair. [pause] The museum's account says that commission came to Picasso through Josep Renau.

Renau's own giant photomurals filled most of that pavilion, with maps, graphs and figures about the Republic. So the man behind this poster stands at the start of the story that ends in Room 205.10.
