[curious] Picasso once remarked that a camera could record not the stages of a painting, but its transformation.

In the spring of 1937, that idea was put to the test. Christian Zervos, the editor of the art magazine Cahiers d'Art, asked the photographer Dora Maar to record Guernica as it grew. From the 11th of May until the painting was finished, she came to Picasso's studio on the Rue des Grands-Augustins in Paris and photographed the canvas again and again.

It wasn't easy. The canvas was enormous and the studio was dim. She retouched her prints and made copies from them to bring the image out. The museum holds 28 of these photographs, and some of them are here.

[pause] Look at the earliest ones. Even in rough outline, the cast is already there. The mother with her dead child. The bull. The horse. The fallen soldier. The woman with the lamp. The figure with raised arms. What changes is everything else. Picasso shifts a pose, takes something out, then takes out something more, until the picture becomes clearer. By the end, the original story has faded, and the meaning is shared out among the main figures.

Now the drawings. Picasso began them at the very start of May, and made nearly fifty in under six weeks. Find Man Falling. It's dated the 27th of May, with the big canvas already well under way, so he was still searching. It's small, in pencil and gouache on tracing cloth. Around it are studies of a hand, a horse's head, the bull's head, and a mother carrying her dead child on a ladder.

[warmly] Compare one of the photographs with the painting itself. You're seeing the same painting at two moments, and the distance Picasso travelled between them.
