[warmly] "They say: city. And I reply: countryside." The sculptor Alberto Sánchez said that, and it sums up this whole room.

Most of the avant-garde fell in love with the big city. Alberto and the painter Benjamín Palencia went the other way. They walked out to the edges of south-east Madrid, to the bare hills, the empty lots and the dry, unwatered fields, and made them the ground of a new art.

Their walks crossed the Almodóvar hill in Vicálvaro, which they renamed Cerro Testigo, Witness Hill. Poets joined their outings: Federico García Lorca, Rafael Alberti, Miguel Hernández. And so did the painter Maruja Mallo. People came to call the group the School of Vallecas.

[pause] Palencia had seen the avant-garde first-hand in Paris, and brought Surrealist ideas home. But the Vallecas artists kept their distance from André Breton's rules. For them, Surrealism was a way to look beneath the surface of the land itself. Earth, rock, worn and eroded shapes, things picked up on their walks, all became images full of poetry. The room text calls it a telluric poetics, a poetry of the earth.

Now find Mallo's small painting, Earth and Excrement, from 1932. Think back to her fair in Room 205.06, all bright colour and noise. That was her first manner. In the 1930s her palette turned muted and sombre. The subject here is unsettling, and the paint seems to become earth itself. It belongs to the series Sewers and Bell Towers.

[quietly] Mallo was a member of this circle, close to Alberto and Palencia both as a painter and as a friend. Around her hang works from the circle: Alberto's Maternity, Palencia's compositions, Rodríguez Luna's Birds in the Melon Field.

From a city fair to a handful of dark earth. That's the journey this room asks you to make.
