[curious] For five hundred years, Western art chased one goal: the perfect likeness. Then photography arrived and seemed to settle the matter. A camera sees the way an eye does. So what was left for photographers to do?

This room gives two answers from Spain, in the years after the dictatorship.

The first answer was fiction. In the late seventies, a group of photographers around the magazine Nueva Lente turned against the serious, humane documentary style of the generation before them. They borrowed collage and surrealism. They revived old hand-made techniques. And they staged their pictures like theatre.

[lightly] The room text names one series as the high point of that mood: Ouka Leele's Hairdresser's, from 1979. Look at these small prints closely. They began as black-and-white photographs, and then she coloured them by hand. The colour is not a record of anything. It's pure invention, laid on top of reality.

The second answer stayed with documentary, but changed how it told stories. The curators have a striking line for the reason. Spain had lived through, in their words, "a dictatorship that occurred in black and white."

[quietly] Cristina García Rodero's series Hidden Spain was, for the curators, a major milestone. It's an anthology of Spanish customs, some rooted in an unchanging past, others brand new in the seventies. Find her picture called The King of the Daisies, taken in Viana do Bolo, in Galicia, in 1986.

Around them hang Koldo Chamorro, Manuel Vilariño, Marta Sentís and others. Some invent, some record. Most do a little of both.

[warmly] One woman paints colour onto a photograph. Another keeps to black and white, and finds a country hiding in plain sight.

Room 19 has a short Extra on Perejaume and the landscape. The next main stop is Room 20, 'The African Is at the Center'.
