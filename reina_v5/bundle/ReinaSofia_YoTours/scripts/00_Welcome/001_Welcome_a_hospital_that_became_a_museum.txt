This museum began with a king's wish to gather all of Madrid's hospitals under one roof. Work on a great General Hospital finally began here in the eighteenth century.

The name to remember is Francesco Sabatini, an architect born in Palermo. He gave the hospital its long, grand front. He also thought hard about health: air flowing right through the wards, high ceilings, whitewashed walls. The museum calls these ideas ahead of their time.

Then the money ran out. When Charles the Third died, near the end of the century, building stopped, and only about a third of the plan was ever finished. Even so, it worked as a hospital for nearly two centuries, until it closed some sixty years ago.

Most of the plans for it after that meant knocking it down. Critics fought back, and the old hospital was declared a historic monument. It opened as an art centre in 1986. The two glass and steel lift towers you walked between were added to frame this entrance. And in 1992, Picasso's Guernica came to live here.

Here's how the guide works. Every stop gives you a room number and the room's title, the way the museum names them, so you always know where you are. You begin on this floor, Floor 1, with Richard Serra's steel sculpture in Room 102. Then up to Floor 2, for the avant-gardes of the early twentieth century and Guernica. Floor 3 is closed for now, so you skip it. Floor 4 holds art from 1975 to today, in Rooms 1 to 21. And you finish on the roof terrace of the Nouvel building.

Some stops are marked Extra. They are short and optional. Skip them whenever you like.

One plain fact before you start. The museum is rehanging its whole collection, in a process due to finish in 2028.

First, stay on Floor 1. The garden in the middle of the building, reached from the cloister, has a short Extra. The next main stop is Room 102, with Richard Serra's steel sculpture. If you can't see a sign, ask at the information desk.
