In 1975 an artist cut holes in an old building on a New York pier. The police closed it on the day it opened.

The artist was Gordon Matta-Clark. The building stood on Pier 52, on the Hudson River, a relic of the city's industrial past at a time when the port was emptying out. Matta-Clark made a series of cuts in its roof and walls, sometimes in dangerous conditions. Through the openings came changing patterns of light, and reflections off the river. His film, Day's End, records the whole thing. After the police shut it, the building stood empty until it was pulled down.

This room is about the edges of cities. Every city, the room text says, has areas on its outskirts where working-class leisure mixed with nightlife and sex work. Along the New York docks, in San Francisco before AIDS, and in Madrid's emerging Chueca district, these were also places of cruising, a furtive drift through a territory of desire, where men could meet men. Artists worked there too, with performances, cuts into buildings and documentary photographs.

Now the small black-and-white photographs. David Wojnarowicz was self-taught, a painter, writer and activist, and this is one of his rare series in photography. He had friends wear a mask of the French poet Arthur Rimbaud and photographed them around New York: on the piers, in the subway, in cinemas. Wojnarowicz saw himself in Rimbaud: a violent youth, a refusal of respectable life, being gay, and a wish to live on the margins.

He made these photographs in 1978 and 1979, before the AIDS crisis. The museum calls them the last collective vibration of the Lower East Side's queer art scene before it fell apart.
