Fifty years of art, from 1975 to today, and it begins with one man standing alone with his hands tied.

This is Floor 4, the museum's contemporary collection, open in this form since February 2026. It's laid out as a loop around the building. Rooms 1 to 3, here between the two lifts, are the introduction. Then three routes carry you all the way round. Route I, Rooms 4 to 10, is a history of feelings: feminism, desire, the years of AIDS and heroin, and grief. Route II, Rooms 11 to 14, along the back of the building, is a gallery of sculpture. Route III, Rooms 15 to 21, coming back down the far side, is about the museum itself, the market, video and photography.

Behind these first rooms is the Alacena, the Pantry. It's a long wooden structure, about 29 metres, built from discarded boards by the designer Jorge Penadés, as a place to stop, sit and talk. You'll also find portable chairs around the floor.

Now the painting. Juan Genovés made it in 1975, the year Franco died. It belongs to a series of hyperrealistic figures on bare white grounds. The man is alone. His hands are bound and his eyes are lowered. He could be facing a summary trial, an interrogation, or worse. He stands for the defenceless citizen facing the machinery of the state.

Genovés wanted these paintings to work like press photographs, as evidence. Even the title, Document number, with the number left open, sounds like a file.

The rest of the room shows how artists answered the last years of the dictatorship and the years after it. Concha Jerez crosses out the news itself. Picasso's etchings recall a dark episode, when a series of his prints was destroyed at a Madrid gallery. And the room makes one more point. This museum was founded on the return of Guernica to Spain.

Next door, Room 2, 'Material Counterculture', holds the painted boards that once covered the door of a Madrid bar called La Vaquería.
