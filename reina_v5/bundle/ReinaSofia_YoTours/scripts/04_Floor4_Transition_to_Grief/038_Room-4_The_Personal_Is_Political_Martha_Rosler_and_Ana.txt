A woman stands at a kitchen counter, facing a fixed camera. She picks up a utensil, says its name, and shows you how to use it. Except she doesn't use it the way any cookbook would.

This is Martha Rosler's Semiotics of the Kitchen, from 1975. It looks like a television cooking demonstration, and it's a parody of one. Rosler called it an anti-Julia Child, one that replaces the domestic meaning of the tools with "a lexicon of rage and frustration". Ordinary kitchen routines turn into outbursts of anger. Six minutes, and the kitchen stops being a cosy place. In Rosler's words, when the woman speaks, she names her own oppression.

That's the spirit of this room. Its title says it all: the personal is political. From the late 1960s, the room explains, second-wave feminism gave rise to body art and performance, and it put women at the centre of art as political subjects. In 1969 the Stonewall riots in New York made the fight for LGBTIQ+ rights visible too. Identity stopped being fixed. So did the meaning of words.

Now find Ana Mendieta's film, only a minute and twenty seconds long. Mendieta was born in Cuba and worked between North American and Latin cultures. Here, trails of blood run down her forearms, and when the action ends, only the traces remain, like writing. The work has a sacrificial quality. Some of her blood actions were filmed at home, to speak about violence that happens in private.

There's much more here. Hannah Wilke's Hello Boys, Eulàlia Grau's Discrimination of Women, photographs by Nan Goldin and self-portraits by Robert Mapplethorpe. Take your time. This is one of the fullest rooms on the floor.
