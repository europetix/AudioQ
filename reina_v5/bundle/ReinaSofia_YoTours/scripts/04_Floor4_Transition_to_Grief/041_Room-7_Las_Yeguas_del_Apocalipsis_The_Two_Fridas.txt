In 1963, a photographer went down into the catacombs of Palermo, in Sicily, and photographed the dead.

His name was Peter Hujar. The bodies he found lay mummified along the corridors of an ossuary, and he photographed them as real portraits. His look is calm and close, far from morbid. In 1987, Hujar himself died of complications associated with AIDS. In between, he turned that same intimate gaze on friends who were ill and dying.

That's the thread of this small room. The AIDS pandemic emerged in the mid-eighties and peaked worldwide a decade later. The room argues that the art it produced was not loud or expressionist. It was conceptual, and it worked through what is missing. To say something, here, is not to express it but to point out its absence.

Which brings us to the largest photograph in the room. Las Yeguas del Apocalipsis, the Mares of the Apocalypse, were two artists from Santiago de Chile, Pedro Lemebel and Francisco Casas. The photograph records an action of theirs from 1989, The Two Fridas, a title that calls up Frida Kahlo's famous double self-portrait. It was taken by the photographer Pedro Marinello Kairath.

And now the room's last idea. The room text says the intimacy of these works doesn't sit inside them. Each of us brings it. Look, too, at Feliciano Centurión's Jellyfish and José Leonilson's Safer Sex Guidelines.
