"The end of the world is a fantasy, but death is still real." Those words, by the writer Hannah Black, close the text of this room, the last of Route I.

The room begins with a date that changed the century. Its text says the twenty-first century began with a terrorist attack, in New York in 2001. In Spain, the Madrid attack of the eleventh of March 2004 reopened that anguish. Since then, the room argues, we have learned a new kind of grief, for people we never knew. And we have learned how fiction, in the form of false news, can have very real effects.

At the centre is a work from much earlier. Juan Muñoz made The Wasteland in 1986. It was his first floor piece. He named it partly after T. S. Eliot's poem, because, as he put it, the floor is barren.

Look at the floor first. It's linoleum, laid as an illusion. Muñoz based the pattern on an image of an ancient mosaic from Antioch, and the unsettling sense of space comes from Renaissance perspective. He wanted it to be something real, a floor people could walk across.

Then find the dummy. It's a ventriloquist's figure, cast in bronze. The text puts it simply. It stands in front of the show and waits, speechless. A ventriloquist's dummy only speaks when someone gives it a voice. This one never will. It's made of metal. And so the piece becomes a kind of funeral monument to representation itself.

That's the triumph of fiction the title of the room refers to. Not a hope, but a condemnation.
