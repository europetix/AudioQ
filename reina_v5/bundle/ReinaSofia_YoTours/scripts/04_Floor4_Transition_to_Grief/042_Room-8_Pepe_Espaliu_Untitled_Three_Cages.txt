Each of these cages is more than seven metres long, and only about half a metre wide. Just wide enough for a body. And all three are empty.

Pepe Espaliú was born in Córdoba. From the mid-eighties he built a refined, very personal visual language, drawing on psychoanalysis and conceptual art, in which his own body became political ground. He did this at a time when being gay, and living with AIDS, were things Spain preferred not to talk about. He made these cages in 1992. He died the following year.

Look at how they're made. Black iron wire, welded, and you can see straight through it. The museum describes the body here as an empty space, a fragile container, a boundary that can change. The structure is completely transparent. The meaning is not. That tension is the work.

The room text is direct about the context. In Spain, AIDS was linked to many factors, but the spread of needles was devastating. And the illness was never only individual. It was social. Espaliú became known for speaking out against the isolation of people with HIV. So yes, the cages recall that confinement.

But the room asks you to see something else in them as well. His last years were marked by illness and isolation, and also by the urgency of shared care. The cages, the room says, hold a proliferation of affection and care. They're about needing other people.
