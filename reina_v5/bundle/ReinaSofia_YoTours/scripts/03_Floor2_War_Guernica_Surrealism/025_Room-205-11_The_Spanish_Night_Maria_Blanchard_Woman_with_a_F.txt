Long before the avant-garde, the world already had a picture of Spain. Romantic imagery had fixed it in the early nineteenth century: songs and dances, Holy Week, bullfights. By the end of that century, flamenco had become the very image of popular culture. And the modern artists loved it.

This room takes its name from an exhibition held here in 2007, the first to look at flamenco inside avant-garde art. Think of the Spanish guitar, hanging in cafés and studios. The Cubists adored it, a hollow object that seems to come alive when you strum it. Think of the dancers, whose dresses, mantillas and fans make the body look possessed by movement.

Now find María Blanchard's Woman with a Fan. You may remember Blanchard from Room 204.02. She learned Cubism first-hand, in Paris, and this is one of the most ambitious paintings of her early years. The fan ties it to an old Spanish tradition. The woman is probably a dancer, and Blanchard splits her into overlapping planes of colour, so that she seems to move in several directions at once. The room text hears echoes of the Futurists and of Marcel Duchamp.

In 1915 she showed it in Madrid, at the first exhibition of Cubist painting the city had seen. The critics were mixed, to put it politely. That cool reception was one of the reasons she left Spain that same year and settled in Paris for good.

Around her, the Spanish night goes on. Man Ray photographed the flamenco dancer Vicente Escudero. Natalia Goncharova drew Spanish costumes. Miró painted a Spanish dancer, and the young Dalí a Roma man from his home town, Figueres.
