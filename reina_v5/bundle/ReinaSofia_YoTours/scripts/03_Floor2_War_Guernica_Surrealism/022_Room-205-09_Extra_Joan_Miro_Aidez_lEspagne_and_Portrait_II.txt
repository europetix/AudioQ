Miró's biggest work for the Paris pavilion has vanished. It was a huge mural, known as The Reaper, or The Catalan Peasant in Revolt, and no one knows where it is today.

It came at the end of the most radical decade of his career, when Miró tried strange materials and twisted his figures almost out of recognition. He said he wanted to assassinate painting.

What you can see from that year, 1937, is this small stencil print. It's barely bigger than a sheet of notepaper. A figure raises an enormous fist, with the words Aidez l'Espagne. Help Spain.

Now look at the large canvas, Portrait II. Miró dated it to the very day, the eleventh of June 1938. The war was still going on. But the figure has become almost a sign. Two rounded shapes make the body and the head, and a few small marks suggest a face.

The colour sits in broad, calm areas. The museum's text finds it less anguished than the paintings before it. It looks forward to the more lyrical pictures known as the Constellations, which he began after leaving Paris under the Nazi threat.

One small cry for help, and one large, quiet portrait. Between them, a missing wall.
