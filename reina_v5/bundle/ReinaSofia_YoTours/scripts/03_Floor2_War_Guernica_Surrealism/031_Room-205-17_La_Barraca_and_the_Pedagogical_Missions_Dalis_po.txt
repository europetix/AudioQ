In the early 1930s, a team might arrive in a Spanish village with a film projector, a puppet show, a mobile library and copies of paintings from the Prado. Often, it was the first time anything like it had come.

That was the idea of the Pedagogical Missions. They began in 1931, under the Second Republic, led by the educator Manuel Bartolomé Cossío. In just under five years they reached almost seven thousand towns and villages.

A year later came a second project. La Barraca was a travelling university theatre, directed by the poet Federico García Lorca and Eduardo Ugarte. Students from every discipline took the great Spanish classics, Cervantes, Calderón, Lope de Vega, to small towns, in modern stagings. The Civil War brought it to an end. Look for Benjamín Palencia's designs for La Barraca's sets, and its poster with a mask. The figures called Lady and Gentleman in Green belong to the Missions.

Behind both lay the reforming ideas of the Free Institution of Education, which had also given Madrid the Residencia de Estudiantes. It was a student residence and a real meeting place for new ideas from abroad. Among those who lived there were Lorca, Salvador Dalí and Luis Buñuel.

Now find Dalí's portrait of his friend Buñuel, painted in 1924. Dalí, Buñuel, Lorca and Pepín Bello formed a tight circle at the Residencia. Dalí and Buñuel would go on to write the scripts of two famous Surrealist films, Un chien andalou and L'âge d'or.

Here, though, Dalí is in a classical mood. Buñuel's head and shoulders stand out hard and solid, like blocks, against a landscape of tiny buildings. And the clouds behind him come from an old master. Dalí had seen Mantegna's Death of the Virgin at the Prado, and borrowed its clouds.

A young man painting his friend, long before either of them was famous.
