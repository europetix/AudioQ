"The act of looking is always equivalent to combat." Carl Einstein wrote that, and he meant it.

He was a poet, critic and theorist of the avant-garde. He believed that finding new ways of seeing carried a political responsibility. He had no time for the idea of the lone genius. And in the Spanish Civil War, he fought with the Durruti Column. Look for Agustí Centelles's photographs here of Buenaventura Durruti leading volunteers to the Aragón front, and of Durruti's burial. At the burial of an anarchist leader killed in battle, Einstein gave a historic speech. The old, single "I", he said, had given way to a "collective syntax".

He looked at African sculpture the same way, as something shared, social and political. Some of the masks he collected are in this room, Yoruba, Senufo, Baule and Punu. He thought they did what Cubism did on canvas, layering different times and spaces in a single form. Beside them hang artists he wrote about, Picasso and Juan Gris among them.

Now find Miró's Pastorale. You may remember his House with Palm Tree, back in Room 202.03, painted in fine detail. Here, only a few years later, all that detail is going. In a studio on the Rue Blomet in Paris, which he shared with André Masson, Miró was working toward what the two of them called painting-poetry. The country theme is still there. But the things have turned into signs. This is where the language you know as Miró's begins.

That's the end of Floor 2, a journey from Madrid's bohemia, through Guernica, to a single page of signs.
