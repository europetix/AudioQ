Thirty-one sculptures, thirty years of work, one table, and not a single weld holding any of them in place.

This is the start of Route II, a gallery of sculpture along the back of the building. After the grief of the last rooms, it's about touch, weight, and how objects and bodies meet.

The table is by Juan Navarro Baldeweg, a painter and an architect. In 1974 he went to study at MIT, in the United States, under György Kepes. That stay set off the sculptures and installations he became known for. Over the following three decades he kept making small pieces for a series called Gravity and Balance. Here thirty-one of them sit together, on more than nine metres of pine, like a retrospective you can read from end to end.

Walk slowly along it. Brass, bronze, rope, lead, marble, brick, even gold leaf. Nothing is welded. Every piece holds its place by balance and counterbalance alone. His real subject is the pull of gravity itself.

The rest of the room tells a larger story. In the late seventies, sculpture took in the geometry of minimalism and broke works into small parts, rearranged into new patterns. In 1980, an exhibition at the Guggenheim in New York called New Images from Spain gave this new Spanish sculpture international attention. Susana Solano was in it. Her piece here is called Take It Easy, with Emergency Ladder. Cristina Iglesias belongs to the same moment, alongside the British sculptors Anthony Caro and Tony Cragg.

For a while, Spanish and British sculptors seemed to be speaking the same language. The room calls it a dream of simultaneity. In the following decade, it slipped out of reach.
