On a February day in 1982, a bride set off through Madrid with her bridesmaids. Her name was Paz Muro, and she was on her way to marry contemporary art.

It was the first ARCO, the big new art fair, held at the fairgrounds on the Paseo de la Castellana. Muro followed the script of a real wedding. She walked in procession from her home. On arrival, a wedding march played from a player built into her dress. Then fellow artists and visitors painted all over her and her clothes. The marriage, as the museum puts it, was consummated.

It was a joke with a point. Muro was mocking a commercial art world in love with one kind of new painting, the Transavantgarde. The photographs and invitations here record the whole day.

In the same room, the artist Tino Calabuig filmed that same first fair, and not kindly. He interviewed everyone, from Chillida to the Minister for Culture. Even Maruja Mallo, whose paintings you met on Floor 2.

That's what this room is about. The curators call it a tentative self-portrait of the Reina Sofía. It traces how Spain built an art scene over the last fifty years. Look for the posters for the Spring Festival of the old Spanish Museum of Contemporary Art, whose collection later passed to this museum.

And look for something very small. It's a piece of gold, about five centimetres across, cast by Eduardo Chillida in 1986 and titled simply Reina Sofía. The Ministry of Culture had asked him for a symbol for the new Reina Sofía Art Centre. His design was cast into several gold medals, and one was given to Queen Sofía when the centre opened.

A museum that hangs its own story on the wall is inviting you to judge it. That takes some nerve.

Room 16 has a short Extra on video art of the 1980s, with Chillida's original drawing for that symbol. The next main stop is Room 17, 'A More Painted Painting'.
