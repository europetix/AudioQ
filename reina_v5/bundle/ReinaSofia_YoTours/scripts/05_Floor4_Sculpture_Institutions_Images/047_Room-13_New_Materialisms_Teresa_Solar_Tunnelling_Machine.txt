What would a machine look like if it were grown instead of built? Teresa Solar, an artist from Madrid, gives one answer in this room.

Her Tunnelling Machine dates from 2022. The label is almost bare. A clay body, two limbs of porex and resin, a steel frame, a coat of varnish. More than three metres from end to end, like something that has wandered in and stopped.

The room is called New Materialisms. Its text begins in the 1990s, when artists turned their radical imagination on materials themselves. Soft parts, "insubordinate bodies", strange geometries. Painting and sculpture were taken apart until the results escaped every label.

Then comes a second wave, much closer to now. The curators speak of a way of thinking that no longer puts human beings at the centre. Some of these works picture what might be left of us after extinction. Others imagine creatures that mix up the natural kingdoms and the categories of science.

That's where this machine belongs. A tunnel boring machine is about as industrial as objects get. Solar made hers mostly out of clay, the very earth such a machine would dig through. So is it the digger, or the ground?

Take your time with it. See where the clay meets the resin of the limbs, and where the steel quietly holds everything up. Does it read as a vehicle, an animal, or something in between? The room text mentions totems, magic and science fiction. All three fit.

Around it, June Crespo, Itziar Okariz and Sergio Prego push the same questions.

A machine for making holes, made of earth. It doesn't dig. It waits for you to finish the thought.

Room 14 has a short Extra on Ángela de la Cruz and a canvas tossed under a chair. Then the route turns the corner and runs down the left side of the building to Room 15, 'Institutional Genealogy'.
