The title of this room is not a curator's phrase. "The African is at the center" is a slogan used by the communities who organise Conciencia Afro, a festival here in Madrid. It asks for visibility, recognition and an end to racism, for the African diaspora and the Black communities of Spain.

The room brings together artists who reflect on that identity, on the Black body as a political body, and on the presence left by centuries of colonisation. In Western countries this is a minority, the text says, yet its cultures are celebrated in places such as Cuba, Brazil and the United States.

Start with the large print by Belkis Ayón. She was a Cuban printmaker from Havana, and in the nineties she worked in black and white. Her starting point was the Abakuá, an Afro-Cuban secret society open only to men. From its myths she took the figure of Sikán, a woman sacrificed by the men of her own community. Ayón saw Sikán as her alter ego.

This print is called Perfidia, Treachery, and dates from 1998. Knowing Sikán's story, it's hard not to think of her as you look. Ayón died the following year, in her early thirties.

Then turn to Julie Mehretu's canvas from 2023, painted in ink and acrylic. Where Ayón tells a myth, Mehretu builds with marks and layers, and leaves you to find your own way in.

Around them, Juan Roberto Diago's self-portrait is made on jute cloth. Agnes Essonti's photo-essay is called A Journey. And the room text gives Pocho Guimaraes the credit of a pioneering voice.

The slogan puts the African at the centre. For a few minutes, this room does exactly that.

Last room on this floor: Room 21, 'Gender Practices', next to the lift. Look for Catherine Opie's colour portraits, each sitter facing straight out.
