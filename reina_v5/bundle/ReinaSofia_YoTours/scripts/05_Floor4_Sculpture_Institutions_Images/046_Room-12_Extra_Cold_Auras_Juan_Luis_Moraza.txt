Two thousand, four hundred and ninety-six heels. Not shoes. Just the heels.

Juan Luis Moraza made them in resin in 1994 and set them out in a grid on the floor. They run from the flat little heels of children's shoes to tall, thin stilettos, so the surface rises and falls like a landscape.

The title, Ecstasy, Status, Statue, plays on the roots of the word statue, where ecstasy, status and stature meet. The piece is a tribute to Bernini. A heel, after all, is a tiny pedestal. It lifts a body up, the way a plinth lifts a statue. And pedestals, in public squares, are symbols of power. So this floor asks what a sculpture is, and who gets raised up on one.

The room takes its name from a book. In the late 1980s José Luis Brea set out to bring back kinds of art that painting's triumph had pushed out of sight. In 1991 he published Las auras frías, Cold Auras. He described a clear cooling of the hot aura of expressionism. And he saw the new Neo-Baroque art as a work of grieving in response to AIDS. Pepe Espaliú's Saints are in this room too, a few rooms after his cages.
