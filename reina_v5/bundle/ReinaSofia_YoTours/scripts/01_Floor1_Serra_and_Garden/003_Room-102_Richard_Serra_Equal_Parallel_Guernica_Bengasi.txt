The title of this sculpture names two air raids, almost half a century apart. Guernica, the Basque town bombed by the Condor Legion in April 1937. And Benghazi, the Libyan city attacked by the United States Air Force in April 1986, while Serra was making this work. That raid was a reprisal for a bomb in a Berlin discotheque, blamed on Libyan agents.

It's an unusual title for Richard Serra. He generally said his works don't refer to particular people, places or events. Here he set two moments side by side, as a parallel, on the premise that they are equal.

The sculpture belongs to this building. Serra made it for the exhibition that opened the Reina Sofía art centre, and it entered the collection two years later.

Now look at what's actually in front of you. Four solid blocks of weathering steel. Two are square and two are long, and all have the same depth. Each one is about one and a half metres high, the height of the windowsills in this building. Serra called them equal elevations. They alternate along the floor, and in the middle he left an empty space about the size of a block.

Take a slow walk past them. Above you is a smooth barrel vault, full of white light. The steel sharpens the symmetry of the old hospital architecture, and you start to feel the room as much as see it. The empty gap is part of the work too.

Before you go, look for two smaller blocks of forged steel in the same room. That's Inversion, made fifteen years later.

Two bombings, four blocks, and a space between them that nothing fills. You'll meet the first of those bombings again upstairs, in Picasso's Guernica.
