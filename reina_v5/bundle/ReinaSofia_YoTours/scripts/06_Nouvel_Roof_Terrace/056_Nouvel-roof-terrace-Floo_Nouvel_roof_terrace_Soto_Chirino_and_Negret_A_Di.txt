"There are no longer spectators. There are only participants." Jesús Soto said that about his work in the early eighties, and up here, in the open air, you can test it.

This terrace display is called A Different Order. The curators tell the story of geometric sculpture from the sixties to the eighties. It was a sculpture that needed your body to move around it before it could work. Soon its clean forms spread into city squares and corporate plazas, beside the mirrored glass of new office towers. And then, the curators say, it hardened into a cliché that hid what these sculptors had really achieved.

Start with Soto's Penetrable. Soto was one of the key Latin American artists of kinetic art, and in the late sixties he began a series of spaces like this one: frames hung with flexible tubes, made to be walked through. For him, empty space was not nothing. It was matter in another state, like a jelly you could push aside. Follow the staff's guidance on whether you can step inside today.

Martín Chirino's long steel form is from 1971. He was one of the founders of the El Paso group, with Antonio Saura and Manolo Millares. Here, hollow volumes rise and fall like a wave. The title, Mediterranean, comes from his journeys to Italy and Greece. Its polished finish has a watery shine the museum links to his native Canary Islands, and the whole thing seems to float, defying gravity.

Last, Edgar Negret's Red Watchman. Ten painted columns, each one turned a little further than the one before, as if the figure were swinging round to look at you. A giant version has guarded the presidential palace in Bogotá since 1978. Negret was thinking of Gaudí, and of the warrior-like chimneys on the roof of the Casa Milà in Barcelona. On a rooftop, he's right at home.
