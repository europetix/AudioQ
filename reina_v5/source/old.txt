 How to use this guide
 This is a walk-through audio guide to the Museo Reina Sofía, Spain’s national museum of
 twentieth-century and contemporary art. It follows the collection from the avant-garde and Picasso’s
 Guernica on Floor 2, down to the post-war art of Floor 1, and up to the contemporary collection on
 Floor 4. The guide is built around two kinds of tracks. Knowing the difference will let you move at your
 own pace.


 Room introductions
 Most tracks are short room introductions. Play one as you walk into a new room: it tells you what's
 there, what to look at first, and which works have their own dedicated track. In the header these are
 marked R-INTRO. They usually run between one and a half and two and a half minutes.

 Single-work tracks
 The rest focus on a single painting or sculpture. Play one when you are standing in front of the work it
 describes; in the header these are marked W-FOCUS. They usually run between two and three minutes.

 Numbers and room signs
 Every track carries a number — 001, 002, and so on — in the order you walk the tour, and the audio
 files are numbered to match, so you can simply play them in sequence. Where a room number is known
 it is printed at the top of the track; on Floor 2 these match the gallery’s wall signs. Floors 1 and 4 are
 mid-rehang (a reinstallation runs to 2028), so there the guide places you by theme rather than by door
 number — trust the work in front of you.

 Pauses and movement
 Each track ends with a short silent pause built into the audio. That's your moment to look, photograph if
 you want to, and walk to the next stop. When you reach a new room, play the next track. When you're
 standing in front of a marquee work, play its dedicated track. The audio always tells you what to play
 next at the end of each track. You don't have to remember anything.

 On honesty and change
 Museums move things — and the Reina Sofía more than most right now. The collection is in the middle
 of a major reinstallation that runs to 2028, so rooms shift, especially on Floors 1 and 4, and works go off
 for loan or restoration. The tour names what the museum itself associates with each part of the
 collection and guides you by sight, but check the live notices at museoreinasofia.es before you go, in
 case a particular work has moved or is travelling.

 The visit, in shape
 T00 at the entrance → Floor 2 for the new century and Cubism, the Surrealists, the 1937 Pavilion and
 Guernica, and the Civil War → down to Floor 1 for the post-war years, the El Paso painters and Basque
 sculptors, the return of figuration and the art of the Transition → up to Floor 4 for the contemporary
 collection and the rooftop → T-LAST on the way out. It runs a little over two hours at an unhurried
 pace.

 Welcome to the Reina Sofía
 Welcome to the Museo Reina Sofía — Spain's national museum of twentieth-century art, and the home
 of the most famous painting the country has ever produced. You're standing in what was, for two
 hundred years, a hospital. The long neoclassical block with the deep courtyard is the old San Carlos
 Hospital, finished in the eighteenth century by Francesco Sabatini, the architect Charles the Third
 brought from Italy; the patients only moved out in the nineteen-sixties. It reopened as an art centre in
 1986, and in 1992 the King and the Queen the museum is named for inaugurated the permanent
 collection. The glass-and-steel wings with the red lifts came later — the architect Jean Nouvel wrapped
 a modern building around the old one in 2005.

 Here's the shape of what's inside, because it helps to know it before you climb. This is the story of
 Spain's most turbulent century, told in paint and iron. It begins upstairs on Floor 2 with the great
 avant-garde — Cubism, Miró, Dalí, the dream of a new art for a new world — and it walks you straight
 into the catastrophe that broke that dream: the Civil War, and Picasso's Guernica. From there the
 collection carries you through the long grey years of the dictatorship and out the other side, into
 democracy and the open, restless art of right now.

 Two things to know. The museum hangs its collection by theme, not by artist — so Picasso, Miró and
 Dalí turn up in several rooms, not one each. And the whole collection is being re-hung in a project that
 runs to 2028, so a few rooms may have shifted since this guide was made. Don't worry about the
 numbers. I'll keep you oriented by what's in front of you. Take the lift to Floor 2, and let's begin.

 The Second Floor: Spain enters the modern age
 This is the floor most people come for, and it holds the heart of the collection. Everything up here was
 made roughly between 1900 and the end of the Second World War — the decades when Spanish artists
 tore up the rulebook and then watched their country tear itself apart. The rooms run in a loop, and
 they're hung by theme rather than by name, so you'll meet Picasso, Miró and Dalí more than once as the
 story moves.

 It opens quietly, with a young Picasso still finding his feet. Then it gathers speed: Cubism, the language
 he and Juan Gris built out of broken planes; the Surrealists and their dreams; and then, at the centre of
 the floor, the room everything has been leading toward — the Spanish Pavilion of 1937 and Guernica.
 After that come the rooms of the Civil War itself.

 A word on the light. It's kept low up here, and the rooms are cool — a lot of this work is on paper, and
 paper is fragile. Bring a layer if you feel the chill. Take your time; this is not a floor to rush. Just along
 here, in the first rooms, is where it begins.

 Picasso, Woman in Blue
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In the opening rooms of Floor 2, among the earliest works on the floor — a tall portrait of a woman in an
    enormous ruffled blue dress, her face powdered pale, her eyes fixed somewhere past you.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    This is Mujer en azul — Woman in Blue — and Picasso painted it in Madrid in 1901, when he was nineteen.
    He'd taken a studio in the city and was running the art journal Arte Joven, mixing with the writers of the
    so-called Generation of '98. You can see him trying on influences like coats: the flash of Toulouse-Lautrec in
    the Parisian showgirl pose, Van Gogh in the colour, and — underneath it all — the long shadow of the
    Spanish masters down the road at the Prado, El Greco's nervy brushwork and Velázquez's court portraits.
    LISTEN FOR
    Here's what makes her worth stopping for. Picasso sent this painting to the big National Exhibition of Fine
    Arts in 1901. It won nothing but a polite honourable mention, and he was so unbothered — already on his
    way to Paris and his real career — that when the show closed, he simply didn't come back to collect it. The
    painting vanished into storage for decades. It was only rescued years later by an art historian who recognised
    what it was, and it passed into the national collection, which is how it ended up here. So you're looking at a
    masterpiece its own maker walked away from at nineteen — the first chapter of the most influential career in
    modern art, left behind in a Madrid storeroom. When you've had your fill of her, the route carries on into the
    Cubist rooms, a few steps on.

 José Gutiérrez Solana, The Gathering at the Café de
 Pombo
    WHERE YOU ARE
    A large, sombre group portrait in the early rooms — a dozen men in dark coats crowded around a marble café
    table under a hanging lamp, one of them standing at the centre as if presiding.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    La tertulia del Café de Pombo, painted by José Gutiérrez Solana in 1920. A tertulia is a regular gathering for
    talk, and the most famous in Madrid met on Saturday nights at the Café de Pombo, near the Puerta del Sol,
    hosted by the writer Ramón Gómez de la Serna — the figure standing at the centre, holding court. Around
    him Solana painted the writers and artists of the avant-garde of the day, himself among them, off to one side.
    LISTEN FOR
    Solana belonged to what's often called España negra — black Spain — and you can feel it in the palette: the
    browns and bruised greys, the slightly cadaverous faces, the gloom that hangs over even this convivial scene.
    It's a portrait of a whole intellectual world that the coming century would scatter. Cafés like this were the real
    engines of modern Spanish culture, where the painters and poets you'll meet upstairs argued art into being
    over coffee and brandy. Within twenty years most of these gatherings would be broken up by war and exile.
    Solana catches them at the table, mid-conversation, before the lights went out. The Cubist rooms are just
    ahead.

 Joan Miró, House with the Palm Tree
    WHERE YOU ARE
    An early Miró, and it will surprise you if you only know the later star-and-squiggle work — a crisp, sunlit
    Catalan farmhouse, every tile and furrow picked out in almost obsessive detail.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    La casa de la palmera — House with the Palm Tree — from 1918, when Miró was twenty-five and had not
    yet become "Miró." This is the family farm at Mont-roig, in the countryside south of Barcelona, the place he
    loved more than anywhere on earth. He painted it in what's called his detallista — detailist — manner, giving
    equal, loving attention to every blade and rooftile, part Catalan folk art, part the new flat colour he'd picked
    up from the Paris avant-garde.
    LISTEN FOR
    Hold this in your memory and then go and look at his 1934 painting a few rooms on, the one that's just signs
    floating on colour. The distance between them is the whole story of how a modern artist is made. Everything
    in his late work — the love of the Catalan earth, the bright unmixed colour, the sense that a few marks can
    hold a whole world — is already here, just spelled out in full rather than in shorthand. He spent the rest of his
    life learning what to leave out. This is the "before." When you're ready, the Cubists are a little further on.

 Cubism, and the art of breaking things open
 These rooms hold the movement that changed everything. Cubism was invented in Paris, between
 roughly 1907 and 1914, by Picasso and Georges Braque — two painters who decided you could show
 an object from several angles at once, shattering it into planes and reassembling it on a flat canvas. It
 sounds like a parlour trick. It was actually the moment Western art stopped trying to copy the world and
 started rebuilding it.

 The room's other great name is Juan Gris — the third Cubist, a fellow Spaniard in Paris, whose
 paintings have a cool, crystalline order the others' don't; where Picasso is restless, Gris is exact, almost
 architectural. And keep an eye out for the iron. Julio González was a Catalan metalworker who taught
 Picasso how to weld, and then turned welding into sculpture — line drawn directly in steel, with the air
 left showing through. It's a silent revolution sitting in the middle of the room.

 The collection threads these figures through several rooms, so you'll see more of them as you go. When
 you're ready, the dream-rooms of the Surrealists are next, right along here.

 Juan Gris, Portrait of Josette
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In the Cubist rooms — a portrait of a seated woman built from clean, interlocking planes, far more ordered
    and legible than the Picassos nearby.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Retrato de Josette, painted by Juan Gris in 1916. Josette was his companion, and this is the most important
    portrait of his career. Gris was the third great Cubist — a Madrid-born painter who followed Picasso to Paris
    and to the same ramshackle studio building — but his Cubism has a character all its own: where Picasso and
    Braque shattered things, Gris assembled them, with the cool precision of a man who'd trained as a
    draughtsman. He called his method the opposite of theirs — they worked from the object toward abstraction;
    he worked from abstraction back toward the object.
    LISTEN FOR
    Notice how readable she is. The planes lock together like inlaid wood; the colours are sober and architectural;
    there's a calm, almost classical balance to the whole thing. Gris brought order to a movement built on
    disorder, and for that the others slightly looked down on him — and slightly envied him. He died young, at
    forty, and the Reina Sofía holds several of his finest works, so look around for more. This crystalline clarity is
    the other face of Cubism, the one that doesn't get put on the posters. The Surrealist rooms are next, through
    here.

 María Blanchard, Cubist composition
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In the Cubist rooms, near the Gris — a figure built from faceted planes, but warmer and more tender than the
    men's Cubism around it, the geometry never quite extinguishing the human being inside.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    María Blanchard, a Cubist of the first rank who has only lately been given her due. Born in Santander in
    1881, she lived with a severe physical disability and the cruelty it drew, and she went to Paris and threw
    herself into Cubism alongside her close friend Juan Gris, Diego Rivera and the sculptor Lipchitz. The Reina
    Sofía holds her work from the high Cubist years around 1916.
    LISTEN FOR
    Notice what she does that the others resist — she lets feeling back in. The planes are rigorous, the structure
    real, but there's a melancholy, an inwardness, that's all her own; soon after this she left strict Cubism behind
    for tender, sombre paintings of mothers, children and the poor. She belongs with Maruja Mallo and Ángeles
    Santos upstairs as one of the women this museum is working to move back to the centre of the story. The
    fractured still lifes of pure Cubism are right here.

 Pablo Picasso, Cubist still life
    WHERE YOU ARE
    A small, dense Cubist canvas in these rooms — a tabletop of objects shattered into brown and grey facets, a
    guitar or bottle or newspaper just legible inside the fracture.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    An early Picasso from around 1912, the years when he and Braque were inventing Cubism together in Paris,
    "roped together like mountaineers," as Braque later put it. The Reina Sofía's early Picassos let you set the
    teenage realist of Woman in Blue downstairs against the revolutionary of a decade later — the same man,
    having blown the rules apart.
    LISTEN FOR
    This is the laboratory phase. They'd taken a single object, walked around it in their minds, and laid every
    viewpoint flat on the one surface at once — top, side, front, all together — so the painting stops being a
    window and becomes a thing in its own right. Look for the scraps of lettering and the hint of an everyday
    object; that's the handhold they left you. It's drab on purpose: colour would only distract from the structural
    revolution. Juan Gris took this language and made it sing; the sculptor Julio González made it stand up in iron
    — just beyond.

 Julio González, iron
    WHERE YOU ARE
    A spare, open sculpture of welded iron — not a solid mass but a few rods and planes drawing a figure or a
    head in the air, with as much empty space as metal.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Julio González, the Catalan who, more than anyone, invented modern iron sculpture. His family were
    metalworkers in Barcelona; he learned to forge and weld as a trade, moved to Paris, and in the late 1920s
    made a historic alliance — he taught his friend Picasso how to weld, and together they opened up a whole
    new way of making sculpture: not carving or casting, but drawing directly in steel.
    LISTEN FOR
    That's the phrase to carry — "drawing in space." Instead of a solid body, González gives you the lines of one,
    with the air left showing through, so the empty space becomes part of the figure. It looks effortless and
    modern; it rests on a lifetime of real blacksmith's skill. Everything that follows on the floor above —
    Chillida's forged iron, Oteiza's open boxes — descends from this silent revolution in a Paris workshop. His
    countryman Gargallo pushed metal another way, round this corner.

 Pablo Gargallo, metal mask
    WHERE YOU ARE
    A sculpture in sheet metal or bronze — often a head or mask — that turns solidity inside out, building a face
    as much from the hollows and gaps as from the metal itself.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Pablo Gargallo, an Aragonese sculptor and a contemporary of González, working the same revolutionary
    seam: metal as line and void rather than mass. He's famous for masks and heads in hammered copper, lead
    and iron, in which a curl of metal stands in for a lock of hair and an empty socket does the work of an eye.
    LISTEN FOR
    The trick that delights everyone is the way absence becomes presence — a hole reads as an eye, a gap as a
    cheekbone, so your mind completes the face out of nothing. Gargallo kept one foot in classical elegance even
    as he embraced industrial materials, which gives his work a strange grace the rougher moderns lack. Between
    him and González, you're watching sculpture step out of stone and into the machine age. The Surrealist rooms
    are next, a short way on.

 The dream takes over
 You've come into the Surrealist rooms — and you'll notice the museum asks you not to photograph in
 here, partly for the works on loan, so let your phone rest and just look. This is where Spanish art goes
 inward, in the late nineteen-twenties, and follows the dream wherever it leads.

 The names you'll meet are the famous ones — Dalí above all — but the surprise of these rooms is how
 many others were doing extraordinary things at the same moment, and how young most of them were.
 Several had met as students at the Residencia de Estudiantes in Madrid, the legendary hall of residence
 where a teenage Dalí shared corridors with the poet Federico García Lorca and the future film-maker
 Luis Buñuel. What grew out of those friendships — the films, the paintings, the scandals — is half of
 what hangs here.

 Keep two things in mind as you go. First, Surrealism wasn't only about shock; it was a serious attempt
 to paint the unconscious, the logic of dreams. And second, watch for the women — they were central to
 this story, and the museum has been working to put them back at its heart. Take it slowly, over here.

 Salvador Dalí, The Great Masturbator
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In the Dalí rooms — a large canvas, soft yellow and pale, dominated by a great drooping head in profile, eyes
    closed, nose pressed to the ground, with a woman's face rising out of the back of it.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    This is El gran masturbador, painted in 1929 — the most personal painting Dalí ever made, and the one that
    announced him to the world. That sagging profile is a self-portrait of a kind: its shape is taken directly from a
    rock formation on the shore at Cap de Creus, near his home in Catalonia, a coastline he knew better than his
    own face. Out of it spill his obsessions — desire, disgust, shame — pinned to the canvas with a hyper-real
    precision he'd borrowed from the old masters and turned to brand-new ends.
    LISTEN FOR
    The date matters. The summer of 1929 was when Dalí met Gala — the Russian émigrée, then married to the
    poet Paul Éluard — who would become his wife, his manager, and his lifelong obsession. This is the painting
    of a young man tipping headlong into love and terror at once. Look at the rising female face and the figure

 The war, and how it was seen
 These rooms are the reason people sometimes say the Reina Sofía is the closest thing Spain has to a
 museum of its Civil War. After the hush of Guernica, the walls fill with the raw machinery of how that

 kind of art you came expecting.

 You'll find propaganda posters, many of them startlingly modern in design, made to be pasted on city

 The Basque sculptors: Chillida and Oteiza
 Two names dominate Spanish sculpture of these years, and they came from the same small corner of the

 cradling emptiness.

 proved his point. Stand between the two of them and you're watching a real conversation about the same
 question — what is emptiness worth? — answered in opposite directions. Chillida first, right along here.

    different — a little further on.

 Richard Serra, Equal-Parallel: Guernica–Bengasi
    WHERE YOU ARE
    A room given over to four enormous blocks of rust-brown steel, set on the floor in an alternating arrangement
    with an empty space at the centre. Walk around and between them — that's the point.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Equal-Parallel: Guernica–Bengasi, made by the American sculptor Richard Serra in 1986. Four solid slabs
    of weathering steel — two squares, two rectangles — each raised to about a metre and a half, the exact height
    of the windowsills in the room. Serra usually insisted his work referred to nothing but itself, to weight and
    space and your own body moving through it. This piece is his one exception: the title yokes together two
    bombings of civilians — Guernica in 1937, and the United States Air Force's attack on Benghazi in Libya in
    April 1986, the very month he was making it.
    LISTEN FOR
    It was built specifically for this museum, for the 1986 exhibition that opened the Reina Sofía as an art centre.
    And then it became the subject of one of the strangest scandals in museum history. After the show it went into
    storage — and somewhere over the following years, it was simply lost. In 2006 the museum was forced to
    admit it had mislaid a thirty-eight-tonne steel sculpture: the storage firm had gone under, and nobody could
    say where the slabs had gone. What you're walking around is the replica Serra made to replace the vanished
    original. So this is a work about two erasures — two cities bombed off the map — that then, impossibly,
    erased itself. Stand in the empty centre for a second and let that sink in. When you're ready, there's one more
    floor — the newest art in the building — up at the top. This way to the lift.

 The top floor: the art of the democracy
 One more climb, to the top of the Sabatini building, and into the present. Floor 4 holds the
 contemporary collection — the art of Spain from roughly 1975, the year Franco died, all the way to
 now. After everything you've walked through downstairs, this floor feels like a held breath being let out.

 The late seventies and eighties were Spain's great exhale — the years of la Movida, the explosion of
 freedom and colour and noise that followed the dictatorship, when Madrid stayed up all night and artists
 tore into every style at once. Out of it came a generation that finally rejoined the international art world
 as equals: painters like Miquel Barceló, sculptors and installation-makers like Cristina Iglesias and Juan
 Muñoz, set among foreign and Latin-American voices the museum has deliberately woven in.

 I'll be honest with you here, more than anywhere: this is the floor most in flux. It's the front line of a
 re-hang that runs to 2028, and contemporary collections rotate fast by nature. So treat the next few stops
 as the kinds of thing you'll meet rather than a fixed checklist, read the room sheets, and follow what
 catches you. A few artists worth seeking out, through here.

 Miquel Barceló, matter and life
    WHERE YOU ARE
    A large, heavily worked canvas that almost bulges off the wall — thick crusts of paint, often earth-coloured,
    sometimes scattered with what look like real organic traces: fruit, fish, the residue of a meal or a sea floor.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Miquel Barceló, born in Mallorca in 1957, the leading Spanish painter to emerge from the Movida years and
    the first of his generation to win real international fame. He builds his surfaces up into near-relief, mixing
    pigment with sand, soil and organic matter, so the painting becomes a kind of living, decaying landscape you
    could almost dig into.
    LISTEN FOR
    Barceló paints what he lives among — the dry Mallorcan earth, the Mediterranean sea-bed, and later the light
    and termite-mounds of West Africa, where he spent long stretches. There's a direct line back to Tàpies
    downstairs in all that matter, but where Tàpies's walls are grave and grey, Barceló's teem — with heat, decay,
    abundance, life. Stand back and it's a landscape; step close and it's a crust of stuff caught mid-transformation.
    Two sculptors who reshaped Spanish space are just beyond.

 Cristina Iglesias, built nature
    WHERE YOU ARE
    An installation rather than an object — perhaps a hanging canopy or a screen of woven, vegetal-looking
    material, or a chamber you can step toward, often casting intricate latticed shadows.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Cristina Iglesias, born in San Sebastián in 1956 and among the most admired sculptors Spain has produced
    since the war. She makes spaces rather than statues — suspended ceilings, walls of cast foliage, passageways
    and wells — in materials like resin, bronze, woven esparto and water, that blur the line between architecture
    and nature, the built and the grown.
    LISTEN FOR
    Her work plays a subtle trick on you. What reads as dense vegetation turns out to be carefully cast and
    constructed; what looks like a natural grotto is engineered to the millimetre. She's fascinated by thresholds —
    the canopy you stand under, the screen you can't quite see through, the water you can't reach — so the
    experience is always about your own body, waiting at an edge. It's the inheritance of Chillida and Oteiza's
    obsession with space, carried into a new generation. Juan Muñoz, who filled space with unsettling figures, is
    round this corner.

 Juan Muñoz, the crowd
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Figures — small, grey, oddly featureless human forms, often grouped, sometimes laughing, sometimes
    balanced on impossible perches — that you come upon as if you'd interrupted them.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Juan Muñoz, born in Madrid in 1953 and lost far too early in 2001, was a foremost Spanish sculptor of the
    uncanny human presence. He filled rooms with grey resin figures — his "conversation pieces" — caught
    mid-gesture, mid-laugh, mid-something, never quite acknowledging you, so that you become the intruder in a
    scene whose meaning you've just missed.
    LISTEN FOR
    The unease is deliberate and beautifully judged. The figures are almost identical, almost faceless, often
    slightly too small, and they relate to one another and to the architecture but never to you — and that exclusion
    is the whole feeling. He was a storyteller without a story, building theatrical spaces of silence and isolation.
    He died suddenly at forty-eight, just as the world fully recognised him; the loss still shadows Spanish art.
    Beyond these names, the floor opens out to the wider world — a short way on.

 Susana Solano, iron enclosures
    WHERE YOU ARE
    A large sculpture in iron and steel mesh — a cage, a tank, an enclosure you can look into but not enter,
    austere and discreetly forbidding.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Susana Solano, born in Barcelona in 1946, one of the major Spanish sculptors of the generation after Chillida
    and Oteiza, and for years among the most internationally respected. She works in industrial iron, lead and

    not pass; the space is offered and withheld at once. She inherits the Basque sculptors' obsession with what
    emptiness can hold, and gives it a cooler, more architectural, faintly anxious edge — the void as something
    guarded. The museum's reach beyond Spain is over here.

 The world in the room: international and Latin
 American art
 The contemporary floor is also where the Reina Sofía makes its boldest argument — that it is not a
 museum of Spanish art that occasionally borrows from abroad, but a museum of modern and
 contemporary art with a particular, deliberate tilt toward Latin America and the global South.

 So among the Spaniards you'll find international heavyweights — Louise Bourgeois, Bruce Nauman,
 the conceptual and minimal currents of the later twentieth century — set alongside Brazilian
 Neo-Concrete art like Lygia Clark, Italian Arte Povera, and a growing presence of Latin American
 artists, including politically charged and queer voices such as the Chilean Pedro Lemebel. Photography,
 video and installation crowd in beside painting and sculpture.

 It changes often, and it's meant to feel restless — this is the part of the collection still being written,
 where the museum argues out loud about what art is for now. Don't try to see it all. Let something stop
 you. When you're ready, there's one last place to go — up and out — this way.

 Lygia Clark, Bichos
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Small geometric objects of hinged metal plates — folded, angular, looking like they want to be picked up and
    reshaped.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Lygia Clark, the Brazilian pioneer of Neo-Concrete art, and her famous Bichos — "Critters" — of the late
    1950s and sixties: flat metal shapes joined by hinges so they can be folded and refolded into endless
    three-dimensional configurations. They were made to be handled, manipulated, brought to life by the viewer's
    own hands.
    LISTEN FOR
    That was the radical idea — to break the artwork out of the frame and the pedestal and put it in your grip,
    making you a collaborator rather than a spectator. Clark went further still, toward art as something close to
    therapy and shared bodily experience. Her presence here is the museum stating its case out loud: that the story
    of modern art runs through São Paulo and Rio as surely as through Paris and New York. One final stop, right
    this way.

 Pedro Lemebel, protest and performance
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Most likely photographs, video or documents rather than a single object — the record of performances and

    WHAT YOU SEE

    LISTEN FOR

    canvas, beyond the straight and the official. Lemebel made his own body and his words the work, insisting on
    dignity and desire under a regime that denied both. Set him against Guernica downstairs and you can see one
    continuous thread across the whole museum: art as a way of refusing to be silenced. The roof is waiting, just
    along here.

 Out onto the roof
 To finish, leave the old hospital and cross into the Nouvel building — the glass-and-steel wing with the
 great red lattice roof that the architect Jean Nouvel wrapped around the Sabatini block in 2005.

 This is where the museum keeps much of its breathing room: the big temporary exhibitions, the library,
 the café in the covered courtyard, and — best of all to end on — the rooftop terraces, where
 contemporary sculpture sits in the open air under that red canopy, with one of the finest high views over
 the rooftops of Madrid. After hours inside Spain's hardest century, it's the right place to come up for
 light.

 That's the collection, from the avant-garde's first dream to the art of right now. Whatever's currently up
 here changes by the season — so treat the roof as your full stop, catch your breath, and let the city back
 in. When you're ready, I'll see you back at the entrance for a last word.

 Back out into Madrid
 That's the century, more or less — from the hope of the avant-garde, through the scream of Guernica,
 into the long silence of the dictatorship and out into everything that came after. It's worth saying what
 makes this museum unusual. The Prado, up the road, is a treasure-house: the spoils of kings, gathered
 for pleasure. This place is something else. It's a museum that takes its own country's worst wound and
 keeps it on the wall, unhealed, on purpose — so that nobody gets to look away. That's why Guernica
 doesn't sit behind glass. You were meant to stand close enough to feel small in front of it.

 You've walked the whole collection now — the avant-garde and the war down on Floor 2, the long grey
 aftermath and the Transition on Floor 1, and up here, the open present. Whatever's left to discover is in
 the Nouvel building across the way: the big temporary exhibitions, and the rooftop terraces, where
 there's open-air sculpture and one of the finest views in Madrid. It changes constantly; that's rather the
 point.

 When you step back outside, you're in the corner of Madrid they call the art triangle — the Prado and
 the Thyssen are a short walk north, and Atocha station is right there if you're moving on. But give
 yourself a minute first. Sit in the Sabatini garden, or in the courtyard under Nouvel's red roof. A
 museum like this asks a lot of you. It's allowed to. Thank you for walking it with me.
