[warmly] In October 1916, Juan Gris painted his partner, Josette. His Cubist friends had broken things into pieces. Gris built her, the way you'd build a house.

He had worked in Paris for years, first drawing for magazines, before he turned to Cubism. Around 1916 his painting grew simpler and clearer. He described his pictures as a "flat and coloured architecture".

[pause] Look at her shape. Head at the top, body widening down to the lap. That's a pyramid, the classical composition of the French painter Corot. Then look at her hands, resting crossed in her lap. That pose comes from Cézanne's portraits of his own wife, seated in a yellow armchair. Gris was looking back at the masters, as Picasso would too.

It's a calm picture, almost still. Form matters more than colour here, and more than feeling. The painting was a gift from the collector Douglas Cooper.

[curious] Now find a larger, much brighter canvas: María Blanchard's Cubist Composition. Blanchard faced prejudice twice over, as a woman and for her disability. She trained in Madrid, won a grant to go to Paris in 1909, and joined the circle of Gris, Jean Metzinger and Jacques Lipchitz. In 1916 she settled in Paris for good.

Her picture is nearly abstract. Planes of lemon yellow, pink, orange and green hide objects you can barely make out. It's meant to unsettle you. Then, in one place, she paints a baroque moulding in careful detail, the one solid thing to hold on to. Can you find it?

Two Spanish painters in Paris, in the same years. One made Cubism calm. The other made it dizzying.
