[curious] Sabatini planned this courtyard for a hospital. He imagined two fountains here, and trees like those in Madrid's Royal Botanical Garden. It was the largest courtyard in his design, and one of the few parts that was actually built.

Today it's a garden, with sculpture among the plants. Look for a tall bronze figure, well over two metres high. This is Pablo Gargallo's Great Prophet. He modelled it in plaster in 1933, meaning it for bronze. Money was short, and the bronze was only cast after his death. The figure is too big and too thick for the sheet metal he often used. Yet light still passes right through it. The critic Pierre Cabanne saw it as a study of volume and emptiness, finished by your own eye.

[quietly] Now find two rust-brown steel crosses, bent towards each other in a curve. This is Eduardo Chillida's Toki egin, a homage to the Spanish mystic San Juan de la Cruz. Toki egin is Basque for "make way". The two crosses embrace and seem to push against gravity, like the union of the soul with God in the saint's poetry. Chillida wanted space itself to be the subject, not only the steel around it.

Back inside on Floor 1, Room 102 holds Richard Serra's steel sculpture.
