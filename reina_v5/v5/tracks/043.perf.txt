[quietly] "I've got your soul." As Hervé Guibert told it, that's what his friend Miquel Barceló said to him while painting his portrait.

Guibert was a French writer, critic and photographer. In 1990 he published a book called To the Friend Who Did Not Save My Life. In it he made public that he was HIV-positive, at a time when that was, in effect, a death sentence. He wrote about it with a bare, unsparing honesty that turned his own life into fiction. The book became a landmark.

Barceló was his friend in those last years. Guibert died in 1991. The next year, two of his novels came out with Barceló at their centre, as a character called Yannis.

[pause] In 1990, Barceló painted these portraits. They're tiny, about the size of a sheet of paper folded in half. Guibert described the sittings in violent terms. He wrote of a death's head under a red hat, with two incandescent blue eyes.

Look at them now, one after another. Most are titled Untitled. A few have names, like The Specter, Le Cri paisible, Belfegor. The room calls them portraits that are still unreleased.

[reverent] What Barceló is testing here is the limit of painting. How do you paint someone who is disappearing? How do you bring death onto a canvas, and show an absence? His own answer comes from his recent autobiography, and it closes the room's text. To paint is to erase.
