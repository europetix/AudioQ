[warmly] In the summer of 1937, Paris held a great international exhibition about art and technology in modern life. Spain was a year into its civil war. And the Republic decided to build a pavilion there anyway.

It had two aims. To show that culture belonged with social progress, and to make visitors face the danger of fascism. The architects, Josep Lluís Sert and Luis Lacasa, chose what they called simple, popular architecture. Find their model, made in 1987. It's a light, modern box, nothing like the monumental pavilions of other countries. Inside there were films, concerts, dance and theatre. Josep Renau's huge photomurals filled most of the pavilion.

The curators commissioned work from Picasso, Miró, Alberto Sánchez, Julio González, José Gutiérrez Solana and one foreigner, the American Alexander Calder.

[curious] Calder's fountain ran with mercury. Real, liquid mercury. It ran over three irregular surfaces, across the pool, and struck a paddle. That set the mobile swinging above it, with its red disc and a single word. ALMADEN.

What you see here is a maquette, at one third of the full size. It copies the model Calder made to get his idea approved. The mercury is cut and painted cardboard.

[pause] So why mercury? Almadén is where Spain's mercury mines are, a key resource in wartime. Calder's fountain stood near Guernica to point at the economic interests behind the uprising against the Republic. In March 1939, General Yagüe took the mines.

Before you go on, look for two small drawings of a horse. They are studies for Guernica. Solana's Procession of Death and Horacio Ferrer's Madrid 1937, Black Aeroplanes, hang here too.
