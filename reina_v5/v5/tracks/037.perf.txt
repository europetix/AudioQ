[wry] Madrid in the early eighties wanted two things at once. It wanted the world to notice it. And it wanted to catch up, fast.

In January 1983 the gallery owner Fernando Vijande brought Andy Warhol to Madrid for a show called Pistolas, cuchillos y cruces: Pistols, Knives and Crosses. Warhol made thirty-five works for it. Knives belongs to that moment. The museum credits that show with putting the city on the international contemporary art map.

Look at the canvas. It's a kitchen knife, repeated, in vivid colours against a dark ground. An everyday tool, the kind in any drawer. And at the same time a sign of violence and of death. Warhol had been shot and nearly killed in 1968, and this series carries his long preoccupation with fragility and mortality.

[pause] Now the small rust-coloured block. This is Richard Serra's model, from 1981, for a sculpture in Madrid's Plaza de Callao. It was never built. The project was first dropped over public health concerns. A second site was chosen at Atocha. Then the mayor, Enrique Tierno Galván, the mayor of La Movida, died, and the plan died with him.

[quietly] That's the point of this room. After Franco, Spain wanted to be in step with the world. Galleries became meeting points, and live art opened new doors. Manel Armengol's photographs of The Living Theatre are part of that story. But the room calls the Transition a rushed process to make up for lost time, without a social or institutional foundation to hold it up. A steel sculpture that stayed a model is the proof.

The second lift sits just past this room. On the other side of it, Room 4, 'The Personal Is Political: Feminisms and New Gender Presences', begins Route I.
