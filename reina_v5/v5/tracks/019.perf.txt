[amused] In 1928, the philosopher José Ortega y Gasset gave a young painter her own show, in the rooms of his magazine, the Revista de Occidente. Maruja Mallo hung four paintings of Madrid's summer street fairs, the verbenas. This is one of them.

At first it's pure noise and colour. There's a shooting gallery, and one of those machines where men hit something to test their strength. Then the odd characters start to appear. Find the one-eyed giant. Find the priest enjoying a sideshow. And find the man with deformed feet, begging, with a guitar slung on his back.

The satire is gentle, but it's there. Mallo puts every piece of a traditional Madrid fair into a whirlwind of lines, and the result has an undeniably Surrealist air. You'll meet her again in Room 206.01, in a very different mood.

[pause] Now turn to the quiet picture. Salvador Dalí painted it two years earlier, before he became a Surrealist. He was still trying on the styles of the day, Cubism and a cool, classical realism.

The young woman is his sister, Anna Maria. She was the model he painted most often in those years. At his first solo show, in Barcelona, six of the seventeen paintings were of her. Here he shows her from behind, leaning at the window, looking out over the bay of Cadaqués.

[quietly] Everything is still. The lines run level, like the horizon. Dalí was drawing on Ingres and Picasso, and he had seen Mantegna's Death of the Virgin at the Prado. You can feel that old Italian calm in the landscape. And look at the surface. It's oil on papier-mâché, not canvas.

A fair that never stops moving, and a window where nothing moves at all. Two young painters, and two completely different ways of being modern.
