[amused] Here's a painting that works like a class photograph. A whole Madrid art scene, posed in one grand imaginary building.

Guillermo Pérez Villalta painted it in the mid-seventies, for his first exhibition at the Vandrés Gallery. It's a group portrait of his generation, the artists, critics and gallery owners around him. The museum calls it a manifesto painting for what became known as the New Madrid Figuration.

Look at how it's built. Pérez Villalta trained in architecture, and you can tell. The figures stand in an invented courtyard with echoes of sixteenth-century Mannerism. Behind them, a garden opens up like a maze, inspired by the gardens of Andalusia. Each person is placed with care and given a pose or a symbol, the way Raphael arranged the thinkers in his School of Athens. He liked to call himself an "artificer", a maker of images.

The room takes its title from a phrase by the critic Juan Manuel Bonet: "a more painted painting." After years of conceptual art, a new current claimed that the subject of painting is simply painting itself. In Spain, it fitted the mood of the eighties, when the country joined the Common Market and its painters sold well abroad.

[wry] The curators are frank about what happened next. The movement's supporters rejected conceptual and political art, and its friendly critics were quickly celebrated in the media. Then, in later decades, institutions largely turned their backs on it.

From a distance, the lines look less strict. Look for Manolo Quejido's work in this room. It isn't a painting at all. It's a real wooden chair, with a real typewriter sitting on it.

[lightly] A machine, sat on a chair, in a room about paint. Even here, the rules were never as pure as they sounded.
