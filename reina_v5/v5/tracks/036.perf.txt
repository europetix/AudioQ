[curious] In the early hours of the eighth of June 1976, a bomb went off in a bar on Calle Libertad, in Madrid.

The bar was called La Vaquería. Its crowd was bohemian and countercultural, and the sign at the entrance promised painting, music and poetry, whisky and sandwiches. The bomb was the work of a far-right group, the Guerrilleros de Cristo Rey. It left the bar partly destroyed and the glass of its door in pieces.

So two young artists covered the broken door with boards and painted them. These are those boards. The central panel is by Borja Satrústegui, dark and textured, close to the matter painting of Antoni Tàpies. The two side panels are by Ceesepe, who was still a teenager. One he never finished. The other he drew in marker pen, and it has faded over the years.

[quietly] Months later, the same group came back and fired on the boards. Look closely and you can still see the bullet holes.

That's the turn in this room. What was made to patch a door became a document of the Transition, the fragile years between dictatorship and democracy. All around you is the material culture of that moment: comics and illustration, painting, photography, video, fashion and furniture. These scenes gave visibility to lives that had stayed hidden under Franco. They came to be known as La Movida.

Much of this art was first shown far from any museum, at night, in bars and private spaces where young people could let go. [wry] And the new democratic politicians were quick to adopt it as their emblem.

Find Guillermo Pérez Villalta's painting of figures spilling out of a rock concert, too. It's the same night-time crowd, caught on canvas.
