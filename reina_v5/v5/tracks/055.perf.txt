[quietly] Here's a number the museum puts in its own room text. Women artists make up less than fifteen percent of the work it holds. And the next sentence is just as plain: equality at the Reina Sofía "remains a work in progress."

That honesty sets the tone for this last room on Floor 4. It's about the decades since the mid-nineties, when feminism and the fight for LGTBIQ+ rights reshaped what art could show, and who it could show. Queer theory treated gender as something built by society, not fixed by biology.

Pilar Albarracín's photographs date from 1993 and are titled simply Women. They are round, and of different sizes, like portholes onto other lives.

[warmly] Then come Catherine Opie's portraits. In the mid-nineties she made a series of fifty colour portraits of people in San Francisco: members of the leather community, drag queens, performance artists, transgender people, close friends. Each one is named, by their real name.

Now look at how they're made. Opie borrowed the language of Hans Holbein, the great Renaissance portrait painter. Frontal poses. Restrained gestures. Rich detail. The museum says she builds "dignity, intimacy and permanence."

[pause] That's the turn. For centuries, this kind of portrait was kept for white, heterosexual, well-off men. Opie gives the same seriousness to people who were usually pushed out of the frame, and in doing so, the text says, affirms their right to exist and be seen.

Find the portrait of Crystal Mason. The gaze comes back at you as calmly as any sitter's in a Holbein.

That's the end of Floor 4. To finish, take the glass lift tower next to this room down to Floor 1, and follow the signs to the Nouvel building. There, take a Nouvel lift up to the roof terrace on Floor 3. If the terrace is closed today, the last two stops work anywhere.
