 Joan Miró, Painting (Snail, Woman, Flower, Star)
    WHERE YOU ARE
    A wide canvas of flat, glowing colour — soft blues and earth-reds — across which a few simple shapes and a
    line of hand-painted words seem to float free of gravity.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    This is Miró's Pintura of 1934, usually called by the words written across it — Snail, Woman, Flower, Star.
    By this point Miró had burned away almost everything a painting was supposed to have. No scene, no
    horizon, no story you could retell — just a private alphabet of signs, a kind of poetry made of shapes, drifting
    on a coloured ground. He once said he wanted to "assassinate painting," to kill off the polite, decorative
    version of it, and works like this are him doing exactly that.
    LISTEN FOR
    Don't try to decode it like a puzzle — that's not how it works. Let your eye wander the way it would over a
    night sky, picking out a curve here, a star there, the looping handwriting that names the snail and the woman
    and the flower without ever picturing them. This is the heart of what people mean by "a Miró": the childlike
    directness that is in fact the product of ferocious discipline, every shape placed with the care of a calligrapher.
    Look at the date again — 1934. Spain was two years from civil war, and the lightness here would not survive
    it. The works Miró made for the doomed Republic, a few years and a few rooms on, come out of a much
    darker place. For now, though, this is the dream at its most weightless. A couple more, not far on.
