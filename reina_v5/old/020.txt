 Óscar Domínguez, decalcomania
    WHERE YOU ARE
    A strange, mottled canvas whose textures look grown rather than painted — spongy, cavernous, vaguely
    volcanic forms that the eye keeps trying to read as rock, coral or cloud.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Óscar Domínguez, the Surrealist from Tenerife, in the Canary Islands, and a master of a chance technique
    called decalcomania — spreading wet paint, pressing another surface onto it, then peeling it away to leave a
    richly accidental texture that he'd then coax into a landscape or a figure.
    LISTEN FOR
    It's automatism made physical — the Surrealist dream of letting accident, not the conscious mind, do the
    inventing. Domínguez would find the image hiding in the random blot, the way you find shapes in clouds,
    and bring it forward. His best work has a brooding, geological strangeness, full of the volcanic light of his
    home islands. He lived hard in the Paris Surrealist circle and died by his own hand in 1957; there's a real
    melancholy under the playful method. Surrealism's most notorious collaboration wasn't a painting at all — it
    was on a screen, just ahead.
