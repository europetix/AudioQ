 Alexander Calder, Mercury Fountain
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In the reconstructed Pavilion, not far from Guernica — a low metal basin in which a heavy, mirror-bright
    liquid slides and drips through a series of shallow trays, feeding a thin mobile that quivers at the top. It sits
    behind protective glass.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    This is the Mercury Fountain, designed in 1937 by the American sculptor Alexander Calder — the only
    foreigner invited to make work for the Spanish Republic's Pavilion. The Pavilion's architect, Josep Lluís Sert,
    asked him for something as modern as the Picasso and the Miró it would share the building with, and Calder
    gave them a fountain that ran not with water but with liquid mercury, tumbling down step by step until it set
    the little wire mobile trembling.
    LISTEN FOR
    The mercury was the whole point. It came from the mines at Almadén, in southern Spain — at that moment
    one of the largest mercury sources on earth, and a town under siege by Franco's forces. So a fountain of
    running silver metal stood in for a besieged Republican town, a quiet companion-piece to the screaming
    canvas a few metres away. The original fountain lives now at the Miró foundation in Barcelona, where Calder
    gave it; what flows here is the museum's reconstruction of it for the rebuilt Pavilion. We know now what they
    didn't fully grasp then — that mercury vapour is poison — which is why it's sealed behind glass today.
    Beautiful, toxic, and political to its core: that was the Pavilion in miniature. From here, the rooms turn to the
    war itself, just beyond.
