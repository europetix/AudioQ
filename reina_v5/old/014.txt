TRACK 014 · 205                                     W-FOCUS                               241 WORDS · ~2 MIN

 Salvador Dalí, Figure at a Window
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Nearby, and a complete change of weather — a calm, tender, almost photographic picture of a young woman
    seen from behind, leaning on a windowsill, looking out over a still blue bay.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Figura en una ventana — Figure at a Window — from 1925, painted in oil on paper when Dalí was just
    twenty-one, years before the melting clocks and the moustache. The woman is his younger sister, Ana María,
    who was his favourite model before Gala arrived. The window looks out over the water at Cadaqués, the
    Catalan fishing village where the family spent its summers, and where Dalí would live for most of his life.
    LISTEN FOR
    Hold this one next to The Great Masturbator hanging a few steps away, and you can see a whole personality
    in the gap between them. Here there's no provocation at all — just a brother's unhurried attention to his sister,
    the light on her dress, the patient blue of the bay. It belongs to a long Romantic tradition of figures seen from
    behind, gazing out — the German painter Caspar David Friedrich did it a century earlier — where the point is
    that we look with her, not at her. Within a few years Dalí would blow this calm apart and never paint anything
    this peaceful again. That's why it's worth lingering on: it's the last clear photograph of him before Surrealism,
    and Gala, and fame rearranged everything. Miró's room is right this way.
