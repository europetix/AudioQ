 Julio González, iron
    WHERE YOU ARE
    A spare, open sculpture of welded iron — not a solid mass but a few rods and planes drawing a figure or a
    head in the air, with as much empty space as metal.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Julio González, the Catalan who, more than anyone, invented modern iron sculpture. His family were
    metalworkers in Barcelona; he learned to forge and weld as a trade, moved to Paris, and in the late 1920s
    made a historic alliance — he taught his friend Picasso how to weld, and together they opened up a whole
    new way of making sculpture: not carving or casting, but drawing directly in steel.
    LISTEN FOR
    That's the phrase to carry — "drawing in space." Instead of a solid body, González gives you the lines of one,
    with the air left showing through, so the empty space becomes part of the figure. It looks effortless and
    modern; it rests on a lifetime of real blacksmith's skill. Everything that follows on the floor above —
    Chillida's forged iron, Oteiza's open boxes — descends from this silent revolution in a Paris workshop. His
    countryman Gargallo pushed metal another way, round this corner.
