 The Basque sculptors: Chillida and Oteiza
 Two names dominate Spanish sculpture of these years, and they came from the same small corner of the
 Basque Country — close enough to be rivals, and they were.

 Eduardo Chillida worked iron the way a poet works words: heavy forged beams that reach out and curl
 back, holding empty space inside their grip. Trained first as an architect, he was forever interested in
 what the gap, the air, the wind did to the metal around it; his great combs of steel out on the Atlantic at
 San Sebastián are the famous example, but even the smaller pieces here carry that sense of weight
 cradling emptiness.

 Jorge Oteiza went at the same problem from the opposite side. Where Chillida added, Oteiza subtracted
 — hollowing his forms out until what's left is mostly the void, the absence, the boxes he called
 "metaphysical." He more or less argued himself out of making sculpture altogether, deciding he'd
 proved his point. Stand between the two of them and you're watching a real conversation about the same
 question — what is emptiness worth? — answered in opposite directions. Chillida first, right along here.
