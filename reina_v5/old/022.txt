TRACK 022 · FLOOR 2                                W-FOCUS                               240 WORDS · ~2 MIN

 Luis Buñuel & Salvador Dalí, Un Chien Andalou
    WHERE YOU ARE
    A small darkened room with a screen, somewhere among the Surrealist galleries. Find a seat; it only runs
    about sixteen minutes, and it loops.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Un Chien Andalou — An Andalusian Dog — made in 1929 by two friends from the Residencia de
    Estudiantes: the film-maker Luis Buñuel and the painter Salvador Dalí. It's the most famous Surrealist film
    ever made, and it opens with the single most notorious image in cinema — a razor blade drawn across a
    woman's eye. (Stay for it or look away; you'll know it's coming now.) After that it abandons story altogether:
    ants pour from a hand, a man drags two grand pianos loaded with dead donkeys and priests across a room,
    time and place refuse to behave.
    LISTEN FOR
    The two of them built it, by their own account, by each rejecting any image that "made sense" — they wanted
    only what arrived unbidden, the logic of a dream with the safety rails removed. It was meant to scandalise the
    polite Paris avant-garde, and it did; Buñuel claimed he went to the premiere with stones in his pockets in case
    the audience attacked. Watching it here, surrounded by the paintings, you understand the room you're in
    differently — these weren't just pictures of dreams; this was a whole generation trying to film the inside of a
    sleeping head. When you surface, the floor changes mood completely. The Republic is about to go to war,
    through here.
