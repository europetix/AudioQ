 Paris, 1937: the Pavilion and the wall it was built
 around
 Slow down here. You're walking into the room the whole floor has been building toward, and it helps to
 know what you're about to see before you see it.

 In 1937, Spain was a year into its civil war, and the elected Republic was losing. That spring it did
 something audacious: it built a pavilion at the World's Fair in Paris — a calm, modern, glass-and-steel
 box — not to show off, but to plead its case to the world, to say we are still here, and look what is being
 done to us. The government asked Picasso, the most famous living Spaniard, to paint a mural for one of
 its walls. They gave him the size — it had to fill the wall — and otherwise total freedom.

 The rooms here reconstruct that pavilion. Around the one painting you'll find the other works that stood
 in it: a fountain of running mercury by the American Alexander Calder, a striking figure in sheet iron by
 Julio González, a lost mural by Miró known now only from photographs. They were all part of the same
 argument. And then there is the wall itself — the reason this room exists, and the reason this museum
 exists in the form it does. It's right in front of you.
