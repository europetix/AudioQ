 The war, and how it was seen
 These rooms are the reason people sometimes say the Reina Sofía is the closest thing Spain has to a
 museum of its Civil War. After the hush of Guernica, the walls fill with the raw machinery of how that
 war was fought for hearts and minds — and it's worth slowing down for, because almost none of it is the
 kind of art you came expecting.

 You'll find propaganda posters, many of them startlingly modern in design, made to be pasted on city
 walls overnight; photographs from the front and from the bombed cities; magazines, pamphlets, the
 photomontages of artists like Josep Renau, who turned the cut-and-paste techniques of the avant-garde
 into weapons. Both sides used images, but it's the Republic's that mostly survive here, because this is, in
 part, the Republic's memorial.

 Two things are worth carrying through these rooms. First, how new it all felt — this was one of the first
 wars fought as much in the press and the cinema as on the ground. And second, how it ends: not in
 victory, but in defeat, exile, and forty years of silence under Franco. The art on the upper floor is what
 that silence eventually produced. Before we leave, one photographer deserves a moment of his own —
 over here.
