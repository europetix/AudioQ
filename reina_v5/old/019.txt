 Maruja Mallo, The Fair
    WHERE YOU ARE
    A crowded, brilliantly coloured scene of a Madrid street festival — booths and bunting and crowds — but
    look twice, because the crowd is full of impossible people.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    La Verbena — The Fair — by Maruja Mallo, from 1927: one of a series she painted of Madrid's popular
    street parties. At first glance it's pure joy, a snapshot of a summer verbena. Then the details start to
    misbehave: a one-eyed giant looms over the stalls; a beggar with grotesquely deformed feet shuffles through
    with a guitar slung on his back. Mallo takes the most ordinary Spanish scene there is and lets the dream leak
    into it.
    LISTEN FOR
    Mallo is one of the long-overlooked figures of this museum. She belonged to the brilliant generation around
    Lorca and Dalí — and she was one of "Las Sinsombrero," the hatless ones, a circle of modern women who
    once famously took off their hats in the middle of Madrid as a small act of rebellion, and who were largely
    written out of the history afterwards. She was independent, celebrated in her day, championed by the
    philosopher Ortega y Gasset, then scattered into exile by the Civil War. For decades her name faded while the
    men's grew. Standing here, you can see what was almost lost: a painter who could make a street party feel like
    a vision. The film is next — and for that one, you'll want to sit down. It's close by.
