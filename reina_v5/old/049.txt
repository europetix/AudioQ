 Louise Bourgeois, cells and spiders
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Something that unsettles before you understand it — perhaps a caged "cell" of objects, a stitched figure, or
    the leg of a great spider.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Louise Bourgeois, the French-American artist who, across a career of almost eighty years, turned private
    memory, the body and the family into among the most powerful bodies of work of the century. The museum
    holds her among its international voices. Her spiders she called Maman — mother — a tribute, not a horror:
    her mother was a weaver, patient and protective. Her caged "Cells" trap furniture, clothing and fragments into
    chambers of memory you can only peer into.
    LISTEN FOR
    Bourgeois made art as a way of surviving her own childhood, and she's unflinching about it — desire,
    jealousy, fear, repair, all of it stitched and caged and cast. The work looks psychological because it is; she
    said sculpture let her give shape to feelings she couldn't otherwise hold. Now Spain reaches for the
    recognisable world again, not far on.
