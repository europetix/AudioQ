 Joan Miró, Portrait II
    WHERE YOU ARE
    A small, fierce canvas nearby — a head, more or less, but reduced to a few flat shapes, a couple of staring
    dots for eyes, set against a flaming red-and-black ground.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Retrato II, painted by Miró in 1938 — a world away from the weightless Snail of four years earlier. By 1938
    Spain was at war and Europe was sliding toward another one, and Miró's work went dark and savage. The
    playful signs harden here into something almost ferocious: a face stripped down to its rawest components,
    glaring out of a scorched colour field.
    LISTEN FOR
    Set it beside the 1934 painting hanging ahead of you and you can watch the temperature of his art drop. Same
    private alphabet, same flat brilliant colour — but the lightness has curdled into alarm. This is Miró registering
    catastrophe in the only language he had: not by painting soldiers or ruins, but by letting the shapes themselves
    grow teeth. It's a reminder that abstraction isn't an escape from history; sometimes it's the most direct record
    of it we have. Two more women the museum has worked to put back at the centre are a few steps on.
