 Francis Bacon, Lying Figure
    WHERE YOU ARE
    A single, unmistakable canvas — a lone human figure, distorted almost to meat, slumped or sprawled on a
    bare structure inside a flat, airless field of colour.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    This is the museum's one major Francis Bacon, a Lying Figure from the 1960s. Bacon was the supreme
    British painter of the body in extremis — not the wounded body of war, but the everyday horror of simply
    being flesh, alone, in a room. He worked from photographs and memory rather than from a live model,
    smearing and twisting the face and limbs until the figure seems caught mid-spasm, then set them in cool,
    hard-edged spaces that only sharpen the violence.
    LISTEN FOR
    It's worth asking why this hangs here, in a museum so bound up with Spain's own history. Bacon revered the
    Spanish masters — Velázquez above all, whose screaming pope he reworked obsessively — and his caged,
    howling figures are a direct descendant of the tradition that runs through Goya's black paintings up the road at
    the Prado. So this lone foreigner isn't really an outsider; he's the international face of a very Spanish
    obsession with the suffering body. After the political wounds of the floors below, Bacon makes it private —
    just a person, in a room, with no war to blame. Now Spain reaches for the recognisable world again, just
    along here.
