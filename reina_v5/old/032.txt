 The matter and the scream: Tàpies and El Paso
 The first thing to find is the abstraction that came out of the nineteen-fifties — and you'll feel it before
 you read a single label, because so much of it looks wounded.

 The presiding figure is Antoni Tàpies, a Catalan who turned the humble wall into his subject. Around
 him, look for the group called El Paso — "the step" — founded in Madrid in 1957: Antonio Saura's
 furious black-and-white figures, Manolo Millares's torn and knotted sackcloth, Rafael Canogar's raw
 gestures. They had almost nothing in common stylistically, but they shared a conviction — that after the
 catastrophe, beauty was a lie, and only raw matter told the truth.

 None of it is comfortable. None of it was meant to be. Under a censorship that watched every
 recognisable image, abstraction became a loophole: a painting that names nothing can't be accused of
 saying anything, yet these scarred surfaces say a great deal. It was protest that learned to speak in a
 language the regime couldn't quite read. Let's look at four of them in turn, beginning with the master of
 the wall, right this way.
