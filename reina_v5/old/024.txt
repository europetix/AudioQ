 Picasso, Guernica
    WHERE YOU ARE
    The long wall of Room 206. You'll know it the moment you turn the corner — almost eight metres of black,
    white and grey, with a crowd standing silent in front of it. There's no glass, no barrier but a low rope. Stand
    back far enough to take in the whole wall; it's three and a half metres tall.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Guernica, 1937. On the twenty-sixth of April that year, German and Italian warplanes flying for Franco
    bombed the Basque town of Gernika on a busy market day, for hours, until it burned. When the news and the
    photographs reached Paris, Picasso — who had been stuck for a subject — found it. He painted this enormous
    canvas in barely five weeks, in his studio on the Rue des Grands-Augustins, in the greys of newsprint, as if
    the painting itself were a front page. His companion Dora Maar photographed it as it changed, day by day;
    those photographs hang nearby.
    LISTEN FOR
    Everyone goes first to the left — the bull, the screaming horse, the woman holding her dead child. But give
    the right-hand side its due. There, a figure is falling through a burning building with her arms thrown up, and
    that is where the painting reaches its true pitch of panic. Look for the broken sword and the small flower in
    the dead soldier's hand; the lightbulb glaring like an eye at the top; the lamp held out by a ghostly arm, reason
    trying to see in the dark. Picasso refused to let the painting come home while Franco lived — he said it could
    only return when Spain was a republic again, when liberties were restored. It waited out the dictatorship in
    New York. It finally arrived in Madrid in 1981, six years after Franco died. It has not left since. When you
    can step away — and it takes a few minutes — the rooms of the war itself are close by.
