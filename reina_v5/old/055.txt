TRACK 055 · FLOOR 1                                 W-FOCUS                               275 WORDS · ~2 MIN

 Juan Genovés, The Embrace
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In the gallery devoted to the Transition. Look for a painting seen as if from above: a crowd of plain, faceless
    figures rushing together into one another's arms.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    This is El abrazo — The Embrace — painted by Juan Genovés in 1976, the year after Franco died. Genovés
    was a committed political painter, and in 1976 the clandestine democratic opposition asked him for an image
    to campaign for an amnesty for political prisoners. He gave them this: ordinary people, backs and limbs only,
    no faces at all, flinging themselves into a collective embrace. By stripping away every individual feature he
    made it belong to everyone — a whole country longing to forgive and be forgiven.
    LISTEN FOR
    It became the image of the Transition. It was turned into an Amnesty International poster, and printing it got
    Genovés himself arrested and jailed for a week. And it's bound up with one of the darkest nights of those
    years — in January 1977, far-right gunmen murdered a group of labour lawyers in their office on the Calle de
    Atocha, just up the road; a reproduction of this very painting hung on their wall. A sculpture based on it now
    stands near the spot. For decades the original sat in the museum's basement, brought out only occasionally;
    the painter felt it belonged to all democrats, not to a store-room. It was finally given pride of place, and in
    2025 it became the centrepiece of the new Transition gallery, as Spain marked fifty years since the
    dictatorship ended. Few paintings carry this much of a nation's hope. The last work on this floor is very
    different — a little further on.
