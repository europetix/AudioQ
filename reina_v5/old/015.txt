 Salvador Dalí, The Invisible Man
    WHERE YOU ARE
    A tall, busy canvas in the Dalí rooms in which, at first, you can't quite see the point — a swarm of figures,
    ruins, clouds and fragments of landscape that won't settle into a scene.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    El hombre invisible — The Invisible Man — which Dalí worked on between 1929 and 1932. Keep looking,
    and a seated human figure slowly assembles itself out of all that clutter: a cloud becomes hair, a waterfall a
    torso, a ruin a thigh. This was Dalí's first full experiment with what he called the "double image," the
    cornerstone of his paranoiac-critical method — training the eye to see one thing hidden inside another.
    LISTEN FOR
    It's deliberately hard work, and that's the point — Dalí wanted you to feel your own mind doing the
    assembling, flipping between the parts and the whole the way you do with an optical illusion. He took the
    trick from old masters of disguise like the sixteenth-century painter Arcimboldo, who built faces out of fruit
    and fish, and pushed it toward the unconscious. He never quite finished this one, which somehow suits it — a
    picture about a figure that refuses to fully appear, left forever half-emerged. Give your eyes a moment to lock
    onto the man, then watch him dissolve again. Miró's room is just along here.
