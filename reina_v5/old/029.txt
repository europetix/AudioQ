 Robert Capa, Spanish Civil War photographs
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Among the photographs in the Civil War rooms — small, grainy black-and-white prints that hit harder than
    anything painted.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    These are images by Robert Capa, the Hungarian-born photographer who, more than anyone, taught the
    world what this war looked like. He came to Spain in 1936 with his partner Gerda Taro, also a brilliant
    photographer, and threw himself at the Republican front lines — close enough that his most famous picture,
    The Falling Soldier, seems to catch a militiaman at the very instant a bullet hits him, arms flung back, rifle
    slipping from his hand.
    LISTEN FOR
    That single frame became one of the most famous war photographs ever taken — and among the most
    argued-over; historians still debate exactly when and how it was made, and whether it shows what it appears
    to show. Capa went on to define modern war photography and to coin its hardest rule — that if your pictures
    aren't good enough, you weren't close enough — before being killed by a landmine in Indochina in 1954.
    Taro had died in Spain years earlier, crushed during the retreat from a battle. Stand with these prints a
    moment. They're where the twentieth century learned to see its own wars in close-up. Now we'll leave the war
    behind, and make our way to Floor 1.
