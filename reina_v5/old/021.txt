 Salvador Dalí, The Enigma of Hitler
    WHERE YOU ARE
    A bleak, washed-out dreamscape — a vast empty beach under a heavy sky, a dripping telephone, a torn
    snapshot lying on a plate, a tattered umbrella.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    El enigma de Hitler, painted by Dalí around 1939, on the eve of the Second World War. The little torn
    photograph on the plate is of Hitler; the dead telephone, the umbrella — borrowed from the failed
    peacemaker Chamberlain — and the whole drained, anxious atmosphere make it one of Dalí's strangest and
    most unsettling pictures.
    LISTEN FOR
    Dalí claimed it came to him as a premonition, and refused to explain it cleanly — typical of him. His morbid
    fascination with Hitler scandalised his Surrealist comrades and helped get him expelled from the group; they
    were anti-fascists, and couldn't stomach his coyness. Whatever he intended, the painting captures a real
    historical dread — the held breath of Europe in 1939, waiting for catastrophe on an empty beach. Surrealism's
    most notorious collaboration wasn't a painting at all, a little further on.
