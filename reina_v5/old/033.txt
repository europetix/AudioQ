 Antoni Tàpies, matter painting
    WHERE YOU ARE
    A large surface that barely looks like a painting at all — thick, grey, scarred, more like a slab of plastered
    wall pulled off a building than anything hung in a gallery. The Reina Sofía owns Tàpies in real depth, so you
    may find several.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    This is the signature mode of Antoni Tàpies, the foremost Catalan painter of matèria — matter. He mixed
    marble dust, sand, clay and varnish into a heavy paste, troweled it onto the canvas, then scratched, gouged,
    scored and stamped it: crosses, footprints, numbers, the letter T for Tàpies, and for Teresa, his wife. The
    Catalan word tàpies even means "walls."
    LISTEN FOR
    That's the key that unlocks him. He grew up in Barcelona under Franco, in a city whose walls carried
    everything — political graffiti, bullet scars, torn posters, the marks of a population that couldn't speak freely.
    He turned the wall itself into his canvas: mute, weathered, covered in the half-erased signs of people who
    passed by and left a mark. Stand close and read it like a surface in the street rather than a picture on a wall —
    a scratch, a smear, a cross — and it stops being abstract and starts being a record. The fury of El Paso is just
    along here.
