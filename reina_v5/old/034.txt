 Antonio Saura, Sagrario
    WHERE YOU ARE
    A violent black-and-white canvas — a single figure, or a face, slashed and smeared into being with what
    looks like pure rage, the brushwork almost attacking the surface.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    This is Antonio Saura, the most ferocious of the El Paso painters, and Sagrario dates from 1960. Saura
    worked almost entirely in black, white and grey, building portraits, crucifixions and women out of furious,
    looping strokes that twist the face into something between a scream and a smear. The titles are often calm —
    a name, a saint, a lady — which only makes the violence on the canvas land harder.
    LISTEN FOR
    Saura adored Goya and Velázquez, and you can feel the lineage — this is the same Spanish darkness that runs
    through Goya's black paintings, dragged into the age of abstraction. He's not painting a person so much as the
    act of destroying and rebuilding one, again and again. In a country where you couldn't paint the dictator, you
    could paint this: a human form coming apart under pressure. Look at how the white fights the black for the
    surface. Then Millares, who took the same fury and made it three-dimensional, not far on.
