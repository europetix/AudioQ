Much of the Alcázar opens off this courtyard, sooner or later. The city's own description calls it the main courtyard, the one that leads to the palace buildings.

Its name points back to the royal hunt, la montería. The Alcázar's own notes for schools say this is where the king and his court gathered before a hunt. The Lion Gate you came in by was long known as the Puerta de la Montería, and the courtyard shares the name.

Now take your bearings. The tall decorated front facing you is number 5 on the Alcázar map, the Fachada del Palacio del Rey Don Pedro, the façade of King Pedro's palace. Pedro began his palace in 1356, and the Alcázar's map calls it the heart of the whole complex. The city compares this front to an enormous altarpiece, and you can see why: it is divided into three parts, with layer upon layer of ornament.

On your right, behind a row of columns, is number 6 on the Alcázar map, the Cuarto del Almirante, the Admiral's Quarter. For two centuries it housed the Casa de la Contratación, Spain's House of Trade with the Americas.

And in the far left-hand corner, by the palace front, a porticoed gallery leads to number 31 on the Alcázar map, the Patio del Crucero, the courtyard of the Gothic palace. You will come back to it later.

Kings, merchants and visitors have all had to cross this open space. Take a moment in the middle of it before going on.

Walk up to number 5, the Fachada del Palacio del Rey Don Pedro, close enough to see the bands of lettering high on its front.
