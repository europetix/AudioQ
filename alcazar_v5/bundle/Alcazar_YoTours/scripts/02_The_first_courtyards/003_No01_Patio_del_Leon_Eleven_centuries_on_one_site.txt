Eleven centuries is a lot to carry round a palace. Here is the short version.

It begins in 913. Abd al-Rahman the Third, then emir of Córdoba and later its first caliph, ordered a new seat of government here. In the eleventh century, Seville's own Abbadid kings had a palace here called al-Mubarak, the Blessed. The oldest wall archaeologists have found on the site belongs to that century, and it may be Almoravid.

Late in the twelfth century the Almohads built a residence here. Part of it survives a few steps away.

In 1248, after a siege of more than a year, Fernando the Third of Castile took Seville. His son, Alfonso the Tenth, called the Wise, built a Gothic palace. In the middle of the fourteenth century King Pedro the First began the palace at the heart of this visit. The Catholic Monarchs set up their House of Trade for the Americas here. Charles the Fifth was married here. And in the nineteenth century, Bourbon monarchs added and refurbished the rooms upstairs.

In 1931 the new Republic handed the Alcázar to the city of Seville, which still runs it through its own board, the Patronato. Yet the upper floor remains the King's official residence when he is in Seville.

So as you walk, ask of each wall: whose is this? The answer is usually more than one name.

Number 2 on the Alcázar map, the Sala de la Justicia, opens off the far left-hand corner of this courtyard as you face the triple arch: a small square hall with a fountain set in its floor.
