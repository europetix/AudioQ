For more than sixty years, this courtyard was a theatre. People crowded in here to watch plays, until a fire in 1691 brought the show to an end.

First, the gate you just came through. It was long taken to be Almohad, from the twelfth century. Archaeology says otherwise. Miguel Ángel Tabales and his team found that the gate, the wall beside it and the casing of its tower went up in a single campaign, in the time of King Pedro the First, in the middle of the fourteenth century. A radiocarbon sample from the wall points the same way.

The name came later. For centuries this was the Puerta de la Montería, the gate of the royal hunt. People began calling it the Lion Gate after the tile lion over the arch. Who made that lion, and when, has not been established.

Inside, the courtyard had a second life. From the 1620s a playhouse stood here, the Corral de la Montería, one of the city's theatres. In May 1691 a fire broke out in the stables next door and destroyed it. Stables, storerooms and houses followed. In the 1930s, to designs by the architect Juan Talavera, most of them were cleared away to make the entrance you used today.

Now look at the triple arch that leads on. Its three openings are not evenly set out. The middle arch has been shifted to one side, which is why one side of the wall is wider than the other. One reading is that this was deliberate, to line up a direct view from the Lion Gate to the door of Pedro's new palace.

So the first thing you walked through is not a relic of the Almohads. It is a king's work, perhaps aimed at his own front door.
