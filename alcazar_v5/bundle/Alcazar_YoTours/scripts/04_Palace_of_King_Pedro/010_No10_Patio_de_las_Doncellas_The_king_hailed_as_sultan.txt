In this palace, a Christian king of Castile is called a sultan. Over and over, in Arabic, on almost every lower wall panel in the building.

The words read: "Glory to our lord the sultan Don Pedro, may God help him." The historian Julie Marquer has traced them across the palace. Wherever you see bands of Arabic lettering low on these walls, that greeting is likely to be among them. Pedro built this palace from the 1350s and finished it in the 1360s.

His reign was short and violent. He became king at fifteen, and much of his reign was spent at war, in the end against his own half-brother, Enrique. Pedro lost his throne, won it back, and in 1369, at Montiel, he was killed. By most accounts, Enrique struck the blow himself. Enrique's family, the Trastámaras, took the crown.

So history gave Pedro two nicknames. His enemies called him the Cruel, and among their charges was that he favoured Muslims and Jews. Others called him the Just. Both names are propaganda, from two sides of a civil war.

What happened next is stranger. Pedro was buried far from here, in the church of Puebla de Alcocer, in Extremadura. Seventy-seven years later he was moved to a convent in Madrid, Santo Domingo el Real. When that convent was demolished, his remains went to the National Archaeological Museum. Only then did they come to Seville Cathedral, where they lie today.

The king hailed as sultan on these walls took centuries to come home.
