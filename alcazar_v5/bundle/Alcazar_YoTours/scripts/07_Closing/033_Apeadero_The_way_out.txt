You are leaving the Alcázar by its old front door. For a long time, this hall was the way in.

It was built in 1607 by Vermondo Resta, the same architect who dressed up the painted Grutesco wall by the Mercury pool. An apeadero is a place to step down from a horse or a carriage. And from the seventeenth century, this became the palace's main entrance. Look at the way it is laid out: three great spaces, divided by columns carrying round arches. One study points out that it is planned less like a civil building than like a church. Today it does the opposite job. It is the visitors' way out.

Beyond it lies the Patio de Banderas, in the oldest walled enclosure of the Alcázar. The walls around that square belong to the Islamic period. Its enclosure wall is the oldest archaeologists have found here, probably from the eleventh century, possibly built by the Almoravids. And deeper still, about five metres down, excavations found a Roman building from the first century before Christ.

So think back over the walk. An Almohad courtyard of plaster lattice. Alfonso the Tenth's Gothic palace and its sunken garden. King Pedro's palace, where an Arabic inscription calls a Christian king sultan. A painting of the navigators, in the house that licensed the Atlantic trade, the slave trade included. An emperor's wedding under a gilded dome. Renaissance gardens, and newer ones beyond. Every age kept what it found, and added its own.

The story goes on outside. In the Cathedral lie the remains of King Pedro, who took centuries to come back to Seville. And across the plaza, the Archivo de Indias keeps the papers of the Casa de la Contratación.

Thank you for walking these eleven centuries with us. Enjoy Seville.
