The tapestries in this hall are copies. And that was the whole point of making them.

In 1535 Charles the Fifth led a great expedition across the sea against the corsair Barbarossa, who had seized Tunis. The painter Jan Cornelisz Vermeyen went with him and recorded the campaign as an eyewitness. Vermeyen's pictures, with Pieter Coecke's help, became the designs for twelve enormous tapestries. Willem de Pannemaker wove them in Brussels over six years, in wool and silk, with gold and silver thread.

Ten of those originals survive, in the Spanish royal collection. In the eighteenth century, King Felipe the Fifth wanted them preserved. So he turned to the Vandergoten family, weavers he had brought from Antwerp to run a new royal tapestry works in Madrid, and had them weave a full replica set. The tapestries around you come from that set. They are royal property too.

Now follow them along the walls. Ships and coastline, camps and fighting, scene after scene, the way the emperor wanted the story told. Pick one panel and find the smallest figures in it.

And remember what these pictures are for. They were made to show Charles as the defender of the Christian faith. His army took Tunis, then sacked the city, and handed it back to its deposed ruler, Mulay Hasan, who now ruled under Spanish protection. The series does not hide the sack: one of its twelve scenes shows it. But every scene is framed as a victory for the faith.
