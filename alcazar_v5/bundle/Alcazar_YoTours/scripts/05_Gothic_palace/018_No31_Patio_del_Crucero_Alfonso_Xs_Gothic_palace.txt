The palace you have just left belonged to a king who was hailed in Arabic as a sultan. This one was built by his great-great-grandfather, and it speaks another language. Gothic.

Castile took Seville in 1248. In the thirteenth century, Alfonso the Tenth, known as Alfonso the Wise, built a Gothic palace here, over the remains of the Almohad palace. He is remembered as a king of learning. He is credited with the Cantigas de Santa María, hundreds of songs to the Virgin, and he gathered scholars to translate and to write in Castilian. The Alcázar's own map sums up his palace in one line. It staged, it says, the drastic transition to the new Christian order.

But this courtyard is older than his palace. According to the same map, much of the Patio del Crucero belongs to the Islamic period. It had a lower level, and that still survives below you. It is the space now called the Baths, number 34 on the Alcázar map, a sunken garden whose vaults Alfonso's builders made Gothic. You will go down into it later, from the gardens.

Then came the eighteenth century. The Lisbon earthquake of 1755 damaged the Gothic palace. It was repaired. Its great hall, number 32 on the Alcázar map, the Salón de los Tapices, lost its old vaulted ceiling, which was replaced with late Baroque arches. And the entrance was given a stone Baroque portico. Look for that Baroque front. It is the way into the Gothic halls.

So this one courtyard holds three ages at once. Islamic underneath, Gothic within, and an eighteenth-century face.

Through the portico on the Gothic palace side of this courtyard, into number 32, the Salón de los Tapices: a great hall hung with huge tapestries of a sea campaign.
