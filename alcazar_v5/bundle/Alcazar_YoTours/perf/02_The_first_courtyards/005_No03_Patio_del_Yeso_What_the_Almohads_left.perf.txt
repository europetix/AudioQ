[quietly] King Pedro lived in this courtyard before his famous palace was ready. In 1358 this was his residence. And even then, it was old.

This is what remains of an Almohad residence from around 1190. The Almohads were a Berber dynasty from North Africa who ruled al-Andalus from the twelfth century into the early thirteenth. They also built the minaret we now call the Giralda. The Alcázar's own map simply calls this the Palacio del Yeso, from the Islamic period.

Most of their palace is gone. Later palaces swept much of it away. But this courtyard survived. Forgotten for centuries, it was rediscovered in 1855, and restored twice in the twentieth century.

[curious] Now look at the portico. Above the arches, a net of interlaced arches climbs the wall, each one flowing into the next. This pattern is called sebka. It is the same family of ornament you can see high on the Giralda. Below it, a long pool fills most of the courtyard, with a small garden area on each side.

The richness here is all in the pattern itself, repeated until the wall seems woven.

[warmly] Picture Pedro here, a young Christian king of Castile, living under Almohad arches. Soon his own palace, a few steps away, would speak the same language. The Alcázar's school notes call this courtyard a forerunner of the Mudéjar style. You are standing in the first draft.

Back the way you came, through number 2, the Sala de la Justicia. The triple arch of number 1, the Patio del León, is on your left as you come out. Go through it into number 4 on the Alcázar map, the Patio de la Montería: the big open courtyard, with a tall decorated palace front facing you across it.
