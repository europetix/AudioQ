[curious] When restorers worked here in 2017, archaeologists found an older story hiding in the walls of the pool.

The pavilion itself is from the seventeenth century. It went up between 1644 and 1645, to designs by Diego Martín Orejuela. Juan de Medina painted its rich decoration, and Benito Valladares supplied the tiles. Its name is simple. It is the Cenador del León, the lion's pavilion, because it stands right behind the pool that the lion presides over.

[pause] But the pool is older than the pavilion. The excavation found a fourteenth-century phase, and an enlargement in the sixteenth century. Once the pavilion was finished, the pool was rebuilt entirely to go with it.

Then came the surprise. On two walls of the old walkway round the pool, the team found traces of late medieval heraldic decoration in the plaster. The archaeologists, Miguel Ángel Tabales and Cristina Vargas, date it very probably to the time of King Pedro, the builder of the palace you walked through earlier. [quietly] They are careful to call this a hypothesis. But if they are right, the walls round the pool are some three centuries older than the pavilion behind it.

So look past the pavilion to the pool. The seventeenth-century garden room is the newcomer here. The walls round the water may be much older.

Number 46 on the Alcázar map, the Jardín Inglés, the garden of winding paths beyond this pavilion, has a short Extra on the twentieth-century gardens. The next main stop is number 51 on the Alcázar map, the Puerta de Marchena: a Gothic stone gateway standing in the garden, with carved wild men holding shields.
