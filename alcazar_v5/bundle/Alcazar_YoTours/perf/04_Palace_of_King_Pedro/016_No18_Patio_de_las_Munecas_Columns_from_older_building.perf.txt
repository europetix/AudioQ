[amused] There are no dolls in the Patio de las Muñecas, the Courtyard of the Dolls. There are faces.

The Alcázar says the name comes from little heads carved where the arch nearest the entrance springs from its columns. They are tiny. School groups visiting the Alcázar are told to look up and find them. [pause] It is worth doing the same.

This small courtyard was the private heart of King Pedro's palace, with the private rooms arranged around it. Now look at the columns. They are older than the palace. Their shafts and capitals were brought from earlier buildings, some from the age of the caliphs of Córdoba, some Roman, and reused here for their beauty.

Not everything is as old as it looks. The domestic rooms around the courtyard were heavily remodelled under the Catholic Monarchs. And the two upper floors above the arches were added in the nineteenth century.

[quietly] Legend likes to put a woman here: María de Padilla, Pedro's companion and the mother of his children. But she died in Seville in 1361, before this palace was finished. Soon after her death, Pedro declared that he had married her before his marriage to Blanche of Bourbon, and he had their children recognised. Their daughter Constanza later married an English prince, John of Gaunt.

Number 19 on the Alcázar map, the stair to the Cuarto Real Alto, is marked back in the Patio de la Montería, in the corner where the palace front meets the Cuarto del Almirante. The upper royal rooms are a separate timed visit, and two short optional stops for them are at the end of this guide.

The Cuarto del Príncipe, beside this courtyard, has a short Extra on the rediscovered passage to the gardens. The next main stop is number 31 on the Alcázar map, the Patio del Crucero, the courtyard of the Gothic palace. Follow the signs for the Palacio Gótico: back through the Patio de las Doncellas and out of the palace door into the Patio de la Montería. Turn right, keep the palace front on your right, and walk to the corner. The way through to number 31 is there.
