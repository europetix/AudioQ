[curious] On the 22nd of August 2002, archaeologists digging in this courtyard began to find something no one living had ever seen: King Pedro's garden.

The team was led by Miguel Ángel Tabales. Under the later marble paving and a sixteenth-century level, they found walks, a pool and planting beds, laid out as a sunken garden. It had been hidden for more than four hundred years.

How did a royal garden disappear? By 1584 the pool was gone. Under Felipe the Second the courtyard was paved over, with a fountain in the middle. The arcades were remodelled too, and the columns you see around you are Renaissance ones, put up in the sixteenth century in place of the originals.

[pause] Then comes the twist. The scholars Antonio Almagro and Luis Ramón-Laca think the garden was buried soon after Pedro's murder. As far as they can tell, it was never used. It may never even have been finished.

[warmly] So look down at it now. A long pool runs down the middle, with a sunken bed on each side, about a metre below the walkway. The Alcázar's own Patronato restored it after the excavations, with the work completed in 2005.

And the name, the Courtyard of the Maidens? It comes from a medieval legend of a tribute of a hundred maidens. It is only a legend.

[quietly] So this may be a garden King Pedro planned, and never walked in.

Number 13 on the Alcázar map, the Salón del Techo de Carlos Quinto, has a short Extra on the palace chapel and its Renaissance ceiling. Its door opens off this courtyard. The next main stop is number 15 on the Alcázar map, the Salón de los Embajadores, the hall under the gilded dome.
