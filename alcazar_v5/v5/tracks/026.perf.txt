[wry] This gallery is wearing a costume. Underneath the columns and the colour is a plain old wall of the Islamic Alcázar.

In the early seventeenth century, the man in charge of the Alcázar's works was an architect called Vermondo Resta. Between 1613 and 1621 he took this stretch of old wall and turned it into a piece of garden theatre. He kept the wall, and he dressed it in the fashion of Renaissance Italy. That is the Grutesco. The name comes from the Italian word for a grotto, and from the fantastic style of painting that went with it.

Then the painters arrived. Diego de Esquivel spent seven years covering the wall with colour, and he finished in 1629. Think of the timing. The Mercury in the pool had been standing for half a century, and now he had a painted backdrop.

[pause] Look along the wall. The Alcázar's own restoration dossier lists what Esquivel painted: figures and scenes from classical mythology, angels, and exotic birds. See how many you can pick out. By 2023 the paintings were in a critical state, and in July that year a restoration began.

[warmly] Resta's name will come back before you leave. Near the end of the visit you walk out through another of his buildings, number 53 on the Alcázar map, the Apeadero. So this old wall in costume and the hall at the way out were built by the same man.

Number 41 on the Alcázar map, the Fuente de la Fama, has a short Extra on its water organ. It is set into this same long wall, beyond the end of the pool. The next main stop is number 44 on the Alcázar map, the Pabellón de Carlos Quinto: a small square tiled pavilion at the far end of the formal gardens that stretch away from this wall.
