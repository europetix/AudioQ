[curious] In 1340, at the Río Salado, Alfonso the Eleventh of Castile and Afonso the Fourth of Portugal defeated a great Marinid army from Morocco. It was the Marinids' last attempt to invade the peninsula, and the last time Castile and Portugal fought them side by side.

This hall was built in Alfonso's time, in the fourteenth century, possibly to mark that victory. Nobody can prove the link. But the room tells another story, and that one is certain.

Alfonso was a Christian king, and he was Pedro's father. Yet look at what he chose: a square hall in the Islamic tradition, its walls covered in plasterwork of plant-like patterns and heraldic shields. The city's own description calls it the earliest Mudéjar building in the Alcázar. Mudéjar is the word for this mix: Islamic forms of art, made for Christian patrons. So a Castilian king's hall speaks the artistic language of al-Andalus.

[pause] Then look down, at the fountain set into the floor. Water in the middle of a room, a piece of garden brought indoors.

[warmly] Within a generation, his son Pedro would take this taste and build a whole palace in it. This small room is where that story starts.

Through the far side of this hall to number 3 on the Alcázar map, the Patio del Yeso: a lattice of arches over a long pool.
