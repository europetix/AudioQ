[reverent] The men who ran the Atlantic trade wanted protection, so they commissioned it in paint. The Virgin opens her cloak over the people of the voyages, their ships on the sea below.

The painting was made between 1531 and 1536 for this room, the Casa's audience hall, which was also its chapel. It is now attributed to Alejo Fernández, a Seville painter of German origin. It is also called the Virgen de los Mareantes, of the Seafarers. The Alcázar's map says Navegantes, the Navigators.

[curious] So who is under the cloak? Tradition has names for them. The man kneeling in gold is often said to be Columbus, though some see King Fernando. A young man in a red hat is usually taken for one of the Pinzón brothers. A red-haired man is often identified as Charles the Fifth. A man in black may be Hernán Cortés. And an old man with a cane is sometimes called Amerigo Vespucci, or else Sancho de Matienzo. Every one of these names is disputed.

[pause] Now look past them, into the background. Indigenous men and women of the Americas stand there too, in simple white wraps. The historian Carla Rahn Phillips links that dress to adult baptism. On the sea below: a big carrack, caravels, a galley and smaller oared craft. On the side panels are Saint Sebastian, Saint James, Saint Elmo, and Saint John on Patmos.

Phillips reads the altarpiece as the official picture of a Christian empire overseas. She also notes that the painter himself reportedly owned slaves, both Indian and African.

[quietly] It is one of the earliest European paintings tied to the Atlantic voyages. Almost everyone in front has been given a name by someone. The people in the background have none.

Back across number 4, the Patio de la Montería, and through the palace door in number 5, the façade, into number 10 on the Alcázar map, the Patio de las Doncellas: a long pool between sunken garden beds.
