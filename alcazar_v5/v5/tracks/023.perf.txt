[warmly] Long before King Pedro, Seville had a king who wrote poetry. His name was al-Mu'tamid, and number 36, the Columna de Al-Mutamid, remembers him here in his own city.

He lived in the eleventh century and was the third and last of the Abbadid kings of Seville. The Alcázar's own history says their palace here was called al-Mubarak, the Blessed. It was the centre of the city's official and literary life, with poets such as the sovereign himself.

The story most often told is about his wife, I'timad. She was a young poet he met by the river. He bought her freedom and married her. [amused] Once, the story goes, she wept with delight at a rare snowfall in Córdoba. So he had almond trees planted on the hills, so that every spring their blossom would look like snow.

[quietly] It did not end well. The Andalusian kings had called in the Almoravids from North Africa to fight Castile. After their great victory in 1086, the Almoravids turned on the kings who had invited them. Seville fell. Al-Mu'tamid surrendered to spare his family, and he was banished to Aghmat, near Marrakesh. He died there in exile in 1095, and that is where his remains lie.

[pause] The gardens around you began in that Islamic world. They were redesigned in the Renaissance under Charles the Fifth and Felipe the Second, and they have never stopped changing. Today they hold more than twenty thousand plants of at least 187 species.

This garden is also where the restored passage from the Patio de las Muñecas comes out, open again since 2025.

Back along the garden front past number 37 on your Alcázar map, the Fuente de Troya, to number 38, the Jardín de la Danza: two columns stand on its upper level.
