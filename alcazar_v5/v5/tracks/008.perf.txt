[curious] For two hundred years, if you wanted to sail legally from Spain to the Americas, your papers passed through this house.

It began with a royal order. On the 20th of January 1503, at Alcalá de Henares, the Catholic Monarchs founded the Casa de la Contratación, the House of Trade, in Seville. The first plan was to put it in the city's shipyards, the Atarazanas, by the river. That was rejected: they were too damp, and goods would spoil. So the new office moved into the Alcázar, into these rooms, then called the Cuarto de los Almirantes. It stayed here until 1717, when it moved to Cádiz.

What did it do? Almost everything the Atlantic trade needed. It dealt with trade with the Americas and with fitting out and dispatching the fleets. It licensed ships, cargoes and passengers. Its reach took in shipbuilding, navigation and mapmaking.

[quietly] That traffic also included human beings. Enslaved Africans were shipped to the Americas under royal licence, and the Crown required those shipments to be registered with the officials of this house. It is part of the story of these rooms, and it should be said plainly.

[pause] The Casa's papers did not stay here. Today they are in the Archivo de Indias, across the plaza, among the great archives of the Spanish empire in the Americas.

[warmly] For two centuries, then, a royal palace was also the office of an ocean. And the officials who met here commissioned a painting that still hangs in their old audience hall.

Through to number 7 on the Alcázar map, the Capilla de la Virgen de los Navegantes, the old audience hall: the Virgin spreading her cloak over a crowd, with ships at her feet.
