[curious] When restorers worked on this pavilion in 2017, the archaeologists found an older story hiding in its walls.

The pavilion itself is from the seventeenth century. It went up between 1644 and 1645, to designs by Diego Martín Orejuela. Juan de Medina painted its rich decoration, and Benito Valladares supplied the tiles. Its name is simple. It is the Cenador del León, the lion's pavilion, because it stands right behind the pool that the lion presides over.

[pause] But the pool is older than the pavilion. The excavation found a fourteenth-century phase, and an enlargement in the sixteenth century. Once the pavilion was finished, the pool was rebuilt entirely to go with it.

Then came the surprise. Under the plaster of the pavilion, the team found traces of late medieval heraldic decoration. The archaeologists, Miguel Ángel Tabales and Cristina Vargas, date it very probably to the time of King Pedro, the builder of the palace you walked through earlier. [quietly] They are careful to call this a hypothesis. Only two walls showed it. But if they are right, parts of these walls are some three centuries older than the pavilion itself.

So look at the pavilion behind the lion. On the surface it is a seventeenth-century garden room. Underneath, it may hold something much older.

Number 46 on your Alcázar map, the Jardín Inglés, has a short Extra on the twentieth-century gardens. The next main stop is number 51 on your Alcázar map, the Puerta de Marchena: a Gothic stone gateway standing in the garden, with carved wild men holding shields.
