[warmly] Above this gate, a lion made of tiles keeps watch. It gives the entrance its name, the Puerta del León, the Lion Gate. And behind it is a palace that has been a seat of power for eleven centuries.

The word alcázar comes from the Arabic al-qasr, the palace or fortress. In 913, Abd al-Rahman the Third, then emir of Córdoba, ordered a new seat of government here. Ruler after ruler built on this ground, and much of their work survives side by side. You will see part of an Almohad courtyard, a Gothic palace from the thirteenth century, and King Pedro's Mudéjar palace from the fourteenth. Then come Renaissance gardens, and gardens laid out in the early twentieth century.

[pause] It is still a royal palace. The Alcázar calls itself the oldest royal palace still in use in Europe, and its upper floor is the Royal Family's official residence in Seville. Together with the Cathedral and the Archivo de Indias, it is a World Heritage Site.

Now, how this guide works. Almost every stop has a number. These are the numbers on the Alcázar's own map, 1 to 53, and the route map that comes with this guide uses the same numbers. The signs on site use the Spanish names, so you will always hear the number with its Spanish name.

There are three lengths. The full tour takes about two hours, the essential version about an hour and a quarter, and the highlights about forty-five minutes, all with walking. Whichever version is on your phone, play it in order. The numbers may jump, and every stop tells you where to go next. Some stops have a short Extra, and we will always say so first. The Cuarto Real Alto, the upper royal rooms, is a separate timed visit, with two short optional tracks at the end of this guide.

Through the gate, into number 1 on the Alcázar map, the Patio del León: the courtyard just inside, with a triple arch leading on.
