[curious] The gold over your head is not King Pedro's. It went up about sixty years after this hall was finished.

This was the heart of Pedro's palace, his main hall and his throne room. Here he received the important people of his time. The city's own description reads the room like a Muslim qubba. The square floor stands for the earth. The dome stands for the universe. And a band of carved muqarnas, folded into a star, joins the square to the circle.

[pause] The dome is called the media naranja, the half orange, and the room was long known as the Hall of the Half Orange. A master named Diego Ruiz made it in 1427. It replaced an earlier dome from Pedro's own time. So you are standing in a fourteenth-century room under a fifteenth-century sky.

How do we know when the hall itself was finished? It tells us. An Arabic inscription on the doors of this hall records that the building of this fortress was completed by the grace of God. And it gives the year twice. Once in the old Spanish era, the year 1404. Once in the Muslim reckoning, the year 767. In our calendar, that is 1366. [wry] A Christian king, dating his throne room in Arabic, in two calendars at once.

Later kings added their own layers. The wrought-iron balconies high on the walls were made under Felipe the Second, at the end of the sixteenth century. Around the same time, the painter Diego de Esquivel filled the frieze below the dome with portraits of the monarchs of Spain. Pedro's hall became a gallery of the whole royal line.

[quietly] One night in this room mattered more than all the others.

Stay in number 15, the Salón de los Embajadores. Picture it on a night in March, almost a century after Diego Ruiz finished the dome.
