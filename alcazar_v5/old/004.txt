The Hall of Justice
OBJECT



 STOP & LOOK
 Step into the small square room on the left of the Patio del León — the Sala de la Justicia, the Hall of
 Justice. It's a compact, beautifully decorated room with a marble fountain in the floor, stucco
 plasterwork on the walls, and a coffered wooden ceiling above.


This room was built to commemorate a victory in battle.

The battle was the Battle of Río Salado, fought on the thirtieth of October, thirteen forty, near
Tarifa on the southern coast of Spain. King Alfonso the Eleventh of Castile, allied with King
Afonso the Fourth of Portugal, defeated a much larger Marinid army that had crossed from
Morocco to support the Nasrid emir of Granada. It was the last major Moroccan invasion of the
Iberian Peninsula. After Salado, no significant African Muslim army would cross into Spain again.
The Reconquista was, from that moment, no longer threatened from the south. It would only end
one way.

Alfonso the Eleventh built this room around the year thirteen forty to celebrate that victory. Look
up at the ceiling — that's a Mudéjar artesonado, an interlaced wooden lacework called lacería,
polychromed and gilded, organized around the geometry of the eight-pointed star. The walls
below are covered in plasterwork — yeserías — with intertwined floral motifs, Arabic-style
inscriptions in cursive script, and the heraldic shield of the Order of the Band, which Alfonso the
Eleventh himself founded in thirteen thirty.

The floor is paved with terracotta tiles, with a small white marble fountain set flush into the
center. Water from the fountain flows out through a narrow channel into the next courtyard — the
Patio del Yeso. We're heading there in a moment. The fountain and channel are not just
decoration. They're a deliberate architectural quotation of Andalusi palace traditions, where
running water linked interior rooms to exterior gardens, blurring the line between the two.

For a Christian king's throne room of justice, built to celebrate a Christian victory over a Muslim
army, the decoration is entirely Islamic in form and inspiration. This is the central paradox of the
Mudéjar style. The conqueror, in his moment of triumph, chose to express that triumph in the
visual language of the conquered. There are several theories about why. The simplest is that the
Castilian kings genuinely admired Andalusi architecture more than any Christian architecture
available to them in the fourteenth century. The cathedrals being built in northern Spain —
Burgos, León — would have felt foreign and cold to a king who had grown up in Sevilla, where
the local building tradition was thoroughly Islamic.
