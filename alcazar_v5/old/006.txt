The Hunters' Courtyard and the Facade of 1364
★★ HERO



 STOP & LOOK
 Walk through the three large arches at the end of the Patio del León. You enter a much larger
 rectangular courtyard — the Patio de la Montería, the Hunters' Courtyard. Stop in the middle of it. In
 front of you, the great facade of Pedro the First's palace. To your right, the Casa de la Contratación.
 To your left, the entrance to the Gothic Palace. The facade of Pedro's palace is the one you came to
 see.


There. That's the photograph everyone takes.

Take it. Take it again. Take it from a different angle. Then come back to me, because I want to
tell you what you're looking at, because what you're looking at is one of the strangest objects in
the history of European architecture.

This is the main facade of the palace of Pedro the First of Castile, built between thirteen
sixty-four and thirteen sixty-six. The king who built it was Christian. He was a Catholic monarch in
the line of Fernando the Third and Alfonso the Tenth — descended from the very kings who had
taken Sevilla back from Muslim rule a hundred and sixteen years earlier. He had been crowned
in front of Christian altars. He waged wars in the name of Christ. He was, by every formal
measure, a Christian king.

And he commissioned a facade that reads, from top to bottom, like a Nasrid Muslim palace.

Let me show you what's actually carved in the stucco above your head. Look up at the upper
band, the one running along the top of the central portico. It's a long inscription, partly in Castilian
Gothic letters and partly in Arabic Kufic script. The Castilian portion declares — and I'll quote it
short — "the very high and very noble and very powerful Don Pedro, by the grace of God King of
Castiella and León, commanded these Alcázares and these palaces". So far, so Christian. A king
named Pedro, by the grace of the Christian God, claiming credit.

But the Arabic Kufic inscription, repeated four times in each direction, says something else. It
says wa-l■ gh■liba ill■ All■h. "There is no victor but Allah." That phrase wasn't Pedro's
invention. It was the official motto of the Nasrid dynasty of Granada — the last Muslim kingdom
in Spain, the one whose Alhambra palace was being decorated in exactly the same decades, in
exactly the same style, by exactly the same workshops. The Nasrid sultan Muhammad the Fifth
— Pedro's close ally and friend — repeats that phrase, in identical Kufic script, all over the
Alhambra.

So Pedro, a Catholic king, in his own royal palace, in the heart of his own kingdom, in his
moment of architectural triumph, chose to inscribe on his palace facade the motto of a Muslim
dynasty crediting all victory to Allah.

This wasn't done by accident. Pedro and Muhammad the Fifth were genuine allies, more than
once. They came to each other's military aid in their respective civil wars. When Pedro decided to
build a new palace in thirteen sixty-four, Muhammad sent him his best craftsmen — Granadan
stucco workers, tile setters, carpenters who had just finished work on the Court of the Lions in
the Alhambra. They were joined by Mudéjar craftsmen from Toledo and from Sevilla itself, who
had been working in this style for the local Castilian aristocracy for over a century. Together, they
built this facade.

Look at the details now. The horseshoe arches. The sebka latticework — those interlaced
diamond motifs above the arches, same as the Patio del Yeso, same as the Giralda. The blind
polylobed arches alternating with the openings. The wooden eave on top, carved into muqarnas
— the stalactite-like wooden honeycomb that's a signature of Islamic architecture. The blue and
white Kufic tile band. The proportions, three-three-three, exactly mirroring the upper galleries of
the Comares Palace in the Alhambra.

There is a great deal of ink that has been spilled trying to explain why Pedro did this. The
cleanest answer is the most personal one. Pedro grew up in this city. The buildings he loved
most were Andalusi. The aesthetics he wanted around him were Andalusi. He had Muslim and
Jewish advisors at his court. He signed mutual defense treaties with the Nasrid emir. The
accusation of his Christian rivals — that he was too friendly with Muslims and Jews — is one of
the reasons his enemies called him Pedro the Cruel. To them, his cosmopolitan tastes were
treason. To him, this facade was simply what beauty looked like.

He died for it, in a way. In thirteen sixty-nine, three years after this palace was finished, Pedro
was murdered by his half-brother Enrique de Trastámara at Montiel — a death that ended the
original Castilian Borgoña dynasty and started the Trastámara line that would lead, eventually, to
Isabel the Catholic and her grandson Carlos the Fifth. Pedro is buried in the cathedral's royal
chapel just across the plaza, in the crypt below the floor of the Capilla Real. The half-brother who
killed him replaced him on the throne. But the palace Pedro built has outlasted them all.

Stand here and look at this facade for as long as you want. It's the heart of the Mudéjar style in
all of Spain, and arguably the most consequential thing Pedro left behind. Then we'll explore the
building it leads to.

But before we go inside Pedro's palace, there's another wing of the Alcázar we should see — on
your right, the doorway leads into rooms that, for two hundred years, ran the entire Spanish
empire.
