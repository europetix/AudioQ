TRACK 11
The Hall of Ambassadors
★★ HERO



 STOP & LOOK
 Walk through the doorway at the far end of the Patio de las Doncellas. You enter a square room with
 triple horseshoe arches on three of its four walls, walls covered in tile and plaster, and — above you
 — a gilded wooden dome that fills the entire ceiling. This is the Salón de los Embajadores, the Hall of
 Ambassadors. Look up.


This was Pedro the First's throne room. And, almost certainly, it is the most spectacular single
room in Spain.

The dome above you was carved and gilded by a master named Diego Ruiz in fourteen
twenty-seven — sixty years after Pedro built the room beneath it. It replaced an earlier wooden
dome that Pedro had originally installed, which had deteriorated. Diego Ruiz's dome is the one
you see today. The Spanish call its shape a media naranja — half an orange — and the room
itself is sometimes called the Sala de la Media Naranja, the Hall of the Half Orange, for that
reason.

It is, technically, a sixteen-sided polygonal dome interlaced with carved gilded wooden patterns
called lacería — ten-fold knot patterns, the most complex in the woodcarver's repertoire. There
are only four other domes in the world that use this specific ten-fold knot construction. All four are
in Spain.

The dome is sixteen meters high. The walls below it are covered with what is, by mass, the
densest concentration of medieval Andalusi decoration anywhere outside the Alhambra. Tile
dadoes — alicatados — at the lower level, two meters tall, made of cut interlocking tile pieces in
geometric patterns. Above the tile, carved stucco yeserías covering every square centimeter —
vegetal patterns, geometric panels, inscriptions in Arabic Kufic and cursive script, Castilian coats
of arms in stucco. Wooden balconies — the mirador galleries above the doors — were added at
the end of the sixteenth century to allow the Habsburg royal family to watch ceremonies from
above.

The room is square. It opens, through triple horseshoe arches supported by paired marble
columns, into three different antechambers on its north, west, and east sides. The fourth side —
the south, the original qibla wall, the side that would have faced Mecca in the earlier Abbadid hall
called Al-Turayya that may have stood on this exact spot — was kept solid by Pedro's architects.
The orientation of the room was changed in the thirteen sixties. Whatever Muslim throne hall
preceded this one faced east toward Mecca. Pedro's hall opens north, toward the Patio de las
Doncellas. The change was deliberate. The new room belonged to a Christian king.

But the room is named for a function it acquired later — not Pedro's throne room, but the
ambassadors' reception hall. From the late fourteenth century onward, when Castilian and later
Spanish monarchs received foreign delegations in Sevilla, they did so here. The ambassadors
from the Holy Roman Empire, from England, from the Italian states, from the Aztec emperor's
heirs after the conquest of Mexico — all stood under this dome.

Look at the gothic frieze just below the dome. The painted wooden panels on it are portraits of
fifty-six Spanish kings, from the Visigothic ruler Chindasvinto in the seventh century to King
Felipe the Third in the early sixteen hundreds. The cycle was painted by Diego de Esquivel
between fifteen ninety-nine and sixteen hundred. Each king is shown seated, crowned, holding a
sword in one hand and a globe in the other. Below each portrait, the king's coat of arms. Below
the coat of arms, the dates of his reign. Pedro the First — the builder of this room — appears on
the south wall, painted with the rest. He sits among the kings as one of them, neither glorified nor
erased. Just present.

Now — one historical moment that happened in this exact room. It changed Spain.

Just after midnight on the morning of March eleventh, fifteen twenty-six, a wedding took place
under this dome. The wedding of an emperor and his cousin. We're going to spend the next track
entirely on that night, because it was one of the most consequential weddings in European
history and you're standing exactly where it happened.
