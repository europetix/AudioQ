Welcome back.

If you've just come from the cathedral, you're about to do something extraordinary — you're
about to walk from the largest Gothic cathedral on earth into the oldest royal palace still in use
anywhere in Europe. Two UNESCO World Heritage sites, on the same plaza, both inscribed in
the same year, both shaped by the same thousand years of Andalusian history. You picked the
right city.

If this is your first stop in Sevilla — also welcome. The cathedral can wait until later. This building
has its own story to tell, and it tells it differently than anything else in Spain.

What you're standing in front of is called the Real Alcázar — the Royal Palace. The word alcázar
comes from the Arabic al-qasr, meaning fortified palace or large stone building from which power
is projected. There are several alcázares in Spain, but this is the great one. The original. Walls
behind those red battlements were first laid down in nine hundred and thirteen, more than eleven
hundred years ago. The place has been continuously occupied — first by Muslim rulers, then by
Christian kings, now by a constitutional monarchy — every year since. When King Felipe the
Sixth and his family visit Sevilla, they still stay in the upper rooms. You can sometimes tell, from
the queue, if they're in residence — those days the visitor numbers get tightened.

Now, here's the unusual thing about this palace, and the reason it matters.

Almost every other great medieval building in Europe expresses a single moment in time.
Notre-Dame is Gothic. The Doge's Palace in Venice is Venetian Gothic. The Tower of London is
Norman. They each crystallized in one period and stayed there. But the Real Alcázar is different.
It is a thousand-year palimpsest, with every era layered visibly on top of the last. Twelfth-century
Almohad walls. A fourteenth-century Mudéjar palace built by a Christian king using Muslim
craftsmen. A thirteenth-century Gothic palace by Alfonso the Wise. A Renaissance dome over a
Muslim throne hall. Sixteenth-century apartments where the Holy Roman Emperor got married.
Eighteenth-century repairs after the Lisbon earthquake. Nineteenth-century English-style
gardens. Twentieth-century restorations that uncovered things hidden for four hundred years.

You're not visiting a building. You're visiting a stack of buildings, each one preserved inside the
next.

Here's how we'll walk it. Twenty tracks. About an hour and a half of audio. We'll enter through the
Puerta del León, pass through the Patio del León, see the oldest surviving Almohad fragments,
then enter the Patio de la Montería where the great Mudéjar facade of Pedro the First is the
headline event of any visit. From there we'll go inside Pedro's palace — the Patio de las
Doncellas, the Patio de las Muñecas, the gilded dome of the Hall of Ambassadors — then
through the upper Gothic palace of Carlos the Fifth, and finally out into the gardens. Plan two and
a half to three hours total inside.

A note before we start. The Alcázar is busier than the cathedral. There's a cap of seven hundred
and fifty people inside at any one time, and they enforce it. If you booked a timed entry, your slot
matters. Have your ticket ready. Then take a breath, walk through that gate, and let the eleven
hundred years begin.
