TRACK 17
The Mercury Pond, the Grotesco Gallery, and the
Pavilion
OBJECT



 STOP & LOOK
 Walk to the Estanque de Mercurio — the Pond of Mercury, the large rectangular pool with the bronze
 statue of Mercury in its center. From there look up to the south. A long arcaded wall stretches along
 the upper terrace, painted with strange figures and mythological scenes. That's the Galería de los
 Grutescos.


The Mercury Pond is the central organizing element of the upper garden.

Look at it carefully. The pool is rectangular, framed by a balustrade. In the center, on a raised
stone pedestal, a small bronze statue of Mercury — messenger of the gods, patron of merchants
and travelers — stands on one foot, his caduceus in his hand, his winged sandals lifting him into
the air. The figure was cast in fifteen seventy-three by Diego de Pesquera, working from designs
that have been attributed to Bartolomé Morel — the same Bartolomé Morel who, only five years
earlier, had cast the Giraldillo on top of the Giralda. The two bronzes are siblings.

The pool itself, in its current rectangular form, was created during the same Renaissance
renovations of the late sixteenth century. But the spot was already a Muslim water source —
under the pool's eastern edge there are remains of medieval Islamic cisterns that were
incorporated into the new design. The pool was, and is, a working hydraulic feature. Water from
springs north of the city is still channeled to it, and from it flows out in stone channels to feed the
lower gardens and parterres.

Above the pool, the Galería de los Grutescos.

This long, arcaded wall was built between fifteen ninety-three and sixteen twelve, by the architect
Vermondo Resta — an Italian working at the Spanish court — on top of what had originally been
part of the Almohad outer defensive wall. The decision was to keep the wall but to dress it in
Italian Renaissance Mannerist clothing. The decorations are painted directly on the wall —
frescoes in the "grotesque" style — strange hybrid creatures, half-human, half-animal, painted in
vivid colors, mythological scenes, classical figures. The word "grotesque" comes from the Italian
grotta — cave — because this kind of Roman wall painting was first rediscovered, by
Renaissance Italians, in underground Roman ruins that they took to be caves.

The Grutesco Gallery has been heavily restored over the centuries — most recently in the
nineteen nineties. What you see is faithful to the original sixteenth-century composition, though
the actual pigment is partly modern reconstruction.

If you walk to the south end of the gallery, climb the stairs at its eastern end — the stairs are
themselves built into the Almohad wall — you'll come out on top of the gallery, on a raised walk.
From there you have a panoramic view across the lower gardens. It's one of the best views in the
Alcázar, and most visitors miss it.

Now find the Pabellón de Carlos Quinto.

It's south of the Mercury pond, set in a small geometric parterre. Square in plan, brick on the
outside, completely tiled inside, with four semi-circular arches supported by marble columns on
each face. A small dome on top.

This pavilion was originally an Almohad qubba — a small square garden pavilion in the Islamic
tradition, dating from the twelfth century — that survived from the original Muslim gardens of the
Alcázar. Between fifteen forty-three and fifteen forty-six, on the personal commission of Charles
the Fifth, it was converted into a Renaissance summer house in the Mudéjar style. The
conversion was supervised by Juan Hernández. The interior was completely tiled with
sixteenth-century azulejos in geometric and floral patterns. Benches were added around the
inside walls. Charles used it as a place to dine outdoors in summer, sheltered from the sun, with
water flowing past the open arches.

It is the oldest standing building in the gardens. It has been continuously in use for almost five
hundred years.

And — small bonus — it's where, in twenty fourteen, the actors playing Myrcella Baratheon and
Trystane Martell filmed their kissing scene in Game of Thrones season five. Sitting on the bench
inside, kissing, with the camera tracking through one of the side arches.
