TRACK 13
The Dolls' Courtyard
OBJECT



 STOP & LOOK
 Step into the Patio de las Muñecas — the Courtyard of the Dolls. A much smaller courtyard than the
 Doncellas, two stories, surrounded by intricate stucco-and-tile decoration, with a small marble
 fountain in the center.


This was the private part of the palace.

If the Patio de las Doncellas was the ceremonial heart — where the king received dignitaries,
where ambassadors waited, where the public life of the court happened — then the Patio de las
Muñecas was the family heart. Smaller, more intimate, away from the front. The harem, the
family quarters, the rooms where Pedro lived when he wasn't being king.

The name — Courtyard of the Dolls — comes from two tiny doll-like faces carved into the stucco
at the springing of two of the arches. They're easy to miss. Look up at the spandrels — the
spaces between the arches and the wall above — and on two of them, in the corners, you'll see
two small carved faces about the size of a coin. Some art historians think they're craftsmen's
marks. Others think they're a kind of medieval graffiti — a private joke by the stucco workers
signing their work in plain sight. Either way, they've given the courtyard its informal name for as
long as anyone can remember.

The courtyard's architecture is, like the Patio de las Doncellas, fourteenth century below and
sixteenth century above. The ground floor — the elaborate horseshoe and polylobed arches you
see at eye level, the carved stucco between them, the geometric tilework on the walls — is
original Pedro the First, thirteen sixty-five. The upper galleries with their balconies and the
painted ceilings above them were added in the late sixteenth century by Felipe the Second's
renovations.

This is also where the queen lived. Maria de Padilla — Pedro the First's mistress, his great
romantic love, the woman whom Pedro reportedly married in secret in thirteen sixty-one even
though he was technically still married to Blanche of Bourbon — almost certainly held her court in
these rooms. She had been Pedro's companion since they were both teenagers. They had four
children together, all of whom Pedro would attempt, after her death in thirteen sixty-one, to
legitimize as his royal heirs. Their daughter Constanza of Castile would later marry John of
Gaunt, son of King Edward the Third of England, and become the matriarch of an entire English
royal lineage. Her descendants, four generations later, included Henry the Seventh of England,
and through him, every English monarch since.

So this small courtyard — this private corner of a Mudéjar palace in Andalusia — is the
architectural setting of the youth of a woman whose great-great-granddaughter was the mother

Look at the walls again, knowing that. The stucco around you is the same stucco that Maria de
Padilla saw, every morning, when she went out to fetch water from the small fountain. The
columns are the same columns. The tile patterns are the same patterns. The space is
