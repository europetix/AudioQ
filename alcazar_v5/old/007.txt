TRACK 07
The House of Trade
OBJECT



 STOP & LOOK
 Walk to the right side of the Patio de la Montería. You'll find a doorway leading to the Cuarto del
 Almirante — the Admiral's Hall — and the rooms that made up the Casa de la Contratación. Step
 inside.


For over two centuries, every gold doubloon, every silver bar, every chili pepper, every cacao
bean, every potato, every tomato, every coca leaf, every African enslaved person, every Spanish
missionary, every shipment of any kind moving between Spain and the Americas was registered,
taxed, blessed, or licensed in these rooms.

The Casa de la Contratación — the House of Trade — was founded by Queen Isabel the
Catholic on the twentieth of January, fifteen oh three. Eleven years after Columbus's first voyage.
Spain, by that point, had realized that the trickle of returning ships was about to become a flood,
and that flood needed regulation. So Isabel set up a single royal institution to handle it. Every
ship sailing for the Indies needed a license from the Casa. Every cargo coming back was
inspected, taxed, and recorded. The royal fifth — the quinto real, the twenty percent due to the
crown on all bullion from the Americas — was collected here. Navigators were trained and
licensed here. Maps were drawn, kept secret, updated, and kept secret again, in these rooms.

The Casa lived in the Alcázar from fifteen oh three until seventeen seventeen — two hundred
and fourteen years. When Felipe the Fifth eventually moved it to Cádiz in seventeen seventeen,
after the Guadalquivir had silted too much for big trade ships, it took its archives with it. Those
archives — millions of pages — are now in the Archive of the Indies, the third UNESCO building
of this trio, just outside on the Plaza del Triunfo, twenty meters from where you're standing.

But this is where it operated. In these rooms. With these wooden ceilings.

Two things are particularly worth seeing here. The first is the room itself — the Cuarto del
Almirante, the Admiral's Hall. It has a Renaissance artesonado wooden ceiling from the sixteenth
century, geometric Mudéjar-inspired patterns in coffered wood, gilded. The walls now display a
collection of nineteenth and early twentieth-century paintings depicting events in Sevillan history
— the Catholic Monarchs receiving Columbus, scenes from the discovery, allegorical figures of
the four kingdoms of Spain. They're later works, added to dress the room as a museum, but the
ceiling above them is original.

The second thing — and this is the masterpiece of the entire wing — is in the next room. The
Sala de Audiencias. The Audience Hall. Step through.

A continuous marble bench runs around the entire perimeter of the room. That's where the
officials of the Casa de la Contratación sat — administrators, cosmographers, pilots — in council
to decide cargoes and routes. The ceiling above is sixteenth-century Renaissance, with carved
coffers. The walls are hung with tapestries showing the heraldic shields of the various admirals
and navigators who sailed for Spain.

And at the head of the room, above the altar — because this room also functioned as the chapel
of the Casa de la Contratación — is the most extraordinary painting in the Real Alcázar. We're
going to spend an entire track with it next.
