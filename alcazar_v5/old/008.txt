 You're in the Sala de Audiencias. Walk to the altar at the far end. Above the altar, the central panel of
 a triptych retablo. A serene Virgin in a white gown and blue mantle, looking down, sheltering a crowd

This painting is the first time the discovery of the Americas was depicted in European religious
art.

The first. Let me say that again, because most visitors walk past this painting without registering
it. Before this painting, no European religious image had depicted the New World. The act of
evangelization, the encounter with indigenous American peoples, the ships of the Atlantic
crossing — none of it had ever been put into the iconographic vocabulary of Christian art. This
painting, made between fifteen thirty-one and fifteen thirty-six by a Sevillan painter of German
origin named Alejo Fernández, was the first attempt to do so.

It's called La Virgen de los Navegantes — the Virgin of the Navigators — or sometimes La Virgen
del Buen Aire, the Virgin of Fair Winds, or La Virgen de los Mareantes, the Virgin of the
Seafarers. Any of those names will lead you to the same painting.

Now let me show you what's actually in it.

The Virgin Mary stands at the center, on a cloud, robed in white with a deep blue mantle that
opens out from her shoulders to form a kind of tent above the figures below her. This is the
Madonna of Mercy iconography — a medieval European tradition in which the Virgin shelters her
devotees beneath her cloak. The new thing here, the radical thing, is who Mary is sheltering.

On her right, beneath one side of the cloak — look closely — a group of European figures
kneeling in prayer. Tradition identifies them as Christopher Columbus in golden robes nearest
the Virgin, then Amerigo Vespucci, then the Pinzón brothers in their red cloaks, then probably
Vicente Yáñez Pinzón, possibly Juan de la Cosa. The men who sailed.

On her left, beneath the other side of the cloak, a group of indigenous American figures.
Bare-chested, dark-skinned, also kneeling. The first images of indigenous Americans in a major
Spanish religious altarpiece. The painter Alejo Fernández, who never crossed the Atlantic,
painted them based on the descriptions and sketches that had been arriving in Sevilla through
the Casa de la Contratación for forty years.

Slightly behind the Pinzón brothers, almost as a continuation of the European devotional group,
you can sometimes see a figure with imperial features that's been identified as either Fernando
the Catholic or — more commonly — the Emperor Carlos the Fifth himself. The painting was

Below the Virgin, on the lower register, the ships. Seven distinct types of sixteenth-century
Spanish vessel. Caravels, naos, galleons. The full inventory of the early Atlantic fleet. The painter
wasn't a naval architect, but his details are remarkably precise — you can identify each ship type
by the configuration of its rigging.

The retablo around the central painting was almost entirely lost in the nineteenth century and
reconstructed during a restoration in the nineteen sixties. The side panels show Saint Sebastian,
Saint James, Saint Elmo — patron of sailors — and Saint John the Evangelist.

Now — here's the difficult thing about this painting, the thing you should know.

It is, theologically, an image of the Virgin Mary blessing the Spanish project in the Americas. It
places Columbus and the indigenous peoples alike under her protection. It implies that the
encounter between Europe and the Americas was a moment of unification under Christ,
supervised by the mother of God.

What it doesn't show — what the painter couldn't show, because the empire was still building its
myth about itself in fifteen thirty-six — is what was actually happening in the Americas at the
moment the painting was being completed. The population collapse of indigenous peoples from
disease, war, and forced labor. The encomienda system. The first African enslaved people being
shipped across the Atlantic. The destruction of Aztec and Inca political structures. The
transformation of vast cultures into colonized populations.

The painting is beautiful. It is also propaganda — propaganda made before the propagandists
fully knew what they were celebrating. The Virgin of the Navigators shelters everyone under her
cloak. The actual empire being administered from these rooms did not. I think it's worth standing
in front of this painting for a minute and holding both things in your head. The wonder of an art
form expanding to depict a new continent. And the suffering that the institution which
commissioned it caused.
