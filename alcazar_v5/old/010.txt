The Patio de las Doncellas — The Buried Garden
★★ HERO



 STOP & LOOK
 Walk through the central doorway of the facade. You pass through a short vestibule and emerge into
 a long rectangular courtyard with a reflecting pool down its center, surrounded on all four sides by
 arcaded galleries with multi-lobed arches resting on slender marble columns. Stop near the edge of
 the pool. This is the Patio de las Doncellas — the Courtyard of the Maidens.


For four hundred and twenty years, this entire central space looked completely different from
what you're seeing right now.

You are standing in front of the most famous courtyard in Sevilla. The one that ends up on every
postcard, in every guidebook, in every Game of Thrones promotional still. But what you see
today — the long reflecting pool, the sunken garden, the recessed marble pavement around it —
is, paradoxically, both very old and very new. It dates from the original construction of the
courtyard in the thirteen sixties. It also dates from a restoration completed in two thousand and
five.

Between fifteen eighty-one and fifteen eighty-four — during the reign of Felipe the Second — the
original Mudéjar pool and sunken garden you're looking at were buried. They were covered over
with a white and black marble pavement and a single ornamental fountain placed in the center.
The reason was fashion. The Renaissance had arrived. The Italian style, with its taste for flat
parterres of marble and ornamental fountains, had reached the Spanish court. The Habsburg
administrators of the palace decided the medieval Mudéjar pool was old-fashioned. They paved
over it.

For the next four hundred and twenty years, every visitor who came here — from Felipe the
Second's grandchildren in the sixteen hundreds to Felipe the Fifth in the seventeen thirties, from
the romantic travelers of the eighteen hundreds to the early Lonely Planet readers of the
nineteen eighties — saw a Renaissance pavement with a small fountain in the middle. The
courtyard was beautiful, but it was not the courtyard Pedro the First had built.

Then in two thousand and two, an archaeological team led by Miguel Ángel Tabales of the
University of Sevilla — working with the Patronato del Real Alcázar — began excavating beneath
the marble. They were testing a theory that something older might survive underneath. What they
found, by two thousand and five, exceeded everyone's hopes. The original fourteenth-century
sunken garden, with its long T-shaped pool and brick borders, had been preserved almost intact
under the Renaissance fill. The Habsburg pavers had not destroyed it. They had merely buried it.

So in two thousand and four and five, the cathedral chapter of restorers and archaeologists
undertook one of the most consequential restoration decisions in the history of the Alcázar. They

removed the marble. They restored the original Mudéjar pool. They replanted the sunken garden
with the kinds of aromatic plants — myrtle, orange, jasmine — that fourteenth-century records
suggest were originally there. They lowered the central garden by about forty centimeters,
exactly as it had been in Pedro's day.

What you're standing in front of is, therefore, two things at once. It is, on one hand, the courtyard
as Pedro the First, Muhammad the Fifth's craftsmen, and the people who attended Pedro's court
actually saw it in thirteen sixty-six. And on the other, it is a twenty-first century scholarly
restoration of that original. The galleries around you — the arches, the columns, the stucco
above — those are the genuine fourteenth-century construction. The wooden carved ceilings of
the upper galleries are mostly sixteenth-century additions made when the Catholic Monarchs and
the Habsburgs adapted this upper floor for their winter quarters. The lower garden and pool are
the restored original.

Now look at the arches around you. Twelve on the longer sides — seven on one side, five on the
other, with elaborately lobed and interlaced openings. The columns are marble, slender,
twin-paired in places, single elsewhere. Above the arches, sebka latticework. Above the sebka,
an inscription band in Arabic Kufic script — the same "wa-l■ gh■liba ill■ All■h", there is no victor
but Allah, that runs across the facade outside. Above the inscription, polychrome tile panels.

Look at the columns more carefully. Almost every one of them is older than the palace. They
were reused — what the Spanish call expolios — from Roman buildings, Visigothic churches, or
earlier Almohad and Abbadid structures. The Patio de las Doncellas, like the Mezquita of
Córdoba, is built on top of two thousand years of architectural memory. Some of these columns
may have been quarried by Romans in the first century. They have stood in three different
religions of building since then.

The name — Patio de las Doncellas, Courtyard of the Maidens — comes from a medieval
legend. According to the legend, the Muslim emir of Sevilla, before the Christian conquest,
demanded a tribute of one hundred maidens every year from the surrounding Christian villages.
The story is almost certainly invented — there's no historical evidence for any such tribute — but
the name stuck.

The maidens did not exist. The courtyard does. It is, in my view, the most beautiful single space
in the city of Sevilla. Spend as much time here as the visitor flow allows. Then walk through the
doorway at the far end of the courtyard. Behind it is the room that, more than any other, was the
center of political power in fourteenth-century Castile.
