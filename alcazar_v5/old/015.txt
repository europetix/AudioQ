TRACK 15
The Gothic Palace and the Tapestries of Tunis
OBJECT



 STOP & LOOK
 Find the staircase at the southeast corner of the Patio de las Doncellas and climb up to the Salones
 de Carlos V — the Halls of Charles the Fifth, which are actually the much older Gothic palace of
 Alfonso the Tenth, remodeled for the Renaissance court. You'll pass through the Salón del Techo de
 Carlos V — easily recognized by the wooden coffered ceiling with the Habsburg double eagle — and
 into the Salón de Tapices, a vaulted hall hung with enormous tapestries.


You've just walked uphill into a much older building.

This is Alfonso the Tenth's Gothic palace, built between twelve fifty-four and twelve sixty — about
a century before Pedro the First built his Mudéjar masterpiece downstairs. Alfonso, Pedro's
great-grandfather, was the king who took Sevilla's intellectual and cultural heritage and turned it
into a coherent royal project — codifying the Castilian language, compiling the Cantigas de Santa
María, sponsoring the translations of Arabic philosophy into Latin that would change European
thought. His Gothic palace was the original royal residence inside the Alcázar after the conquest.

The building was badly damaged by the Lisbon earthquake of November first, seventeen fifty-five
— the same earthquake that destroyed half of Lisbon and was felt as far away as Finland. The
earthquake hit Sevilla too. Walls cracked, vaults shifted, parts of the upper palace had to be
rebuilt. The architect Sebastián Van der Borcht — a Belgian engineer working in Sevilla, the
same man who would later create the great gilded reja for the cathedral's royal chapel — was
hired in seventeen fifty-five to repair the damage. He gave the Gothic palace its current Baroque
facade facing the Patio del Crucero. Many of the original Alfonsine details were lost in that
reconstruction.

What survives — and what you're walking through — is therefore a building that is partly
thirteenth-century Gothic, partly sixteenth-century Habsburg renovation, partly
eighteenth-century Baroque repair. The room called the Salón del Techo de Carlos V — Charles
the Fifth's Ceiling Room — gets its name from its coffered wooden ceiling, gilded, polychromed,
with the Habsburg double-headed eagle in the center, the Pillars of Hercules with the imperial
motto Plus Ultra carved into the corners. The ceiling was installed in the sixteenth century during
Charles's renovations after his fifteen twenty-six wedding.

Plus Ultra means "Further Beyond." It was Charles the Fifth's personal device — a reference to
the Atlantic crossings, to the lands beyond the Pillars of Hercules at the Strait of Gibraltar, to the
empire he had inherited. Before Charles, the Roman saying had been Non Plus Ultra — Nothing
Further Beyond — referring to the limits of the known classical world. Charles dropped the "non".
He claimed the further beyond as Spanish territory.

Now walk into the Salón de Tapices — the Hall of the Tapestries.

Six enormous tapestries hang on these walls. They depict scenes from Charles the Fifth's Tunis
campaign of fifteen thirty-five — his military expedition against the Ottoman corsair Barbarossa,
who had seized the city of Tunis from a vassal of Charles. Charles's fleet attacked, captured
Tunis, freed Christian captives, and restored the vassal king. It was one of the great propaganda
victories of his reign.

The tapestries you see were woven in the seventeen thirties — about two hundred years after
the actual campaign. They are not the originals. The originals were woven in Brussels in the
fifteen forties by Willem de Pannemaker, based on cartoons painted by the Dutch artist Jan
Cornelisz Vermeyen, who had actually accompanied Charles on the campaign. The originals are
still owned by the Spanish crown and kept in storage. These are eighteenth-century replicas,
woven in the Royal Tapestry Factory of Madrid, hanging here in their place.

What you see, then, is a Habsburg propaganda cycle, in tapestry form, from the twelve sixties
Gothic palace, retold in the seventeen thirties, depicting the fifteen thirties campaigns of an
emperor who got married in this very palace in fifteen twenty-six.

Five centuries layered on each other, in one hall. That's the Alcázar in a nutshell.

When you've taken them in, find the doorway leading back out and down. We're heading into the
gardens. They are vast. They are layered. And they're going to take the rest of your visit.
