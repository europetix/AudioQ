The Oldest Palace Still in Use in Europe
CONTEXT



 STOP & LOOK
 You've just passed through the Puerta del León. You're now in the Patio del León — the Lion
 Courtyard. A wide rectangular space, gravelled, with three large arches on the far wall. Stop here for
 a moment before walking on. There's a layered story under your feet.


You are standing on more than two thousand years of stacked history.

Beneath your feet, archaeologists have found Roman walls. Above the Roman level, Visigothic
foundations of a Christian basilica from the sixth or seventh century. Above the Visigothic, the
Umayyad fort that the caliph Abd al-Rahman the Third ordered built here in the year nine
hundred and thirteen. Above the Umayyad, eleventh-century Abbadid additions when Sevilla was
an independent Muslim taifa kingdom under the poet-king Al-Mu'tamid. Above the Abbadid,
twelfth-century Almohad reconstruction when Sevilla was the European capital of an empire
stretching to Marrakesh. Above the Almohad, thirteenth-century Castilian Christian additions
after Fernando the Third took Sevilla in twelve forty-eight. And above all of that, the various royal
renovations that brought us to today.

The Spanish state lists the Real Alcázar as a Bien de Interés Cultural — a Heritage Site of
Cultural Interest. UNESCO inscribed it in nineteen eighty-seven along with the cathedral and the
Archive of the Indies. But the most striking fact is the simplest one. This is the oldest royal palace
still in use anywhere in Europe. Versailles is younger. Buckingham Palace is much younger. The
Vatican is older as a religious site but the current papal palaces are not. Nothing else has been a
working royal residence continuously, in some form, for eleven hundred years.

Let me give you the headline names that built it. They'll come back over and over as we walk.

Abd al-Rahman the Third — the first caliph of al-Andalus, the one who declared Córdoba a
caliphate independent of Damascus. He ordered the original fort here in nine hundred and
thirteen, on the foundations of a Visigothic basilica that itself had stood on a Roman fortress.
Quadrangular, walled, about a hundred meters square, attached to the city walls. Almost nothing
of this earliest fort is visible above ground today, but the walls of the Patio de Banderas — the
courtyard where you'll exit at the end of this tour — still preserve some of his masonry.

Al-Mu'tamid — the eleventh-century Abbadid king of Sevilla, also one of the great poets of Arabic
Spain. He expanded the palace southward and renamed it Al-Muwarak, the Blessed Palace. He
built a now-vanished throne hall called Al-Turayya — the Hall of the Pleiades — somewhere very
near where we'll stand in the Hall of Ambassadors later. When his Almoravid overlords exiled him
to Morocco in ten ninety-one, he wrote, from a small town near Marrakesh, that he wanted to be
buried back here in Sevilla. He never returned. He died in exile.

The Almohads — the Berber dynasty that ruled both North Africa and most of Muslim Spain from
the mid-twelfth century to the early thirteenth. They made Sevilla their European capital. They
built two more palaces inside this Alcázar, demolishing much of what came before. The only
Almohad architecture you can still see today is the Patio del Yeso, the Patio del Crucero
foundations, the Baños de Doña María de Padilla cisterns, and most of the outer walls.

Then in twelve forty-eight, Fernando the Third — the Castilian king buried just across the plaza in
the cathedral's royal chapel, the one we visited yesterday if you came from the cathedral tour —
took Sevilla after a seventeen-month siege. He moved into the Alcázar. He died in it four years
later. His son Alfonso the Tenth, the Wise, built a Gothic palace inside the complex around
twelve fifty-eight, which still survives as the Salones de Carlos the Fifth in the upper rooms.

And then in the thirteen sixties, a Castilian Christian king did something nobody had done in
Europe before. He hired Muslim craftsmen from Granada, Toledo, and Sevilla itself to build him,
in his own royal compound, a palace in the style of his Muslim allies. We're going to spend most
of our visit inside that palace. His name was Pedro the First — Pedro the Cruel to his enemies,
Pedro the Just to his friends. We'll get to him.

For now — look at those three large arches on the far wall of this courtyard. They're Almohad.
Twelfth century. They opened from the original Almohad palace's outer enclosure into its inner
ceremonial space. The two side arches were originally horseshoe arches in the Islamic style. The
central, taller one was opened later, in the fifteenth or sixteenth century, to align with the new
Mudéjar palace beyond.

Walk slowly toward those arches. We're heading first to a small room on your left — the Hall of
Justice — and from there into one of the most extraordinary Almohad survivals in Spain.
