TRACK 19
The King Still Lives Here
OBJECT



 STOP & LOOK
 If you have a ticket to the Cuarto Real Alto — the Upper Royal Apartments — this is your moment.
 You'll go up a staircase to the upper floor of Pedro the First's palace. If you don't have the ticket, no
 problem — listen anyway, because what's described here tells you something important about why
 this palace is different from every other historic palace you've visited.


Above the rooms we just walked through, on the upper floor of Pedro the First's palace, there are
about fifteen rooms that are still — today, in twenty twenty-six — used as a residence by the King
of Spain.

When King Felipe the Sixth and his family come to Sevilla for an official visit, they stay here.
When Queen Letizia and the princesses join him, they stay here. The current visitor cap of seven
hundred and fifty people inside the Alcázar is partly because the royal family occasionally uses
the upper rooms, and those rooms are above the rest of the palace, and they need quiet and
security.

This is unusual. Almost every other royal palace you might visit in Europe — Versailles, Hampton
Court, the Hofburg, the Royal Palace of Madrid — is a museum. A historical monument. The
current heads of state, if there still are any, live elsewhere. Versailles has been a museum since
the French Revolution. Hampton Court has been a museum since the eighteenth century. The
Hofburg is mostly a museum, though some Austrian state functions still happen there.

The Real Alcázar is a working royal palace. Not a museum. The lower floor is opened to the
public during the day, the gardens are opened, the Casa de la Contratación rooms are opened
— but the upper floor, the Cuarto Real Alto, is the King of Spain's house. The lawnmower still
cuts the grass. The maintenance staff still cleans the rooms every week, whether or not the
family is in residence.

If you have the optional ticket, here's what you'll see. Fifteen rooms furnished as a working royal
apartment. Sixteenth and seventeenth-century coffered wooden ceilings — many of them
adaptations made for the Catholic Monarchs in the fourteen seventies, when Isabel the Catholic
adapted the upper floor of Pedro's Mudéjar palace as her winter residence. The future Felipe the
Second's father, Charles the Fifth, was born in Ghent but spent much of his Spanish residency in
these rooms. The future Felipe the Second himself, conceived shortly after his parents' wedding
in the Hall of Ambassadors directly below you, was born nine months later in Valladolid, but his
earliest childhood was here.

Among the rooms: the audience room, the Catholic Monarchs' bedroom, the oratory of the
Catholic Kings — a tiny private chapel with sixteenth-century azulejos showing the Annunciation

by Niculoso Pisano, the Italian ceramic master who introduced majolica tile-painting techniques

There is a salón of Felipe the Second, decorated with Habsburg portraits. There is a billiards
room added in the nineteenth century. There are rooms used by Queen Isabel the Second in the
eighteen hundreds, by the Bourbon kings of the early twentieth century, by Felipe the Sixth and
Letizia today.

If you've booked the Cuarto Real Alto, photography is not allowed inside. Visits are guided, in
fixed time slots, in the morning only. You need to arrive at the Puerta del León sixty minutes
before your slot, store your bags, pass security, and present yourself at the Cuarto Real Alto
entrance fifteen minutes before your slot. If you don't have it — most visitors don't — you'll skip
this section. But you should know it's there. Above you. Lived in. Occupied.

Because here is the thing the Alcázar wants you to remember.

Every other room in this palace — the Patio del Yeso, the Hall of Justice, the Patio de las
Doncellas, the Salón de Embajadores, the gardens — exists as part of a continuous, unbroken
line of royal use that started in nine thirteen and has not stopped. Eleven hundred and thirteen
years. The same building, used as a royal residence, by every kind of regime — Umayyad
caliphate, Abbadid taifa kingdom, Almohad caliphate, Castilian medieval monarchy, Habsburg
empire, Bourbon dynasty, Republic, Civil War, restored constitutional monarchy. The building
has outlasted all of them. It is still doing its original job.

This is the oldest royal palace still in use in Europe. We say that in the literature, and on the
audio guides, and on the tickets. But the phrase loses some of its weight unless you stop and let
it land. Felipe the Sixth, in twenty twenty-six, sleeps in the same building where Abd al-Rahman
the Third's governors slept in nine fourteen. Eleven hundred years of continuous occupation, in
the same enclosure.

Now — let's go to the closing track. Walk back to the gardens and find a quiet spot. We're almost
done.
