Five hundred years ago this year.

That's the anniversary Sevilla is marking right now, in twenty twenty-six. It's the reason the city
has put up commemorative banners along the Avenida de la Constitución and along the river. It's
the reason there's a special exhibition at the Espacio Santa Clara from late March. It's why every
guidebook for Sevilla in twenty twenty-six has a section on Carlos and Isabel.

Five hundred years ago, on the night of March tenth into March eleventh, fifteen twenty-six, just
after midnight, in this room — under this exact dome — Charles the Fifth, Holy Roman Emperor,
King of Spain, ruler of an empire on which the sun never set, married his first cousin Isabel of
Portugal in a quiet ceremony attended by a small handful of people.

He was twenty-six years old. She was twenty-three. They had never met until that afternoon.

The story of how they ended up here is part state strategy, part personal accident, part Lent, part
funeral. Let me tell it.

By fifteen twenty-six, Charles the Fifth ruled an empire that included Spain, the Holy Roman
Empire of central Europe, the Netherlands, most of Italy, and the rapidly expanding territories of
the Spanish Americas. He was the most powerful Christian ruler in the world. But he had a
domestic problem. He had been born in Flanders and educated in French. The Castilian nobility
resented him as a foreign monarch. The Cortes — the Spanish parliament — had pressed him
repeatedly to marry a Spanish woman, to anchor himself in the peninsula. He resisted for years.
Then, in fifteen twenty-five, he agreed. The chosen bride was his first cousin Isabel — daughter
of Isabel of Aragon and King Manuel the First of Portugal. She was, in other words, the
granddaughter of Fernando and Isabel the Catholic, just like Charles himself. Two of the four
grandparents of Charles the Fifth and Isabel of Portugal were the same two people.

A papal dispensation was required for the marriage, because they were so closely related. The
Vatican granted it.

Isabel left Lisbon in February fifteen twenty-six. She entered Sevilla through the Puerta de la
Macarena on the third of March, accompanied by an enormous Portuguese retinue and a dowry
of nine hundred thousand gold doblas — one of the largest dowries in European history. The city
had erected seven triumphal arches along her route, each dedicated to one of the cardinal

virtues she was expected to bring to the new emperor: Prudence at the Macarena gate, Fortitude
near Santa Marina, Clemency near San Marcos, Peace at Santa Catalina, Justice near San

She arrived at the cathedral, prayed in front of the great altarpiece — the same retablo of Pyeter
Dancart that we saw together yesterday — and then was escorted across the Plaza del Triunfo
to the Alcázar. There she waited. Charles was still on the road from Madrid.

He arrived seven days later, on the night of the tenth of March. Late. By candlelight. He came
directly to the Alcázar. He met his cousin Isabel for the first time in his life, in these very rooms.

By all surviving accounts — letters from courtiers, dispatches from ambassadors, the emperor's
own later memoir — there was an immediate emotional spark between them. The biographer
Pedro Mexía wrote that Charles, upon seeing Isabel, did not want to wait the planned day or two
for the formal ceremony to take place at the cathedral. He insisted on marrying her that same
night.

Several practical reasons reinforced his impatience. Lent had begun. Holy Week was
approaching. Catholic canon law restricted the public celebration of weddings during Lent. The
emperor's beloved older sister Isabel of Habsburg, Queen of Denmark, had died only weeks
earlier in Germany — news that had reached Sevilla only a few days before. The court was
technically in mourning. And the cardinal who would officiate, Cardinal Salviati, the papal legate,
was already in Sevilla and available.

So they were married, by Cardinal Salviati, in this room, just after midnight. The ceremony was
attended by perhaps fifteen or twenty people. The future Felipe the Second was conceived within
weeks. The public wedding Mass — the misa de velaciones — was held the next morning across
the plaza at the cathedral, before the great altarpiece, in front of the assembled nobility of Spain.

What happened afterward, between Charles and Isabel, was unusual for a sixteenth-century
royal marriage. They fell genuinely in love. The next six months — what the chroniclers called the
imperial honeymoon — were spent traveling through Andalusia, visiting Granada where Charles
ordered a Renaissance palace built next to the Alhambra, and returning here to the Alcázar
before continuing north. Their surviving correspondence is one of the great love letters of the
sixteenth century. When Isabel died in childbirth twelve years later, in fifteen thirty-nine, Charles
wore black for the rest of his life. He never remarried.

This is the room where it happened. The dome above you was watching. The ten-fold lacería
knot patterns above your head were already a hundred years old. They watched a Castilian
Christian king named Charles, descended from the Catholic Monarchs who had ended Muslim
rule in Spain, get married in a room covered in Arabic inscriptions praising Allah, built by a king
named Pedro who had once allied with Muslim Granada.
