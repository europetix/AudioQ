The Baths of María de Padilla
OBJECT



 STOP & LOOK
 Find the entrance to the Baños de Doña María de Padilla. It's an underground space — you'll
 descend a short staircase. A long, low, vaulted chamber stretches in front of you, with a pool of dark,
 perfectly still water reflecting the arched ceiling overhead. The temperature drops noticeably as you
 enter.


This is one of the strangest spaces in the whole palace.

The vaults around you are twelfth century. Almohad. They were not built as baths. They were
built as cisterns — underground reservoirs for storing rainwater for the entire upper palace
complex. The Almohads of the late twelve hundreds, who built so much of the Alcázar's
underlying infrastructure, understood that Sevilla is hot and that summer water supply was a
constant problem. They dug these vaults below the original Patio del Crucero — the Crossing
Courtyard — to catch and store the winter rain.

The vaults functioned as cisterns for two hundred years, then continued to function for at least a
century after the Castilian conquest. Sometime in the late fourteenth or fifteenth century, after
they had outlived their original purpose, the local tradition began to call them the baths of Maria
de Padilla. The legend held that Pedro the First's mistress had used these underground pools as
a private bathing place, escaping the summer heat above. There is no historical evidence that
she ever did. The chronicles of Pedro's court don't mention it. The architecture isn't really suited
for bathing — there's no drainage of the right kind, no obvious changing area, no warm pool. But
the legend persisted, and the name stuck.

Today, the standing water in the central channel is partly the residual function of the cistern —
still slowly catching seepage from above — and partly the deliberate retention of water for
aesthetic effect by modern conservators. The reflection of the arches in the still surface is one of
the most photographed views in the Alcázar. The light enters from the sides, the water is dark,
the temperature is fifteen degrees cooler than the outside.

The vaults are also famous for another reason. In two thousand and fourteen, the HBO television
series Game of Thrones used this space — and the gardens above — as the kingdom of Dorne,
home of the Martell family. We'll talk more about that in a few tracks. For now: the scene where
the Sand Snakes meet in secret to plot revenge for Oberyn Martell's death? Filmed right here,
right where you're standing.

Look at the vaults above you. The brick coursing is identical, in technique and dimension, to the
brick of the Giralda's lower shaft, the brick of the Patio del Yeso arcade, the brick of the Torre del
Oro by the river. The Almohad workshops were prodigious — and consistent. The same hands
