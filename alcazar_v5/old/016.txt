You just stepped from medieval Spain into a thousand years of garden history.

The gardens of the Real Alcázar cover something close to seventeen hectares. They are home
to over twenty thousand plants belonging to roughly one hundred and eighty-seven different
species, many of them planted in continuous cultivation since the medieval period. Orange trees
descended from twelfth-century Almohad plantings. Myrtle hedges. Cypresses brought from
Persia along the trade routes a thousand years ago. Citrus, jasmine, oleander, magnolia, palm.
Several of the trees in this garden are over four centuries old — older than the United States,
older than the Royal Society, older than the telescope.

But the gardens are not a single project. They are, like the palaces above them, a layered
accumulation of separate gardens built over centuries.

The oldest layer — the deepest — is the Almohad concept of the paradise garden. Twelfth
century. The Quran describes paradise as a garden with running water, four rivers, shade trees,
fragrant plants, and pavilions. The Almohad caliphs of Sevilla, like the contemporary Almohad
caliphs of Marrakesh, built their palace gardens to evoke this image. Sunken parterres,
geometric quadrants divided by water channels, raised walkways between them. The cool of
running water in summer. The smell of orange blossom and jasmine in spring. The gardens were
not for crops. They were for meditation. They were for reflecting the architecture of paradise on
earth.

When the Castilian Christians took Sevilla in twelve forty-eight, they kept the Almohad gardens.
They did not understand them as theology — Christians have different ideas about paradise —
but they recognized them as beautiful, and they preserved them. Most of the original Almohad
water infrastructure remained in use for centuries.

The next layer was Renaissance. After Charles the Fifth's wedding in fifteen twenty-six, the
gardens were extensively renovated to suit Italian Renaissance taste. The Estanque de Mercurio
— the Pond of Mercury — was added in the sixteenth century. The bronze statue of Mercury
rising from its central fountain was cast by Diego de Pesquera in fifteen seventy-three. To the
south of the Mercury pond, a long retaining wall was constructed, decorated with grotesque-style
painted scenes — the Galería de los Grutescos, the Grotesco Gallery — which we'll see in a
moment. The Italian taste for parterres, perspective views, and ornamental fountains was layered

The Pabellón de Carlos Quinto — the Pavilion of Charles the Fifth — was converted between

Renaissance summer house. Charles the Fifth used it as a personal retreat. It's covered inside

Later still came the Baroque additions of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries. New
fountains. New parterres. New paths.

And finally, in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, the so-called English Garden was
added — an Anglo-Romantic landscape of looser plantings, winding paths, picturesque ruins.
The Jardín de los Poetas, the Jardín del Príncipe, the Jardín de las Damas, the Jardín del
Laberinto — each was created or remodeled in this final wave, when the gardens of the Alcázar
were given their current park-like extent.

So when you walk these gardens, you are walking through four distinct conceptions of what a
royal garden should be, stacked on top of each other. The Almohad paradise. The Italian
Renaissance perspective. The Baroque theatrical garden. The English Romantic park. All four
coexist in the same seventeen hectares.

One more thing before you start walking. Listen.

The defining sound of the gardens of the Alcázar — the thing the Almohads understood about
gardens in this climate eight hundred years ago — is the sound of running water. Almost every
garden has at least one fountain, channel, or pool. Many have multiple. The water comes partly
from rainwater collected in cisterns like the ones under the Patio del Crucero, partly from the
medieval aqueducts that fed water into the Alcázar from natural springs north of the city, partly
from modern municipal supply now. But the sound is the same. Sit on a bench. Close your eyes.
Listen. That is the sound of a paradise garden in the late twelfth century. It is still the sound
today.

Spend as much time here as you can spare. We'll point you to specific highlights in the next
track. But the gardens reward unhurried walking more than any other part of the Alcázar.
