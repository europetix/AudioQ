The King Who Built a Muslim Palace
CONTEXT



 STOP & LOOK
 You're back in the Patio de la Montería, in front of the great facade. Find a spot where you can see
 the facade and stand still. Read it again with what I'm about to tell you in mind.


The king who built this was thirty years old when construction started.

His name was Pedro the First. He was born in Burgos in thirteen thirty-four to King Alfonso the
Eleventh — the same Alfonso who built the Hall of Justice — and Queen María of Portugal. He
was crowned in thirteen fifty at age sixteen. He reigned for nineteen years. He was murdered at
age thirty-four. By the end of his short reign he had become one of the most polarizing figures in
medieval Spanish history.

To his enemies — and he had many — he was Pedro el Cruel. Pedro the Cruel. They accused
him of executing nobles without trial, of murdering his Aragonese cousins, of assassinating his
own half-brothers, of carrying on a public affair with María de Padilla while his wife Blanche of
Bourbon languished in royal imprisonment. They wrote chronicles painting him as a despot, a
Moor-lover, a Jew-lover, a heretic. His brother Enrique would eventually kill him at Montiel in
thirteen sixty-nine and seize the throne. The chronicles written after Enrique's victory shaped
Pedro's reputation for centuries.

To his supporters — and he had those too — he was Pedro el Justiciero. Pedro the Just. They
argued that what looked like cruelty to the nobility was actually equal justice applied without
regard to class. That he protected commoners, Jews, and Muslims against an aristocratic faction
that wanted special privileges. That the murders he committed were largely defensive — in a civil
war that his enemies had started — and that the chronicles attacking him were written by the
victors. The historian Pero López de Ayala, who wrote the harshest contemporary account of
Pedro's reign, was himself a political defector who switched sides and ended up serving Pedro's
killer. His chronicle is a primary source, and it is also a hostile witness.

Modern historians don't agree. Some emphasize his violence. Others emphasize the systemic
violence of all fourteenth-century European kingship and argue that Pedro stands out only
because he lost. The closer you get to his actual decisions, the more complicated he becomes.

What is not in dispute is his cultural orientation.

Pedro had Muslim soldiers in his personal guard. He had Jewish financial advisors, including the
famous treasurer Samuel ha-Levi, for whom he allowed the construction of a synagogue in
Toledo that today is one of the finest medieval synagogues in Spain. He signed mutual defense
treaties with Muhammad the Fifth of Granada that were genuinely military alliances against

shared enemies — not just diplomatic gestures. When Muhammad the Fifth was temporarily
overthrown in Granada in thirteen fifty-nine, Pedro sheltered him in Sevilla. When Pedro was
overthrown by his half-brother in thirteen sixty-six, Muhammad sent troops to help restore him.
The two kings depended on each other.

In thirteen sixty-four, after returning to power, Pedro commissioned this palace. He hired the
same Granadan craftsmen who had just finished the Court of the Lions in the Alhambra. They
worked alongside Toledan Mudéjar masters and local Sevillan artisans. The work took two years.
It was inaugurated in thirteen sixty-six.

Three years later, on the twenty-third of March, thirteen sixty-nine, Pedro was lured to a meeting
at Montiel, in central Castile, by his half-brother Enrique de Trastámara. Enrique killed Pedro
personally, by some accounts with his own hands. Pedro's body was buried first in a small chapel
in Montiel, then moved later — at his own daughter Constanza's request — to the cathedral of
Seville's royal chapel, where his bones lie today in the crypt below the Capilla Real, fifty meters
from where we stood yesterday.

Now look back at his palace facade.

This building is not a museum of a past Spain. It is the personal taste of a specific king. Pedro
chose every motif on this wall. He chose the inscription. He chose the materials. He chose to
write, on his own palace, that there is no victor but Allah — which is either an extraordinary act of
religious tolerance for a Christian monarch, or an extraordinary act of cultural appropriation, or an
extraordinary act of theological humility, or all three at once. The man who built this was
thirty-four years old when he died, and this facade is what he left.

Now — let's walk through the doorway and see what he built behind it.
