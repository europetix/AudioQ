 Édouard Manet, Horsewoman, Full-Face
 (L'Amazone)
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 33, the Impressionist rooms — a woman on horseback facing you.

    Édouard Manet, Horsewoman, Full-Face (L'Amazone), c. 1882 — oil on canvas.
    LISTEN FOR
    The unfinished late Manet, and how modern that bare, frank handling still looks.


 A striking, loosely painted portrait of a woman in dark riding habit — l'amazone, a horsewoman —
 facing you directly, fresh and unfinished-looking.

 Horsewoman, Full-Face (L'Amazone), painted around 1882 by Édouard Manet — the older painter the
 Impressionists revered as their leader, the man who'd kicked open the door for all of them.

 Manet was the great bridge. He came out of the Old Master tradition — he loved Velázquez and Hals —
 but he stripped away their finish and their storytelling, painting modern people in flat, bold, startlingly
 direct strokes that scandalised the official art world. This is one of his last works, painted as he was
 dying, young, of a wasting illness; it has the freshness of a sketch, the paint laid on quick and alive. The
 woman looks straight out at you with cool self-possession — a modern, urban, independent figure,
 exactly the contemporary life Manet insisted was worthy of serious art. Notice how "unfinished" it feels
 compared with everything upstairs; that openness, that visible speed, is the modern sensibility being
 born. Beside him, the master of movement — Degas, right along here.
