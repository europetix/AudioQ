 Germany screams in colour: Expressionism
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Rooms 34–35.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Kirchner, Otto Dix, Franz Marc and the German Expressionists.
    LISTEN FOR
    How German painters used violent colour and distortion to paint feeling, not fact.


 Now the twentieth century arrives in earnest, and it arrives loud. These rooms hold one of the Thyssen's
 great strengths — German Expressionism — and after the French delicacy next door, the change of
 voltage is electric.

 The Expressionists weren't interested in how the world looks; they wanted to paint how it feels —
 anxiety, desire, alienation, raw emotion — and they did it with violent, unnatural colour and harsh,
 jagged forms. Two groups led the charge. Die Brücke — "the Bridge" — young firebrands in Dresden,
 all clashing colour and angular bodies, electric and uneasy. And Der Blaue Reiter — "the Blue Rider"
 — in Munich, more lyrical and spiritual, pushing colour toward pure feeling and, soon, toward
 abstraction.

 This was art made in a Germany sliding toward catastrophe — the First World War, then the chaos that
 followed — and the unease is right there on the surface. The Nazis would later brand it "degenerate" and
 try to destroy it. Feel the nervous energy in these rooms; it's the modern psyche cracking open. Kirchner
 first, just along here.
