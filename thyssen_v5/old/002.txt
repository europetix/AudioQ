 The age of gold and the birth of the face
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Ground floor, Rooms 1–5 — the start of the tour and the oldest works.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Late-medieval and early-Renaissance panels: Duccio, Van Eyck, Memling, Antonello.
    LISTEN FOR
    How painting climbs out of flat gold and learns to build space, faces and the individual.


 You're at the top of the building and the top of the story — the earliest rooms, where Western painting is
 just teaching itself to walk. Take these first galleries slowly; they cover nearly two hundred years, from
 the early 1300s to about 1500, and you can almost watch art change its mind as you go.

 It begins with gold. The first panels glow with beaten gold-leaf backgrounds, because their world wasn't
 the everyday one — it was the eternal, heavenly space of saints and scripture, made for altars and
 prayer. Then, room by room, something extraordinary happens: the gold drains away and is replaced by
 sky, landscape, a real window, a real face. Artists in Italy and in the north — Flanders especially — start
 looking hard at the actual world, and at actual people.

 Watch for that shift as you move not far on. The single biggest invention in these rooms isn't a technique
 — it's the individual: the portrait of one specific human being, looking out at you across five centuries.
 The very first of them are a few steps on.
