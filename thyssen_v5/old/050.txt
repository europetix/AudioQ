 Piet Mondrian, Composition No. XIII (Composition
 2)
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 43, the abstraction room — a pale canvas webbed with short black strokes, a tree only half there.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Piet Mondrian, Composition No. XIII (Composition 2), 1913 — oil on canvas.
    LISTEN FOR
    Mondrian years before the famous grids — caught in the act of dissolving a real tree into pure structure.


 You probably know Mondrian for the other thing — the white grids ruled into blocks of red, blue and
 yellow that ended up on everything from cocktail dresses to album covers. This is Mondrian before all
 of that, and it's the more revealing picture. He painted it in 1913, just after moving to Paris and running
 headlong into Cubism, and it began life as a tree — most likely a eucalyptus. You can still feel the trunk
 and the spreading branches in the cage of short black strokes hovering on the pale, ochre-grey ground.

 Watch what he's doing to it. He takes one living, particular tree and boils it down to a rhythm of little
 horizontals and verticals, hunting for the structure underneath the surface. The Cubists had cracked
 objects into facets; Mondrian goes further, draining the object away until almost nothing is left but a
 scaffolding of marks, floating free.

 This is the hinge of his whole life's work. Within a few years he'll throw out the curve and the diagonal
 too, keep only the straight line and the three primary colours, and call it Neo-Plasticism — an art he was
 convinced could show the hidden order of the universe. So you're standing at the exact moment the tree
 turns into the grid. In this same room the Russian avant-garde takes abstraction somewhere harder and
 more political — El Lissitzky, just here.
