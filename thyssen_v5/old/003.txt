 Duccio, Christ and the Samaritan Woman
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 1, the gold-ground rooms — a small panel glowing with gold.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Duccio, Christ and the Samaritan Woman, 1310–11 — tempera and gold on panel.
    LISTEN FOR
    How a Sienese master cracked open the flat gold world and let real space — and a real conversation — begin.


 One of the oldest paintings in the museum — a small, jewel-like panel against a flat gold ground, with
 slender figures at a well outside a walled pink-and-cream town.

 Christ and the Samaritan Woman, painted around 1310 by Duccio di Buoninsegna, the founding master
 of the Sienese school. It's a small scene from the back of his greatest work, the Maestà, the colossal
 double-sided altarpiece made for Siena's cathedral — when that altarpiece was later broken up, predella
 panels like this one scattered into collections around the world.

 Look at what Duccio is reaching for. The background is still medieval gold, the holy non-space of an
 icon — but the town behind the figures is a real architectural place, with arches and a gate, and the
 figures themselves have weight and gesture and a quiet human exchange passing between them. Christ
 meets a woman at a well and asks her for water; she's surprised he'd even speak to her. It's one of the
 first moments in European painting where you feel a genuine conversation happening between two
 people. Duccio is standing right on the hinge between the icon and the picture — one foot in heaven's
 gold, one foot in the world. Everything else on these floors grows from this moment. The northern
 painters took the next step, close by.
