 Welcome to the Thyssen-Bornemisza
 Welcome. Take a breath — you've arrived at the third corner of Madrid's Golden Triangle of Art, and in
 some ways the most surprising one.

 You may have just come from the Prado, with its Spanish kings and Velázquez and Goya, or from the
 Reina Sofía, with Picasso's Guernica and the wild twentieth century. This museum is the bridge
 between them — and it fills in everything the other two leave out. Early Italian gold-ground panels. The
 Northern Renaissance. German painting. The Dutch Golden Age. The Grand Tour. Nineteenth-century
 America. German Expressionism. Russian abstraction. Pop. Where the Prado is Spanish and royal, and
 the Reina Sofía is Spanish and modern, the Thyssen is the whole story of Western painting, seven
 centuries of it, under one roof.

 And here's the remarkable part: almost none of this belonged to Spain until recently. This is a private
 collection — assembled across two generations by one family, the Thyssen-Bornemiszas,
 German-Hungarian industrialists, who set out quite deliberately to own one outstanding example of
 every chapter in the history of painting. Spain bought the whole thing in 1993, and it has been
 completing the triangle ever since.

 A word on how this tour works, because it shapes everything. The museum is built to be walked from
 the top down. You'll start on Level 2, in the thirteenth century, and as you descend through the building
 you'll move forward through time — down a floor, forward a few hundred years — until you step out
 the bottom into the present. It's one of the most satisfying walks in any museum in the world: the entire
 history of Western art, taken as a single gentle downhill stroll.

 So — to the top. Let's begin in the age of gold leaf and saints.
