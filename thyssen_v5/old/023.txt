 A relaxed, informal group — a family arranged outdoors in a landscape, the figures turning to one
 another with an easy, almost snapshot naturalness.

 Family Group in a Landscape, painted 1645–48 by Frans Hals, the great portraitist of Haarlem and the
 supreme master of the lively likeness. Group portraits were a Dutch speciality — families and civic
 guilds clubbing together to be painted — and Hals made them feel less posed than anyone.

 The magic is in the brushwork. Step close and the lace collars, the hands, the smiling faces dissolve into
 quick, slashing, almost careless-looking strokes — and then step back and everything snaps into
 crackling life. Hals worked fast and loose, leaving the energy of the brush right there on the surface,
 centuries before the Impressionists made that a movement. He's after the fleeting, animated moment —
 a turn of the head, the beginning of a smile, a child squirming — rather than stiff formal dignity. You
 can feel the warmth between these people. It's one of the friendliest paintings in the museum. Now from
 people to pure, swept, silent space — Saenredam's church, just along here.
