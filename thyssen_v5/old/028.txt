    The flirtation and lightness of Rococo France — just before the world cracks open.


 A frothy, sunlit garden scene — young people at play on a see-saw among feathery trees, all pastel silks
 and dappled light.

 The See-Saw, painted around 1750–52 by the young Jean-Honoré Fragonard, one of the great talents of
 the French Rococo — the style of Watteau and Boucher before him, all lightness, leisure and gentle
 eroticism.

 This is painting as pure pleasure. The subject is essentially nothing — privileged young people amusing
 themselves in a lush garden — and that's the point. The Rococo was the art of an aristocracy with time
 on its hands, and it celebrates flirtation, play, and the delicious surfaces of life: shimmering silk, warm
 skin, leaves trembling in soft light. Fragonard's brush is quick and sensuous; the whole thing feels
 improvised and alive, like a stolen afternoon. There's often a sly undercurrent of romance and innuendo
 in his work — a see-saw, after all, is all about who goes up as the other comes down. Within a
 generation, the French upheaval would sweep this gilded world away and brand its art as decadent.
 Catch it here at its most carefree. Now Britain, painting itself with cool grandeur, just beyond.
