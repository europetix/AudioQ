    LISTEN FOR
    What Pollock was actually doing dripping paint on the floor — and why it mattered.


 A large canvas that's pure energy — skeins and spatters of poured paint, brown and silver and black,
 woven into a dense, rhythmic, all-over web with no centre and no edge.

 Brown and Silver I, painted around 1951 by Jackson Pollock — "Jack the Dripper," the wild man of
 Abstract Expressionism and the artist who, more than any other, announced that American painting had
 arrived.

 Pollock did something genuinely new. He took the canvas off the easel, laid it on the floor, and moved
 around and over it, flinging, dripping and pouring liquid paint from sticks and brushes, his whole body
 in motion. The result isn't a picture of anything — it's a record of the act of making it, a frozen dance.
 There's no focal point; the energy spreads edge to edge, "all-over," so your eye keeps roving with no
 place to rest. Critics called it "action painting": the canvas as an arena for an event rather than a surface
 for an image. It looks like chaos, but stand with it and a strange, taut order emerges — rhythm, density,
 control inside the abandon. This was the moment European artists started watching New York. The
 other side of the post-war coin — Europe's tortured human figure, Bacon — is round the corner.
