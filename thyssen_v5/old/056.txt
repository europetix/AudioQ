    LISTEN FOR
    How Dalí paints the split-second of a dream just before waking — and the science behind it.


 A nude woman floating, asleep, just above a flat rock over the sea — and erupting beside her, two tigers
 and a long bayonet leaping out of a burst pomegranate, with an elephant on impossibly thin legs in the
 distance.

 Dream Caused by the Flight of a Bee around a Pomegranate a Second before Waking, painted in 1944
 by Salvador Dalí, the Catalan showman-genius and the most famous Surrealist of all. The sleeping
 woman is Gala, his wife and muse.

 The whole picture is a single instant — the split second before waking — visualised. The long title tells
 you the trigger: a real bee buzzing around Gala as she naps, its tiny sound bursting in her sleeping mind
 into this cascade of images. The bayonet about to prick her arm is the sting that will wake her; the
 leaping tigers come roaring out of a fish out of the pomegranate, dream-logic at full tilt. Dalí called his
 method "paranoiac-critical" — deliberately courting a waking delirium to dredge up these images, then
 painting them with old-master precision so the impossible feels disturbingly solid. He was also
 fascinated by the new science of the atom and by Freud's theory of dreams, and both haunt this canvas.
 The wildest dream content, delivered with the cool exactitude of a Dutch still life. The other face of
 Surrealism — chance and the half-formed — is just along here.
