 Dada and the dream: Surrealism
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 44.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Dalí, Max Ernst and Yves Tanguy.
    LISTEN FOR
    How the Surrealists went hunting for the unconscious — by dream, by accident, by chance.


 The First World War broke something. Millions dead in the mud for nothing — and to a group of artists,
 the whole edifice of Western reason and "civilised" values stood exposed as a murderous fraud. Their
 response was Dada: deliberate nonsense, anti-art, absurdity as protest, mocking a culture that had
 marched itself into the slaughter.

 Out of Dada grew something more lasting — Surrealism. Led by the writer André Breton and electrified
 by Sigmund Freud's new science of the unconscious, the Surrealists set out to liberate the hidden mind:
 dreams, desire, the irrational, the things reason keeps locked away. Some, like Dalí, painted hyper-real
 dreamscapes where impossible things meet with hallucinatory clarity. Others, like Max Ernst and Joan
 Miró, invented chance techniques and floating biomorphic forms to let the unconscious do the
 inventing.

 It became the most famous avant-garde of all, reshaping art, film, advertising and the way we still talk
 about dreams. Welcome to the world beneath the rational. Dalí and the dream-painters, a little way on.
