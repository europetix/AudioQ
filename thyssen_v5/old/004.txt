 Jan van Eyck, The Annunciation Diptych
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 3 — a tiny grisaille diptych painted to look like carved stone.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Jan van Eyck, The Annunciation Diptych, c. 1433–35 — oil on panel.
    LISTEN FOR
    How Van Eyck fakes sculpture in paint so well your eye refuses to believe it's flat.


 Two narrow panels, side by side, that look at first like carved stone statues in shadowy niches — until
 you realise they're paintings, pretending.

 A diptych of The Annunciation by Jan van Eyck, the Flemish genius who, around 1433–35, did things
 with oil paint nobody had done before. The angel Gabriel on one side, the Virgin Mary on the other —
 but painted entirely in cool grey, grisaille, to imitate sculpture, complete with the illusion of stone
 niches, cast shadows, and even reflections in the polished black bases.

 This is a magic trick four hundred years ahead of its time. Van Eyck is showing off — painting is so
 powerful, he's saying, that it can perfectly counterfeit a rival art form, sculpture, and then go further,
 adding reflections and light that real stone could never hold. Get close and look at the bases: the figures
 seem to be reflected in the dark marble beneath them. Van Eyck was among the first to truly master oil
 paint — building up colour and light in thin, glowing layers — and that technical revolution, born in
 Flanders, would change everything. He makes you doubt your own eyes. That's the point. More northern
 marvels are just here.
