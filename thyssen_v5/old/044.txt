    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 35, the Expressionist rooms — a girl in jagged, acid colour.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Ernst Ludwig Kirchner, Fränzi in front of a Carved Chair, 1910 — oil on canvas.
    LISTEN FOR
    The raw, deliberately crude energy of the Brücke group, and the child model at its centre.


 A jarring, vivid portrait of a young girl — acid greens and oranges, a mask-like face, a strange carved
 figure looming on the chair beside her.

 Fränzi in front of a Carved Chair, painted around 1910 by Ernst Ludwig Kirchner, the driving force of
 Die Brücke. Fränzi was a young girl who modelled for the group in their Dresden studio, a freewheeling
 bohemian world the artists treated as a revolt against stiff bourgeois Germany.

 Look at the colour — it's deliberately wrong. Her face is greenish, the background hot orange, the whole
 thing clashing and raw, because Kirchner isn't describing how the girl looked but firing the emotional
 charge of the encounter straight at you. The brushwork is fast and rough; the carved figure on the chair,
 borrowed from the African and Oceanic art the group admired, adds a note of the primal and uncanny.
 There's something both tender and unsettling here, a nervous intensity that runs through all of Die
 Brücke. Within a few years the First World War would shatter this little utopia — Kirchner had a
 breakdown in the army and never fully recovered. The raw nerve of it is the whole point. A colder,
 sharper kind of portrait hangs nearby — Otto Dix, just ahead.
