 André Derain, Waterloo Bridge
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 40 — a London bridge in shocking, unreal colour.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    André Derain, Waterloo Bridge, 1906 — oil on canvas.
    LISTEN FOR
    Fauvism at full blast: the same London Monet painted, but in pure wild colour.


 A London bridge ablaze with impossible colour — the Thames in hot pinks, oranges and emerald
 greens, the water a patchwork of pure, unmixed pigment.

 Waterloo Bridge, painted in 1906 by André Derain, who with Henri Matisse led the Fauves — the "wild
 beasts," as a shocked critic called them in 1905 for their riotous, unnatural colour. The dealer Ambroise
 Vollard had sent Derain to London to paint the city; where Monet had wrapped these same bridges in
 delicate fog, Derain set them on fire.

 This is colour cut loose from description. The Fauves took the lesson of Van Gogh and Gauguin — that
 colour could express feeling rather than copy nature — and pushed it to the limit: here the river is
 whatever colour the painting needs it to be, laid on in bold, separate strokes of dazzling intensity. It's
 joyous, daring, almost crude, and utterly modern. Fauvism burned bright for only a few years, but it lit
 the fuse for everything that followed — it was the first of the twentieth century's great colour
 revolutions, and Matisse would spend the rest of his life refining what these wild canvases began. After
 this burst of colour, painting takes its most radical step yet — into pure abstraction, round the corner.
