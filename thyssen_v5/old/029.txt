 An elegant English portrait — a young woman, fashionably dressed, set with easy grace against a soft,
 feathery landscape painted in silvery, flickering strokes.

 Portrait of Sarah Buxton, by Thomas Gainsborough, who with his rival Sir Joshua Reynolds dominated
 eighteenth-century British portraiture. This panel hung for years at Daylesford House, the English home
 of the Baron and Baroness Thyssen, before joining the collection.

 Gainsborough is the one to watch. He always insisted that portraits were merely his trade and landscape
 his real love — and you can feel it here, in how the sitter melts into a shimmering, half-invented
 parkland behind her, the boundary between figure and setting deliberately soft. Look at the handling of
 the silk and lace: up close it's almost abstract, a flurry of quick, feathery touches, yet from a step back it
 reads as the most refined fabric imaginable. His early landscapes drank from the Dutch masters you saw
 upstairs; his portraits married that love of nature to the faces of the British gentry. The result defined an
 image of that confident, landed class — at ease, gracious, entirely sure of its place in the world. And
 then, in the very next room, all that politeness shatters — Goya, round the corner.
