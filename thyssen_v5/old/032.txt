 Caspar David Friedrich, Easter Morning
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 29 — three women on a path under a pale dawn sky.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Caspar David Friedrich, Easter Morning, c. 1828–35 — oil on canvas.
    LISTEN FOR
    How Friedrich loads an empty landscape with the whole weight of death and resurrection.


 A still, solemn landscape — three small figures on a winding path between bare, leafless trees, under a
 pale dawn sky, the whole scene hushed and luminous.

 Easter Morning by Caspar David Friedrich, the greatest painter of German Romanticism. The Thyssen's
 holding of his work is precious — Friedrich is rare outside Germany — and this small canvas distils
 everything that makes him extraordinary.

 With Friedrich, nothing is just scenery; every element carries meaning. Read this one slowly. The bare
 trees and the season — late winter giving way to spring — speak of death and rebirth. The moon still
 hanging in the sky stands for the passing world and the hope of eternal life; the breaking dawn is the
 Resurrection itself, which is why it's Easter morning. The three small women walking the path become
 every soul making its way toward the light. Friedrich fills the canvas with quiet emptiness and sets tiny
 human figures against the vast, so that you, too, feel small before something infinite. After the noise of
 revolution and war, he offers silence, awe, and the individual soul alone before the universe — one of
 the deepest roots of modern art. Now — down the stairs, into the modern world, right along here.
