 The centre of art crosses the Atlantic
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Rooms 46–49.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    O'Keeffe, Hopper, Pollock and Bacon — mid-century America and Britain.
    LISTEN FOR
    How New York took over from Paris as the engine of modern art after 1945.


 For everything you've seen so far, the capital of Western art was a European city — Florence, Venice,
 Amsterdam, Paris. In these rooms, that ends. After the Second World War, for the first time, the centre
 of gravity crosses the ocean and lands in New York.

 There were reasons. Fleeing fascism, many of Europe's greatest modern artists had emigrated to the
 United States in the 1930s and '40s, bringing the whole avant-garde with them. And after the war, a
 confident, wealthy America was ready to build something new. Out of that came Abstract
 Expressionism — huge, gestural, ambitious canvases by painters like Jackson Pollock, the first
 American movement to lead the world rather than follow Europe.

 But America had its realists too — Edward Hopper, painting the loneliness inside the modern city —
 and Europe was far from finished, with figures like Francis Bacon staring unflinchingly at the post-war
 human animal. These rooms hold some of the Thyssen's most beloved modern works. The quietest of
 them might be the most famous — Hopper, a little further on.
