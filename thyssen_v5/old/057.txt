 Max Ernst, Tanguy and the Surrealist unconscious
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 44 — eerie, invented dream-landscapes.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Max Ernst, Yves Tanguy and the Surrealist unconscious.
    LISTEN FOR
    The Surrealist techniques for letting the unconscious — not the hand — make the picture.


 Strange, hallucinatory canvases — eerie deserted landscapes with unidentifiable forms, or surfaces full
 of odd textures that seem grown rather than painted.

 Work by Max Ernst, Yves Tanguy and other Surrealists who chased the unconscious not through
 dream-photography like Dalí, but through chance and invented technique.

 These artists distrusted the conscious, controlling mind — that was the mind that had built the war. So
 they invented ways to bypass it. Max Ernst pioneered frottage (rubbing paper over textured surfaces to
 coax up images) and grattage (scraping paint), letting accident suggest forests, creatures, ruined worlds.
 Yves Tanguy painted vast, silent dreamscapes populated by soft, boneless forms that resemble nothing
 on earth yet feel weirdly alive. The shared goal was automatism — making art the way you
 free-associate or dream, without the editing, planning mind interfering. It can be unsettling, even a little
 queasy, and that's the intention: you're looking at images dredged from below the surface of thought.
 After the dream, history comes crashing back — and the centre of art crosses the Atlantic for good.
 America, abstraction, and the modern condition, just ahead — down toward the last rooms.
