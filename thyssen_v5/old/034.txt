 The American sublime: Cole, Church and the
 Hudson River School
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 31 — vast, luminous American wilderness.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    The Hudson River School — Thomas Cole, Frederic Church and their circle.
    LISTEN FOR
    How a young nation painted its own landscape as a kind of scripture.


 Big, luminous landscapes — dramatic American wilderness, glowing skies, mountains and rivers
 stretching toward a radiant horizon.

 Paintings of the Hudson River School, America's first homegrown art movement, led by Thomas Cole
 and his brilliant pupil Frederic Edwin Church, working from the 1820s through mid-century.

 To a young United States, the wilderness was the national identity. Europe had cathedrals and ruins and
 centuries of history; America had something it decided was even greater — untouched nature on a
 colossal scale, seemingly given by God. So these painters made landscape into a kind of patriotic
 scripture: glowing, awe-struck, enormous, every sunset and mountain range charged with destiny and
 moral meaning. Church in particular would draw crowds who paid admission to see a single giant
 canvas, as if it were a movie. But look closer and there's often a shadow too — a blasted tree, a sign of
 settlement encroaching — an early, uneasy awareness that this paradise was already being lost to the
 axe and the railroad. It's the American dream and its doubts, painted in light. Few European museums
 tell this story at all; the Thyssen tells it beautifully. From the wild continent to high society, a great
 American abroad — Sargent, round the corner.
