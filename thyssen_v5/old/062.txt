 Francis Bacon, Portrait of George Dyer in a Mirror
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 49 — a contorted man and his reflection in a round mirror.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Francis Bacon, Portrait of George Dyer in a Mirror, 1968 — oil on canvas.
    LISTEN FOR
    Bacon's lover, the warped mirror, and the violence Bacon finds in a single human face.


 A lone man twisted in a round-backed chair, his face and body smeared and distorted, his reflection in a
 mirror behind him just as broken — caged in a bare, airless space.

 Portrait of George Dyer in a Mirror, painted in 1968 by Francis Bacon, the British painter who stared
 harder at the post-war human animal than almost anyone. George Dyer was Bacon's lover and his most
 painted model — a relationship as turbulent as the paint.

 While the Americans next door went abstract, Bacon stayed brutally figurative — but his figures are
 like nothing before him. He smears and wrenches Dyer's face and body until the man seems caught
 mid-motion, mid-dissolve, raw flesh trapped in cold geometric space and doubled in an unforgiving
 mirror. After the death camps and the atomic bomb, Bacon painted the human being stripped of comfort
 and dignity. Yet he revered the old masters — Velázquez above all — so even at his most savage he's in
 dialogue with the tradition hanging upstairs. There's real tenderness buried in the violence here, too: this
 is someone he loved. A few years later Dyer would die, and Bacon would paint his grief for the rest of
 his life. Hard to look at, hard to look away from. After this existential weight, art gets loud, bright and
 ironic — Pop, a short way on.
