 Roy Lichtenstein, Woman in Bath
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 52, the Pop rooms — a comic-strip woman in a bath.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Roy Lichtenstein, Woman in Bath, 1963 — oil and magna on canvas.
    LISTEN FOR
    How Lichtenstein blows up cheap print — Ben-Day dots and all — into high art.


 A big, flat, comic-book image — a woman rendered in bold black outlines, primary colour, and those
 unmistakable rows of mechanical printed dots.

 Woman in Bath, painted in 1963 by Roy Lichtenstein, who, with Andy Warhol, became the face of
 American Pop Art by stealing his style straight from the comics and advertisements.

 Lichtenstein took the lowest, most disposable imagery imaginable — cheap romance and war comics —
 and blew it up to the scale of history painting, copying even the crude printing process: the heavy black
 outlines, the flat zones of primary colour, and above all the Ben-Day dots, the little mechanical dots that
 comic printers used for shading, which he painted painstakingly by hand. The effect is deadpan and
 witty: a throwaway image, monumentalised and frozen, forcing you to ask what makes one image "art"
 and another mere commerce. Is he mocking mass culture or loving it? Both, and neither — that cool
 refusal to tell you is pure Pop. There's real formal beauty in the precision, too. From the comics to the
 junk-and-image collisions of Rauschenberg and the Postpop rooms — right along here.
