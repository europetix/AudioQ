 Painting lets go of the world: abstraction
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Rooms 39–43.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Kandinsky, El Lissitzky and the first abstract painting.
    LISTEN FOR
    The moment art stops picturing things and becomes pure shape, colour and rhythm.


 Here is one of the boldest leaps in the entire history of art: the moment painting decided it didn't need to
 depict anything at all.

 For five centuries — everything you've seen since those gold-ground panels at the top of the building —
 painting had been a window onto something: a saint, a face, a landscape, an apple. Then, around
 1910–15, a handful of artists asked a dangerous question: what if a painting could be like music — pure
 colour, line and form, moving you directly, with no subject at all?

 Several reached it at once. Wassily Kandinsky, coming out of the Blue Rider, let his forms dissolve into
 floating colour and gesture. The Dutchman Piet Mondrian stripped everything down to grids of black
 line and primary colour, hunting for a hidden universal order. And in Russia, a whole explosive
 avant-garde — Goncharova, Malevich and others — raced toward abstraction in their own way. It was
 utopian, almost mystical: many believed they were painting a new spiritual age into being. Kandinsky
 first, a short way on.
