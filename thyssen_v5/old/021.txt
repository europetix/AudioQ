 Holland: a republic of ordinary things
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Rooms 23–28.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Rembrandt, Hals, Saenredam and the Dutch Golden Age.
    LISTEN FOR
    Why a small Protestant republic started painting itself — faces, churches, weather, things.


 Something genuinely new happens in these Dutch rooms, and it's worth pausing to feel why. The
 seventeenth-century Dutch Republic was unlike anywhere else in Europe: Protestant, so no grand
 church commissions; a republic, so no royal court; and prosperous, thanks to trade and a confident
 middle class. So who bought paintings? Ordinary citizens — merchants, brewers, shopkeepers — for
 their own homes.

 And that changed what got painted. Instead of saints and kings, Dutch artists painted the world these
 buyers lived in: their own faces, their families, their flat green landscapes under enormous skies, their
 tidy interiors, the gleam of a lemon on a laid table. Painting came down out of heaven and the palace
 and settled into the living room.

 It produced some of the most quietly perfect art ever made — and one outright genius, Rembrandt, who
 took this ordinary world and found in it the full depth of the human soul. Watch how much feeling these
 painters wring from the everyday: a face, a sky, a swept church floor. Rembrandt first — looking at
 himself, as he did all his life, close by.
