 El Lissitzky, Proun 1 C
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 43 — flat geometric shapes hovering on a pale field.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    El Lissitzky, Proun 1 C, c. 1919 — oil on panel.
    LISTEN FOR
    The Russian avant-garde dream of building a new world out of pure geometry.


 A spare, precise abstract composition — geometric forms, bars and planes, hovering in a weightless
 space that feels part painting, part architectural blueprint.

 Proun 1 C, painted around 1919 by El Lissitzky, one of the driving forces of the Russian avant-garde in
 the white-hot years around the 1917 Revolution. "Proun" was his own invented word — roughly,
 "project for the affirmation of the new" — for these works he saw as a way-station between painting and
 architecture.

 This is abstraction with a mission. Where Kandinsky chased the spiritual and the Dutchman Mondrian
 (also in these rooms) hunted a perfect universal balance of line and primary colour, the Russians
 believed art could literally help build a new society. Lissitzky's floating geometric forms aren't meant to
 depict anything — they're blueprints for a new world, models of a future of pure rational order, rising
 after the upheaval swept the old one away. For a few electric years Moscow was as radical as anywhere
 on earth, before Stalin crushed the avant-garde and demanded propaganda instead. You're looking at
 one of the great hopeful what-ifs of modern art. Now to the movement that broke the world into facets
 and never put it back the same — Cubism, right along here.
