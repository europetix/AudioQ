 A reclining nude — a nymph stretched out beside a spring, seemingly asleep, watched over by a couple
 of partridges and a quiet wooded landscape.

 The Nymph at the Fountain, painted around 1530–34 by Lucas Cranach the Elder, court painter to the
 Electors of Saxony and one of the most productive, recognisable artists of the age. He painted versions
 of this reclining nymph more than once; she's a Renaissance invention dressed up as an antique legend.

 There's usually an inscription with these — the nymph announces that she is the guardian of the sacred
 spring, and warns you not to disturb her sleep. But of course the whole painting is an invitation to look.
 Cranach's nudes are unmistakable: cool, pale, elongated, slightly artificial, often wearing nothing but a
 transparent veil and a piece of elaborate jewellery — eroticism with a knowing, courtly wink rather than
 Italian idealism. She's "asleep," but her eyes aren't quite closed. It's sophisticated, a little teasing, made
 for a worldly aristocratic patron who'd enjoy both the classical alibi and the frank sensuality underneath
 it. And the other German titan of the age painted the most powerful man in England — Holbein, through
 here.
