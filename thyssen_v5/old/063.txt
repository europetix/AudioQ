 Pop: art eats the supermarket
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Rooms 52–56.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Lichtenstein and Rauschenberg — American Pop.
    LISTEN FOR
    How art swallowed advertising, comics and consumer junk — and called it the new reality.


 After the heavy seriousness of the post-war years — all that angst and abstraction — a younger
 generation in the late 1950s and '60s did something cheeky and revolutionary: they let the everyday
 consumer world flood straight into art.

 This was Pop. Instead of tortured souls or pure abstraction, here were comic strips, soup cans,
 advertisements, film stars, packaging — the bright, mass-produced imagery of modern consumer life,
 presented deadpan and huge. Was it a celebration of this new abundance, or a cool critique of its
 shallowness? Pop was deliberately ambiguous, and that ambiguity was the point. It blurred the line
 between high art and low commercial culture until you couldn't tell where one stopped and the other
 began.

 It was mostly an Anglo-American explosion — Warhol, Lichtenstein and Rauschenberg in New York
 foremost among them — and it changed art's relationship with everyday life forever. After Pop came its
 many afterlives, the "Postpop" rooms beyond. Lichtenstein first, over here.
