 A small but commanding portrait — the unmistakable bulk of King Henry VIII, square-on, glittering
 with jewels and gold thread, filling the panel with sheer authority.

 Portrait of Henry VIII of England, painted around 1537 by Hans Holbein the Younger, the German
 master who became court painter to the Tudor king. It is one of the most famous royal portraits ever
 made — the image through which the whole world still pictures Henry VIII.

 This is propaganda of the highest order, and it works on you even now. Holbein squares the king's
 massive shoulders to the picture plane, loads him with cloth-of-gold, fur and gemstones rendered with
 hypnotic precision, and fixes him with a small, hard, frontal stare — so he seems to bear down on you
 with the full weight of absolute power. There's no flattery of charm here; the message is dominance,
 wealth, and an unanswerable will. Holbein was one of the supreme portraitists in history, able to make
 jewels and brocade gleam like the real thing while pinning a sitter's character in a single look. Versions
 and copies of this image spread across Europe and shaped Henry's fearsome reputation for centuries. To
 see the original, this close, is to feel the chill of Tudor power. Now from the cold English court to the
 warm light of Venice, just beyond.
