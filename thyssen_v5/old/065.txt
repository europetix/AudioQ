 Robert Rauschenberg, Express
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Rooms 53–56 — a sprawling collage of silkscreened images.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Robert Rauschenberg, Express, 1963 — oil and silkscreen on canvas.
    LISTEN FOR
    How Rauschenberg floods the canvas with the noise of modern America.


 A big, restless canvas in the final rooms — a teeming collage of silkscreened photographs (a horse and
 rider mid-jump, dancers, a climber, a nude descending a staircase) overlaid with bold, rapid
 brushstrokes.

 Express, made in 1963 by Robert Rauschenberg — one of his most important works and a treasure of
 this collection. It's among the first paintings in which he used silkscreen to print photographic images
 straight onto the canvas. (The museum is marking Rauschenberg's centenary with a special display
 devoted to this very painting.)

 Rauschenberg said he wanted to work "in the gap between art and life," and Express lives right in that
 gap. He bridged the older Abstract Expressionists and the young Pop artists — keeping their gestural
 brushwork while flooding the canvas with the mass-media imagery of modern life. The title hints at
 speed and movement, and the picture is full of it: the leaping horse, the dancers, the wheels, the figure
 descending the stair, all enlarged to a grainy, newsprint roughness and woven together so your eye
 keeps moving, never settling. You've travelled a long way from Duccio's gold leaf at the top of the
 building — from a single sacred image made for prayer to a swirling overload of media imagery made
 for a world drowning in pictures. That whole journey is the point. One more floor to go — a second,
 gentler collection waits below. This way, down to Level 0.
