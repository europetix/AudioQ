 Berthe Morisot and Mary Cassatt
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 33 — intimate scenes of women and children.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Berthe Morisot and Mary Cassatt, the women of Impressionism.
    LISTEN FOR
    What the two great women Impressionists could paint that their male colleagues couldn't reach.


 Intimate, light-filled scenes — a woman, a child, a quiet domestic moment — painted with the same
 broken Impressionist brightness but a distinctly tender, inward eye.

 Paintings by Berthe Morisot and Mary Cassatt, the two outstanding women at the heart of
 Impressionism — Morisot French and central to the original group, Cassatt an American who joined
 them in Paris.

 They were full members of the movement, not followers — Morisot exhibited in almost every
 Impressionist show and was as radical a brush as any of the men. But their subject matter was shaped by
 what society allowed women to access: not the cafés, bars and backstage worlds the men painted, but
 the domestic sphere — mothers and children, women reading, dressing, taking tea, lost in thought. Far
 from a limitation, they turned it into a strength, bringing a closeness and psychological tenderness to
 these private moments that the men rarely matched. Morisot's brushwork is famously free and feathery;
 Cassatt's mother-and-child scenes have a frank, unsentimental warmth. They're a reminder that the story
 of modern art has always had more authors than the famous list admits. Now two painters who pushed
 past Impressionism into something stranger — Van Gogh and Cézanne, close by.
