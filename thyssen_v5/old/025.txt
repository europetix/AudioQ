 Dutch landscape
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Rooms 26–28 — wide skies and low Dutch horizons.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Seventeenth-century Dutch landscape — van Goyen, Ruisdael and their circle.
    LISTEN FOR
    How the new Dutch Republic learned to take pride in plain weather, flat land and big sky.


 The landscape rooms — low horizons, vast cloudy skies taking up most of the canvas, flat green
 country, a windmill or a river, weather rolling through.

 The great Dutch landscape tradition — masters like Jacob van Ruisdael, Jan van Goyen and Salomon
 van Ruysdael, who turned the unremarkable flatness of their own country into one of the glories of
 European painting.

 Notice the proportion: the land is squeezed down to a thin strip along the bottom, and the sky —
 restless, luminous, full of moving cloud — swells to fill two-thirds or more of the picture. That's the
 truth of Holland, a low country under an enormous, ever-changing sky, much of it reclaimed from the
 sea. There are no mountains, no drama of terrain; the drama is all in the light and weather. These
 painters invented landscape as a subject worthy of serious art entirely on its own, with no myth or story
 propping it up — just this place, this light, this passing moment of cloud. Two centuries later the
 Impressionists would call that revolutionary. The Dutch did it calmly, first. Now the world tips into the
 elegant eighteenth century, a little further on.
