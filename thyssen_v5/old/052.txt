 Cubism: the world in pieces
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Rooms 41–42.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Picasso, Braque and Juan Gris.
    LISTEN FOR
    How Cubism shattered the single viewpoint and rebuilt objects from every side at once.


 Around 1907, two young painters in Paris — Pablo Picasso and Georges Braque — looked hard at
 Cézanne's faceted world and then did something no one had dared: they smashed the single, fixed
 viewpoint that had governed Western painting since the Renaissance.

 Their idea was radical and a little dizzying. Instead of painting an object from one spot, frozen in one
 instant, they walked around it in their minds and put all the views down at once — front, side, top —
 flattened and interlocked on the canvas. A face, a guitar, a bottle would shatter into overlapping planes,
 the way we actually build up a thing in our minds from many glances over time. They worked so closely
 in these years, Braque said, that they were "roped together like mountaineers."

 It looks difficult, and it was meant to stretch you. But Cubism is the hinge of modern art — almost
 everything that follows, from abstraction to collage, runs through it. Picasso, the restless giant, first —
 not far on.
