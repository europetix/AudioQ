 A portrait of the artist himself — a solid, middle-aged man in a dark hat, gold chains across his chest,
 emerging from a warm brown gloom, looking steadily out.

 A Self-portrait by Rembrandt van Rijn, from around 1642–43 — one of roughly eighty self-portraits he
 made across his life, more than almost any artist before him. Here he's at the height of his Amsterdam
 success, prosperous and confident, the gold chains a sign of honour and status.

 Nobody has ever looked at themselves the way Rembrandt did — not out of vanity, but as a lifelong
 experiment in what a face can hold. He used himself as the model always available, free, and infinitely
 patient, and across the decades the self-portraits become a single, unflinching autobiography in paint:
 the cocky young man, the successful master you see here, and later the bankrupt, grieving old man who
 painted himself with merciless honesty and overwhelming compassion. Look at how the light finds his
 face out of the shadow, and how loosely, almost roughly, the paint is handled up close — and yet how
 completely alive he is. He's not performing for you. He's studying himself, and letting you watch.
 Beside him, a master of the lighter touch caught a whole family in the open air — Frans Hals, a little
 way on.
