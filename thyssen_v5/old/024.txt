    Why an empty whitewashed church became a subject — and the Protestant world behind it.


 A precise, pale, almost empty painting of a church — clean architecture, soft light, a great calm hush
 hanging over the whole thing.

 The West Façade of the Church of Saint Mary in Utrecht, painted in 1662 by Pieter Jansz. Saenredam,
 the painter who made a whole career out of one subject: the interiors and exteriors of Dutch churches,
 rendered with almost scientific accuracy.

 These Protestant churches had been stripped — the Reformation cleared out the statues, the gold, the
 painted altarpieces — and Saenredam saw the strange new beauty in that emptiness: bare whitewashed
 stone, clear glass, pure architectural geometry, flooded with soft northern daylight. He worked from
 careful on-site drawings and measurements, almost like an architect, then composed paintings of
 extraordinary serenity. There are no dramatic events here, often barely any people — just light, space,
 stone and silence. It's a kind of devotion all its own: holiness found not in spectacle but in clarity and
 quiet. After the hush of the church, step out under the huge Dutch sky — landscape, just ahead.
