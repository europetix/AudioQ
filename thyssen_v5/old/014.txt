 Venice: painting becomes colour
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 11.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Titian and El Greco — the Venetian sixteenth century.
    LISTEN FOR
    How Venice swapped Florentine drawing for pure, smouldering colour and loose paint.


 Now to Venice — and to a completely different idea of what a painting even is. In Florence, art was
 about disegno: drawing, line, design, the intellectual scaffolding underneath. In Venice, it was about
 colore: colour, light, atmosphere, paint itself laid on so you can feel the brush.

 There's a reason. Venice was a city built on water and trade, soaked in shifting light and shimmering
 reflection, awash in luxurious imported pigments. Its painters — Titian above all, with Tintoretto and
 Bassano around him — learned to build a picture out of glowing colour and loaded brushwork rather
 than hard outline. They painted flesh that looks warm, fabric you want to touch, skies that breathe.

 This Venetian revolution in colour is one of the great turning points in art; everyone downstream, from
 Rubens to the Impressionists, drinks from it. And in this same room hangs a young outsider who trained
 here before becoming the most singular painter in Spanish history — El Greco. Venice first, round the
 corner.
