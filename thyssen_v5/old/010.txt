 The German eye
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Rooms 8–9.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Dürer, Cranach and Holbein — the German and Northern Renaissance.
    LISTEN FOR
    The sharp, precise, slightly unsettling way Northern painters looked at the world.


 Step into the German rooms and the temperature changes. If the Italians were chasing grace and
 harmony, the German painters of the same years were after something sharper — intensity, detail, a
 slightly uncomfortable truthfulness, and a love of the strange.

 This was the world of the Reformation, of Luther and religious upheaval, and you can feel the
 seriousness of it. Two giants dominate. Albrecht Dürer — the supreme genius of the German
 Renaissance, a man who travelled to Italy to learn its lessons and brought them home filtered through a
 northern obsession with detail. And Lucas Cranach the Elder — court painter, friend of Luther, and the
 inventor of a very particular kind of cool, knowing, slightly wicked elegance, especially in his nudes.

 Between them they give you the two poles of German art: Dürer's piercing intellectual force, and
 Cranach's sly, courtly charm. Both are here at full power. Dürer first — and it's one of the strangest,
 most crowded faces-paintings you'll ever see, right this way.
