 Paris, 1874: the light moves
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 33 — the Impressionist rooms.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Manet, Degas, Monet, Morisot, Cassatt, Van Gogh and Cézanne.
    LISTEN FOR
    The single richest room in the museum, and the revolution in how light got painted.


 Now to the upheaval whose name everyone knows. In Paris in the 1870s, a group of young painters got
 tired of the brown, polished, studio-bound art the official Salon demanded — grand historical scenes
 finished to a glassy shine. They wanted to paint what the eye actually sees: light, the fleeting moment,
 modern life as it happens.

 So they did something radical. They went outdoors, painting directly in front of the subject. They used
 quick, broken, visible strokes of pure colour to catch the shimmer of sunlight, water, weather. They
 painted not noble myths but ordinary modern things — a train station, a dancer, a boating party, a
 woman on horseback in the park. Critics mocked them; one sneered at a Monet titled Impression,
 Sunrise, and the insult — "Impressionists" — stuck.

 It's hard now to feel how shocking this was, because they won so completely that their way of seeing
 became our way of seeing. Manet, Monet, Degas, Morisot — the room is full of them. Manet first, over
 here.
