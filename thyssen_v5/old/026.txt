 The Grand Tour: elegance and the view
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Rooms 16–24.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Canaletto, Fragonard and Gainsborough — the eighteenth century.
    LISTEN FOR
    The age of travel, souvenirs and flattery — view-paintings and silken portraits.


 The eighteenth century is the age of elegance, wit and travel — and these rooms have a lighter, brighter
 air than the heavy Baroque you've just left.

 Two things drive the painting here. One is the Grand Tour: the fashion among wealthy young
 Europeans, the British especially, for a long educational journey through Italy. They wanted souvenirs
 — and so a whole industry of dazzling city "view paintings" grew up, above all in Venice, where
 Canaletto turned the city itself into the ultimate luxury postcard. The other is the Rococo: a French style
 of feathery lightness, pastel colour, flirtation and pleasure, made for aristocratic salons where life was a
 charming game.

 And alongside the frothy French scenes, you'll find the cool confidence of British portraiture —
 Gainsborough, Reynolds and their rivals, painting the landed gentry with easy grandeur. It's a century
 that prized charm, refinement and surface delight — right up until, at the very end, revolution and a
 darker genius named Goya blow the whole pretty world apart. Venice first, the most beautiful view in
 the building, right this way.
