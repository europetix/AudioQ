    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 5 — a Florentine woman in strict profile, richly dressed.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Domenico Ghirlandaio, Portrait of Giovanna Tornabuoni, 1489–90 — tempera on panel.
    LISTEN FOR
    Why she faces sideways, the Latin verse behind her, and the young wife who died before it was done.


 The face you've seen on every poster and ticket — a young woman in strict profile, golden hair coiled
 and bound, in a gorgeous brocade and red gown, against a dark recess holding a few precise objects.

 Portrait of Giovanna degli Albizzi Tornabuoni, painted 1489–90 by Domenico Ghirlandaio, the leading
 Florentine workshop master of his day — and, not incidentally, the man in whose workshop a teenage
 Michelangelo trained. Giovanna was a young Florentine noblewoman, married into the powerful
 Tornabuoni family.

 And here is the heartbreak inside the beauty. By the time this portrait was finished, Giovanna was
 almost certainly already dead — she died in 1488, at around twenty, in childbirth. So this isn't a portrait
 of a living sitter; it's an act of memory and love, a husband's attempt to hold on to a face. Look at the
 Latin inscription on the cartellino behind her: it's a line adapted from the Roman poet Martial that says,
 in effect, "Art, if only you could also depict her character and soul, there would be no more beautiful
 painting on earth." Ghirlandaio is admitting the limit of his own art — he can give you the perfect
 surface of her, the hair and the jewels and the proud profile, but not the person who is gone. The objects
 in the niche — a prayer book, a brooch, a string of coral — speak of piety, virtue and protection. It's the
 museum's signature image, and once you know the story, that flawless profile becomes one of the most
 calmly moving things in the building. Downstairs lies Germany, and a very different intensity — this
 way to the next rooms.
