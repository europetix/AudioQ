 Francisco de Goya, Tío Paquete
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 29 — a blind beggar's head looming out of the dark.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Francisco de Goya, Tío Paquete, c. 1819–20 — oil on canvas.
    LISTEN FOR
    The Madrid street figure Goya paints with terrifying, modern honesty.


 A small, dark, unsettling portrait — the head and shoulders of an old blind man, his eye-sockets sunken
 and sightless, his mouth twisted into something between a grin and a grimace.

 Tío Paquete — "Uncle Paquete" — painted around 1819–20 by Francisco de Goya, the towering
 Spanish master who bridges the old world and the modern one. Paquete was a real man, a blind beggar
 well known to Madrileños, who sat on the steps of the church of San Felipe el Real and sang and played
 the guitar for coins.

 This was painted at the same time as Goya's terrifying Black Paintings, and you can feel that same
 fearless, unflinching gaze. There's no flattery, no idealising — but there's no cruelty either. Goya, deaf
 himself by now and disillusioned with the world, looks straight at this poor blind man and finds
 something raw and human and oddly tender in him. The thick, slashing brushwork and the way the face
 surges out of the brown darkness make it feel startlingly modern — more like something from a century
 later. Goya is the hinge of this whole museum: behind him, the old masters; ahead of him, the modern
 age he helped to birth. His Romantic counterpart from the north is over here.
