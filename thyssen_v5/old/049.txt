 Wassily Kandinsky, Picture with Three Spots
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 39, the abstraction rooms — colours and marks floating free.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Wassily Kandinsky, Picture with Three Spots, 1914 — oil on canvas.
    LISTEN FOR
    The moment painting lets go of the visible world — and starts behaving like music.


 A canvas of floating, colliding colour — three vivid patches anchoring a swirl of line and hue, with no
 clear "thing" to latch onto.

 Picture with Three Spots, painted in 1914 by Wassily Kandinsky, the Russian-born artist usually
 credited as a founder of abstract art — and a leading light, with Franz Marc, of the Blue Rider. The
 younger Baron Thyssen bought this canvas in Milan, and it anchors the museum's
 pioneers-of-abstraction room.

 Kandinsky was deeply musical — he experienced colours almost as sounds — and that's the key to him.
 He came to believe that colours and forms could touch the soul directly, the way a chord or a melody
 does, with no need to picture a recognisable object in between. So he let the houses and hills and riders
 of his earlier work dissolve until only the pure visual "music" remained. Don't strain to find a hidden
 picture here — there isn't one. Let the three spots and the currents of colour around them act on you the
 way music does when you stop trying to "understand" it and simply listen. He wrote a whole book about
 it, Concerning the Spiritual in Art. Now, a few rooms along, a very different route into abstraction
 begins — where a Dutchman named Mondrian turns a tree into a grid.
