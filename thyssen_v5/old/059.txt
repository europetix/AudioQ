 Georgia O'Keeffe, From the Plains II
    WHERE YOU ARE

    LISTEN FOR
    How O'Keeffe turns the memory of cattle and the Texas plains into pure colour and scale.


 A vast, simplified American landscape — bands of flaming red, orange and lemon-yellow stretching
 across a wide horizontal canvas, more sensation than scenery.

 From the Plains II, painted in 1954 by Georgia O'Keeffe — the pioneering American modernist — as a
 second version of a picture she'd first painted thirty-five years earlier, recalling cattle being herded
 across the Texas plains.

 O'Keeffe found abstraction in the real American land rather than importing European theory. Here she
 takes a memory — the noise and dust of cattle on the vast plains, the flaming colours of a Texas sunset
 — and distils it into pure colour and horizontal sweep. She said of it that "the color is just plain color out
 of the tube — red and orange to lemon," and that it almost shocked her. That's the point: she's after the
 feeling of the immense, empty land, not a faithful view of it. O'Keeffe spent decades in New Mexico
 painting bones, flowers and deserts with this same clean, swelling clarity, going entirely her own way,
 owing nothing to Paris. A quiet radical who insisted she be seen simply as an artist, not a "woman
 artist." Now the most famous lonely room in modern art — Hopper, through here.
