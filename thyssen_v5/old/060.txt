 Edward Hopper, Hotel Room

    WHAT YOU SEE
    Edward Hopper, Hotel Room, 1931 — oil on canvas.
    LISTEN FOR
    The American loneliness Hopper makes out of light, geometry and a dropped letter.


 A woman alone on the edge of a bed in a bare hotel room at night, in her slip, head bowed over a sheet
 of paper, her luggage still packed on the floor.

 Hotel Room, painted by Edward Hopper in 1931 — one of the treasures of this museum and one of the
 defining images of modern American art.

 Hopper is the poet of modern loneliness, and this is his masterpiece of it. A woman sits on the bed in an
 anonymous hotel room; it's night, and she's tired. She has taken off her hat, dress and shoes, and—too
 exhausted to unpack—she is checking the time of her train the next day. That's the whole "event." But
 look what Hopper builds from it: the hard geometry of the room, the stark electric light, the heavy
 shadows, the way she's hemmed in by furniture and walls. Everything is still, silent, suspended. We've
 all been this person — alone in a strange room far from home, caught in a flat, in-between moment.
 Hopper never tells you the story; he gives you the mood and lets your own loneliness fill it in. He once
 said he was just trying to paint sunlight on a wall — but what he really painted was the solitude at the
 heart of the modern city. It's quiet, and it's devastating. From American silence to American energy —
 Pollock, just beyond.
