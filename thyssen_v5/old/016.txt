 El Greco, The Annunciation
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 11 — an Annunciation lit by an unearthly, electric light.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    El Greco, The Annunciation, c. 1576 — oil on canvas.
    LISTEN FOR
    How a Cretan icon-painter turned Venetian colour into something visionary and strange.


 An early, surprisingly Venetian-looking Annunciation — the angel Gabriel and the Virgin Mary, with
 that first flicker of the stretched, flickering style this painter would become famous for.

 The Annunciation, painted around 1576 by El Greco — Doménikos Theotokópoulos, "the Greek." He
 was born in Crete, trained as an icon painter, came to Venice and Rome to absorb Titian and the Italians,
 and then moved to Toledo in Spain, where he became among the most extraordinary and unmistakable
 artists who ever lived.

 This is a relatively early work, so you can still see the Venetian lessons fresh on him — the colour, the
 loaded brush, the Italian architecture. But watch the edges of his style starting to vibrate: the figures
 lengthening, the space tilting, the light turning strange and electric. Within a few years, in Toledo, he'd
 push all of this to its limit — bodies stretched like flames, acid colours, a swirling spiritual intensity that
 looked like nothing before and wouldn't be matched until the modern age, when painters rediscovered
 him with awe. Here you catch him at the hinge: the gifted student becoming the visionary. Down the
 timeline, a young man in Rome is about to blow European painting wide open — the Baroque, a short
 way on.
