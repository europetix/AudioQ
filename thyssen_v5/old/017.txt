 The Baroque: a knock at the door
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 12.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Caravaggio and the new drama of light and shadow.
    LISTEN FOR
    How one painter's hard light and real bodies kicked European art into a new age.


 Cross into the seventeenth century and painting suddenly grabs you by the collar. This is the Baroque —
 the art of drama, of high contrast, of a moment caught at its most intense — and the man who lit the fuse
 was a violent, brilliant, short-lived Italian named Caravaggio.

 His upheaval was simple and total. He painted directly from life, using ordinary people as his saints and
 sinners — dirty feet, real wrinkles, the works. And he plunged them into deep darkness, lit by a single
 raking shaft of light, so the holy moment seems to erupt out of the shadows of an ordinary room. It's
 theatre. It's a spotlight. It pulls you in close and won't let go.

 Painters across Europe were stunned, and within a generation the whole continent was painting in this
 charged, shadowed, emotional new key — from Rubens's blazing energy to the quiet candlelight of the
 north. It starts with one dark canvas, over here.
