 A glamorous full-length society portrait — an elegant woman in flowing dress, painted with dazzling,
 confident, almost liquid brushwork.

 Portrait of Millicent, Duchess of Sutherland, painted in 1904 by John Singer Sargent, the American
 expatriate who became the most sought-after portraitist in the English-speaking world.

 Sargent could make paint perform. Look at the bravura of it — the satin, the skin, the sheer presence of
 the sitter conjured with strokes that look effortless and are anything but. He painted the
 Anglo-American aristocracy and plutocracy of the Gilded Age at the height of their confidence, and he
 flattered them, yes, but he also caught character: intelligence, hauteur, a flicker of the real person under
 the role. Millicent, Duchess of Sutherland, was a famous beauty, society hostess and social reformer,
 and Sargent gives her every inch of glamour while keeping her alive and particular. He learned his fluid
 touch from Velázquez and Hals — you've just seen Hals upstairs — proof of how the whole story
 connects. After this last glow of grand portraiture, painting is about to be turned upside down in Paris.
 Impressionism, a short way on.
