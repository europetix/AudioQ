 Pablo Picasso, Harlequin with a Mirror
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 41, the Cubist rooms — a harlequin and a faceted mirror.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Pablo Picasso, Harlequin with a Mirror, 1923 — oil on canvas.
    LISTEN FOR
    Picasso after Cubism, reaching back to classical order — and the figure he kept returning to.


 A solid, calm figure of a harlequin in his diamond-patterned costume, seated before a mirror —
 monumental and classical, not shattered.

 Harlequin with a Mirror, painted in 1923 by Pablo Picasso. It may surprise you, after that Cubism
 introduction, to find it so legible — but that's Picasso's whole point: having helped invent Cubism, he
 refused to be trapped by it, and in the 1920s swung back toward a serene, sculptural, classical style.

 The harlequin — the diamond-clad clown of old Italian theatre — was one of Picasso's lifelong alter
 egos, and many read this calm figure gazing into a mirror as a kind of veiled self-portrait, the artist
 contemplating himself and his many masks. After the fracturing of Cubism and the trauma of the First
 World War, a number of artists made this "return to order," reaching back to classical solidity and calm.
 But Picasso being Picasso, it was just one room he passed through; over a sixty-year career he
 reinvented himself again and again — Blue Period, Cubism, Classicism, Surrealism — restless to the
 end. Here you catch one of his most poised, beautiful moments. His co-inventor Braque, and the more
 crystalline Gris, are a few steps on.
