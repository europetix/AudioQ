 Canaletto, The Grand Canal from San Vio, Venice

    The eighteenth-century souvenir trade, and the optical trick behind Canaletto's precision.


 A crystalline view of Venice — the Grand Canal opening out under a wide blue sky, palaces lining the
 water, gondolas and working boats catching the light.

 The Grand Canal from San Vio, Venice, painted around 1723–24 by Canaletto — Giovanni Antonio
 Canal — the supreme master of the veduta, the topographical city view. He painted Venice so precisely
 and so seductively that his works became the must-have souvenir for British aristocrats on the Grand
 Tour.

 Look at the light and the air. Canaletto renders the buildings with near-architectural exactness — you
 could almost navigate by this painting — but he bathes everything in a clear, sparkling Venetian
 atmosphere that makes the whole city shimmer. He used optical aids, possibly a camera obscura, to get
 the perspective crisp, and he often adjusted reality a little, nudging buildings for a better composition.
 The result is Venice as you wish it always looked: luminous, orderly, eternal. These views shaped how
 the whole world has pictured Venice ever since — every postcard of the place is downstream of
 Canaletto. Hold this brightness in mind, because the next stop trades it for French perfume and flirtation
 — Fragonard, through here.
