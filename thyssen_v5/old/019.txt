 Peter Paul Rubens, Venus and Cupid
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Rooms 13–15 — a mirror, a goddess, and a glow of warm flesh.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Peter Paul Rubens, Venus and Cupid, c. 1606–11 — oil on canvas.
    LISTEN FOR
    How Rubens uses a mirror to pull you into the picture — and make you complicit.


 A warm, sensuous painting of the goddess Venus seen from behind, gazing at herself in a mirror held by
 Cupid, her golden hair and luminous skin filling the canvas.

 Venus and Cupid, painted around 1606–11 by Peter Paul Rubens, the Flemish master who was the most
 successful and influential painter in all of seventeenth-century Europe — and a diplomat and scholar
 besides. Here Venus, goddess of love and beauty, admires her reflection while her son Cupid holds the
 mirror.

 It's a clever device: the mirror shows us her face while we see her back, so we get all of her at once —
 and it calmly makes us the ones doing the looking, caught in the act of admiring beauty admiring itself.
 But mostly, feel the sheer life in it. Rubens learned from Titian's Venetian colour and Caravaggio's
 drama and fused them into something overflowing — warm, fleshy, glowing, abundant. His figures
 always seem to brim with health and energy and appetite for the world. He ran a huge, busy workshop
 and painted everything from altarpieces to royal commissions, but the signature is always that pulse of
 vitality. And Spain answered the Baroque in its own grave key — Zurbarán, not far on.
