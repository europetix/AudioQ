 A landscape that vibrates — fields and a few houses near a village, the whole surface alive with thick,
 rhythmic, swirling strokes of intense colour.

 Les Vessenots in Auvers, painted by Vincent van Gogh in 1890 — in the last months of his life, at
 Auvers-sur-Oise outside Paris, where he died that July at thirty-seven.

 This is the heart of Post-Impressionism — the generation that took the Impressionists' bright colour but
 wanted more than a fleeting visual impression; they wanted feeling, structure, meaning. With van Gogh,
 the feeling is everything. Look at the brushwork: every stroke is separate, urgent, directional, the paint
 squeezed on thick, so the field seems to ripple and the whole landscape pulses with a nervous, ecstatic
 energy. He's not just recording Auvers; he's pouring his own turbulent inner life into it. Van Gogh sold
 almost nothing in his lifetime and battled terrible mental illness, painting with furious speed in his final
 years — this canvas comes from that last desperate, miraculous burst. Within decades he'd be one of the
 most beloved artists on earth, the patron saint of the suffering, sincere artist. Stand close to feel the hand
 that made it. Beside him, the other great father of modern art took the opposite path — toward order —
 Cézanne, right this way.
