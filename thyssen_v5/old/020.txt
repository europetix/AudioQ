 Francisco de Zurbarán, Saint Casilda
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Rooms 13–15 — a richly dressed woman carrying roses in her skirt.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Francisco de Zurbarán, Saint Casilda, c. 1630–35 — oil on canvas.
    LISTEN FOR
    The miracle of the roses, and why a Spanish saint is dressed like a court lady.


 A striking full-length young woman in a gorgeous, jewel-embroidered gown, carrying roses in the folds
 of her skirt, set against a plain dark ground.

 Saint Casilda, painted around 1630–35 by Francisco de Zurbarán, one of the great masters of the
 Spanish Golden Age — the same seventeenth-century Spain as Velázquez and Murillo. These rooms
 hold Spain's contribution to the Baroque, and nearby hangs a tender Virgin and Child with Saint Rose of
 Viterbo by Bartolomé Esteban Murillo.

 Casilda was, by legend, the daughter of a Muslim king of Toledo who secretly carried food to Christian
 prisoners against her father's will. Caught one day with her skirt full of bread, she was saved by a
 miracle — the bread turned to roses, which is why she carries flowers here. But notice what Zurbarán
 really cares about: the fabric. He renders her embroidered silk and jewels with an almost hypnotic,
 sculptural solidity, the figure lit by a strong, raking light against deep shadow — Caravaggio's drama
 brought to Spanish soil. Zurbarán painted whole series of these single, monumental female saints, richly
 dressed and serene, and shipped many to the churches of Spanish America. Quiet, grand, and
 unmistakably Spanish. Now to the cool Protestant north — the Dutch — a few steps on.
