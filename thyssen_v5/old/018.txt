 Caravaggio, Saint Catherine of Alexandria
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 12 — a young saint half in shadow beside a great wheel.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Caravaggio, Saint Catherine of Alexandria, c. 1598–99 — oil on canvas.
    LISTEN FOR
    The knock of real light and real flesh that changed European painting overnight.


 A young woman kneeling in deep shadow, leaning on a great spiked wheel, a sword across her lap, lit
 by a hard diagonal light that picks her out of the surrounding dark.

 Saint Catherine of Alexandria, painted around 1598–99 by Caravaggio — Michelangelo Merisi — one
 of the very few works by him in Spain and a star of this collection. Catherine was an early Christian
 martyr sentenced to be broken on a spiked wheel; when the wheel miraculously shattered, she was
 beheaded instead. So she leans on the broken wheel and holds the sword that killed her, with the
 martyr's palm beside her.

 Everything that made Caravaggio revolutionary is here. The light is a hard spotlight from the upper left,
 modelling her out of near-total darkness — his famous tenebrism. The model is clearly a real woman,
 not an idealised type; you feel you could recognise her on the street. The textures are almost shockingly
 real — the gleam of the sword, the velvet, the cool skin. And there's psychological weight: she meets
 your gaze directly, calm and grave, an ordinary young woman who has accepted an extraordinary fate.
 Caravaggio lived fast and dangerously — brawls, a killing, flight, an early death — and you can feel
 that raw intensity pressed into the paint. From his shadows, the Baroque exploded outward; one of its
 greatest engines, Rubens, is right along here.
