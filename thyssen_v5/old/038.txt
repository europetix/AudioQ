 Edgar Degas, Swaying Dancer (Dancer in Green)
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 33 — a single dancer caught off-balance in green.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Edgar Degas, Swaying Dancer (Dancer in Green), 1877–79 — pastel.
    LISTEN FOR
    How Degas turns the ballet into something seen by accident, from a strange angle.


 A vivid pastel of ballet dancers — one tipping forward in a swirl of green skirts, the viewpoint high and
 oblique, as if you're looking down from a theatre box.

 Swaying Dancer (Dancer in Green), made 1877–79 by Edgar Degas, the Impressionist who was
 obsessed, above all, with the human body in motion — and especially with dancers.

 Degas insisted he wasn't really an Impressionist — he disliked painting outdoors and cared more about
 drawing than most of the group. But he shared their hunger for the modern, fleeting moment, and
 nobody caught movement like he did. Look at the radical composition: the steep downward angle, the
 dancers cut off by the frame's edge, the asymmetry — he learned this from photography and from
 Japanese prints, both new and exciting in Paris then. It feels snatched, off-balance, now. And notice the
 medium — pastel, which let him work fast and build up shimmering, broken layers of colour, perfect for
 the gauzy tutus and stage light. Behind the prettiness there's a cool, almost clinical eye: these are
 working dancers, exhausted, mid-effort, observed rather than romanticised. From the stage to the open
 air — Monet and the landscape painters, not far on.
