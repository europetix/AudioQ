 Titian, The Penitent Saint Jerome
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 11, the Venetian rooms — a saint in a dark wilderness.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Titian, The Penitent Saint Jerome, c. 1575 — oil on canvas.
    LISTEN FOR
    How the late Titian almost abandons drawing for pure smouldering light and paint.


 A dark, dramatic canvas — an old, half-naked hermit kneeling among rocks and shadow before a
 crucifix, his body lit against the wild gloom of the wilderness.

 The Penitent Saint Jerome, painted around 1575 by Titian — Tiziano Vecellio — the towering master
 of Venetian painting, in the very last year of his long life. Jerome, the scholar-saint who translated the
 Bible, was often shown doing penance in the desert, beating his breast with a stone before the cross.

 This is late Titian, and it's astonishing. Forget crisp outlines — up close the whole picture dissolves into
 smears, scrapes and dragged strokes of loaded paint, the saint's red robe almost flung onto the canvas,
 the rocks and foliage built from rough, broken touches. Step back and it all resolves into trembling light
 and shadow, flesh and stone and fading day. Titian had spent sixty years learning that paint itself — its
 colour, its very thickness — could carry emotion, and here, near death, he paints with total freedom, the
 surface as alive as the saint's anguish. He died of plague in Venice in 1576, the year after this. Painters
 from Rubens to the Impressionists would study exactly this loose, expressive late manner. The young
 Cretan who trained in this Venetian world is right beside it.
