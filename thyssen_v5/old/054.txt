 Braque, Gris and the Cubist tradition
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Rooms 41–42 — tabletop objects broken into planes.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Georges Braque, Juan Gris and the Cubist tradition.
    LISTEN FOR
    What Cubism became after Picasso — the patient, building work of Braque and Gris.


 Cubist still lifes — guitars, bottles, newspaper, the everyday café tabletop broken into interlocking
 brown and grey planes, sometimes with pasted paper and stencilled letters.

 Work by Georges Braque, Picasso's partner in inventing Cubism, and Juan Gris, the Spaniard who
 became its most lucid third voice.

 If Picasso was the wild genius, Braque was the patient craftsman — quieter, more harmonious, deeply
 in love with the materials of painting. He and Picasso practically merged their identities in the Cubist
 years, and Braque made some of the key breakthroughs: introducing stencilled lettering, imitation
 wood-grain, and then collage — gluing real scraps of newspaper and wallpaper onto the canvas, a move
 that detonated centuries of assumptions about what a picture could be made of. Juan Gris, meanwhile,
 brought a crystalline order and rich colour to Cubism, assembling his objects with the cool precision of
 an architect. Look for the everyday subjects — the café table, the guitar, the bottle — because Cubism,
 for all its difficulty, was rooted in ordinary modern life. From the analytical to the irrational: Dada and
 the dream world of Surrealism, close by.
