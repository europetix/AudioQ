 Otto Dix, Hugo Erfurth with Dog
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 35 — a sharp-edged portrait of a man and his dog.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Otto Dix, Hugo Erfurth with Dog, 1926 — tempera and oil on panel.
    LISTEN FOR
    The cold, surgical precision of New Objectivity, and the photographer who sat for it.


 A coolly precise portrait of a seated man in a dark suit, a large dog beside him, every detail rendered
 with an almost photographic, faintly unsettling sharpness.

 Hugo Erfurth with Dog, painted in 1926 by Otto Dix — a leading figure of the Neue Sachlichkeit, the
 "New Objectivity" that followed Expressionism in 1920s Weimar Germany. The sitter, Hugo Erfurth,
 was a celebrated portrait photographer; Dix paints a fellow image-maker.

 After the heat and distortion of the Expressionists, Dix swings to the opposite extreme: a chilly,
 merciless precision, every wrinkle and hair and thread fixed with the clarity of an old master crossed
 with a police photograph. Dix had been through the horror of the First World War and came back
 determined to show the modern world exactly as it was — sober, sharp, stripped of illusion. Even this
 dignified portrait has an edge of cold scrutiny; the New Objectivity painters trusted appearances
 precisely because they no longer trusted feelings. Dix once recalled the advice that "everything we
 Germans paint is a portrait" — and Weimar Germany sat for him without flattery. Within a few years
 the Nazis would brand his work "degenerate" and hound him from his teaching post. A warmer, more
 mystical German vision hangs a little further on — Franz Marc, through here.
