 Claude Monet, Charing Cross Bridge
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 33 — a London bridge dissolving in fog and light.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Claude Monet, Charing Cross Bridge, 1899–1904 — oil on canvas.
    LISTEN FOR
    How Monet paints not the bridge but the London air itself, thick with smoke and sun.


 A misty, almost dissolving view of a London bridge over the Thames — the structure barely there,
 swallowed in coloured fog, with the faint ghost of a train and the river shimmering beneath.

 Charing Cross Bridge, painted in 1903 by Claude Monet, the purest and most committed of the
 Impressionists. It belongs to a famous series he made of the Thames in London, returning again and
 again to the same bridges to catch the city's celebrated fog at different moments.

 This is Monet's whole philosophy in one canvas. His real subject was never the bridge — it was the light
 and atmosphere wrapped around it, which of course never stop changing. So he worked fast, layering
 small touches of lilac, rose, blue and gold to capture the London mist swallowing the iron and smoke of
 the modern city. Stand close and it's almost pure abstraction, a haze of broken colour with hardly an
 edge to be found; step back and your own eye assembles it into water, fog, and fading light. He began
 these on the spot in London and finished many back home in his garden at Giverny, chasing the memory
 of the haze. It looks effortless and dreamlike — and it changed forever how the world sees light. Two
 women helped invent all this and are too often left out — a few steps on.
