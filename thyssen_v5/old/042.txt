 Paul Cézanne, Seated Man
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 33 — a seated man built from blocks of colour.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Paul Cézanne, Seated Man, 1905–06 — oil on canvas.
    LISTEN FOR
    How Cézanne takes painting apart and rebuilds it from facets of colour — the bridge to everything modern.


 A solid, weighty portrait of a man sitting calmly, built from patient, blocky planes of colour — quiet,
 monumental, faintly unfinished at the edges.

 Seated Man, painted around 1905–06 by Paul Cézanne — one of his very last works, made in the final
 year of his life — by the painter Picasso and Matisse both called "the father of us all," the man who
 turned Impressionism into the doorway to the twentieth century.

 Where Van Gogh poured in emotion, Cézanne went looking for structure. He admired the
 Impressionists' colour but found their work too fleeting — he wanted, he said, "to make of
 Impressionism something solid and durable, like the art of the museums." So he rebuilt the visible world
 out of small, deliberate planes of colour, faceting a face or a body into a calmly geometric architecture,
 often hinting at several viewpoints at once. Look how the man sits with the gravity of a mountain,
 assembled patch by patch, the paint thinning to bare canvas at the margins because Cézanne would
 rather leave a passage unresolved than fake it. It looks calm, even awkward — and it's revolutionary,
 loosening painting from the single fixed viewpoint that had ruled since the Renaissance. A few years
 after his death, two young men named Picasso and Braque stared hard at exactly this and invented
 Cubism. First, the explosion of colour and feeling next door — a little way on.
