Here is a Crucifixion that holds back the pain. Gerard David barely dwells on the wounds in the hands and feet. What he gives you instead is a crowd, and colour.

David was born in Oudewater, in the northern Low Countries, and made his career in Bruges, where he's first recorded in 1484. This panel is thought to be one of his earliest, from around 1475, though its date has been much debated.

Christ hangs just off centre, and divides the picture in two. On the left, the Virgin, Saint John and Mary Magdalene are set along diagonal lines. That grouping looks back to Rogier van der Weyden, the great Flemish painter of the generation before. On the right stand the onlookers. Find the man in front, in bright striped clothes. Scholars see Robert Campin behind him, because Campin loved striped costume.

And he has a twin. He and the man beside him are identical to two figures in a famous illuminated prayer book, the Hours of Catherine of Cleves, made around 1440 and now in New York.

Now the colour. Those sharp, almost acid oranges, greens and yellows are David's signature. His real gift, in the end, was landscape, and it leads on to Patinir, whom you'll meet in Room 10.

One honest note. Jan van Eyck's tiny Annunciation, which usually hangs in this room, is on loan to the Mauritshuis in The Hague until January 2027.

But Van Eyck is still here, in a way. Find the small Virgin and Child by a follower of Dirck Bouts, made around 1465 for private prayer. It's built on Van Eyck's Virgin at the Fountain, with the same embroidered cloth behind Mary and the same low wall of stone blocks. The angels who held up the cloth have gone, and Van Eyck's flower garden has turned to grass. On the Child's leg rests a small apple. It marks him as the Redeemer, and Mary as the new Eve.
