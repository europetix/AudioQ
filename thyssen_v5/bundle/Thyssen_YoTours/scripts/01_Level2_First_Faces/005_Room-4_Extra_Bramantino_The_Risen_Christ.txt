This is Christ after the Resurrection. And he doesn't look as if he has won.

For a long time the picture had the wrong painter and the wrong title. When it was first published, in 1898, it was given to the great architect Bramante. A few years later a scholar suggested his pupil instead, Bartolomeo Suardi, known as Bramantino, and today the museum counts it among Bramantino's masterpieces.

The title changed too. It used to be called an Ecce Homo, Christ shown to the crowd before his death. But there's no crown of thorns here, no reed, no bound hands. Instead, look at the wounds in his hands and his side. This is after the Crucifixion, and the dark shape behind him may be the tomb.

Look at his eyes. They're red, as if from weeping. His body has an almost ghostly pallor, while his face, hair and hands are warmer. And on the left, a small, calm world goes on without him: a river, a tall-masted ship, two tents topped with golden balls.

In this room too is a small Crucifixion by Paolo Uccello. Vasari said Uccello was so absorbed in perspective that he ended up as poor as he was famous.
