In 1844, Franz Liszt gave a concert in this building. A plaque outside still remembers it. Back then this was a palace, the home of the Dukes of Villahermosa, and its main floor housed one of the great cultural institutions of Spanish Romanticism.

The sober façades you walked past were given their present shape in the early 1800s, by the architect Antonio López Aguado. Much later, Rafael Moneo made it a museum. The museum's map calls this long room the Villahermosa Gallery, after the house you are standing in. Today it mixes centuries, so a sixteenth-century Venetian hangs here beside a seventeenth-century painter from Rome.

Find the young woman holding two roses. Paris Bordone trained in Titian's workshop, and she's a type Titian and Palma had made popular: poised and beautiful, in a costly dress cut very low. The roses belong to Venus. She has probably just taken them from that fine vessel decorated with satyrs.

Now follow the thin chain in her left hand. It runs along her arm and round her back, all the way to a small monkey, quietly eating on a ledge. In the language of the time, the monkey stood for vice and lust. And she's the one holding the chain. The museum reads the whole picture in an amorous key. Love, kept under control. Just about.

Before you leave, find Mola's Saint John the Baptist preaching, where the desert has turned green and a crowd sits at the foot of a mountain.

Rooms 7 to 10 open off the long side of this gallery. Start with Room 7: a young knight standing full length in a landscape full of plants and animals.
