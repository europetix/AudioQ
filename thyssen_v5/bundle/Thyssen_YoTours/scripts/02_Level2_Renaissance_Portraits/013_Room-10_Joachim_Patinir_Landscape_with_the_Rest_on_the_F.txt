In 1520, Albrecht Dürer travelled to the Low Countries. In Antwerp he visited the workshop of Joachim Patinir, and called him "a good painter of landscapes". Coming from Dürer, that was a compliment.

The museum calls Patinir the great early forerunner of landscape painting. Only around twenty works are given to him, and just five are signed. If you've been to the Prado, you may already have met him there.

The title says this is the Rest on the Flight into Egypt. The Holy Family is escaping King Herod. But look how small they've become. Mary sits with the Child by a fountain, their luggage around them: a wicker basket, saddle-bags, a rough walking staff. Behind them, Joseph is searching the branches of a tree for something to eat.

Now let your eye travel. Patinir puts you up high, and builds the world in bands, one behind another, broken by tall trees. On the left, hills roll away and a river winds into the distance. On the right, a fortress sits on a rocky hill among the trees. The story is still there. But the real subject is the world itself.

For a very different mood, find Jan de Cock's Temptation of Saint Anthony. The hermit kneels and prays on the left, while on the right the Devil sends a group of women wearing almost nothing but thin veils. Strange hybrid creatures, straight out of Bosch, crowd the landscape. It's meant as a lesson in chastity, though the museum admits it is certainly not devoid of erotic connotations.

And in Jan Mostaert's large Expulsion of Hagar, Abraham sends a mother and her son out into a desert that looks remarkably green.
