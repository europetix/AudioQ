For about forty years, this portrait was published as George Washington's cook, painted by Gilbert Stuart. It isn't by Stuart, and he wasn't Washington's cook.

Welcome to Level 1. The walls turn white here, and the story moves into the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. It begins in America, thanks to Baron Hans Heinrich. As a boy he devoured Karl May's novels of the Wild West, and in 1979 he started buying nineteenth-century American painting, when hardly any European collector did. One of the museum's own writers calls him a rara avis, a rare bird, in European collecting. Today the museum calls this collection an essential point of reference in Europe.

Now, the man in white. He wears an unusual hat that looks a little like a chef's toque. But that kind of toque came decades later, and his coat and neckcloth point to about 1780. So who is he? He may have been a servant in a wealthy British household. He may have been a visitor from Africa. The best clue is the hat. Its closest match is worn by a free West Indian, in a painting of the island of Dominica made a few years earlier. If he was from Dominica, he may have come to England just before the French took the island in 1778.

The painter is unknown too. The broad strokes follow the manner of Sir Joshua Reynolds, and the hand is clearly talented. His catalogue entry admits that he may always remain partly a mystery. So look at him properly. A confident, beautifully painted face, and a name we've lost.

Nearby is John Singleton Copley, Boston's leading portraitist on the eve of the American Revolution. He painted Catherine Hill, a merchant's wife, in pink and pearls, with a direct, calm gaze.
