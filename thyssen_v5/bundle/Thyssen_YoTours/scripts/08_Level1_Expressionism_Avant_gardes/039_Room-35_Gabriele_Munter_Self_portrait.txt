Gabriele Münter painted herself at least sixteen times in oil. This is one of them, from around 1910. And she never let the public see it. She never exhibited it, and it stayed in her studio until her death, more than fifty years later.

At the time, Münter was at the heart of the avant-garde in Munich, and within two years she would help found the group called the Blue Rider. Her portraits of friends from those years are bold and flat, with thick dark outlines. This one is different. The brushwork is rough and textured, light and shadow are strong, and almost everything except the face is only sketched in. Look at the neckline of her smock, pulled off-centre, so that the whole picture tips slightly off balance.

She once wrote that painting a portrait is "the boldest and the most difficult, the most spiritual, the most extreme task of the artist." Here she sets herself that task in private. She is not showing off as a painter. She is looking for her own soul.

Now the two Kirchners in this room. They were among the very first modern paintings the Baron bought, and they arrived together. Doris, in a high ruff collar, was Kirchner's companion in Dresden. Late in life Kirchner gave it a date years too early, which scholars say is impossible.

The other is Fränzi, a girl of about twelve from a working-class district of Dresden. Her face is yellow-green, her lips deep red, and she stares straight at you. Behind her is the mural of Kirchner's studio, nudes on a deep green ground. The chair she leans on is carved in the shape of a woman, most likely by Kirchner himself.
