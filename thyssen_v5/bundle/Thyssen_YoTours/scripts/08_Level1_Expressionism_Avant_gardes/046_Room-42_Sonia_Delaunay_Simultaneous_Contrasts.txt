There is a good chance this painting spent years hanging the wrong way up. Sonia Delaunay signed it as if it were upright. But compared with her other pictures of the same name, it clearly belongs on its side, wider than it is tall.

How does a painter lose track of which way is up? One scholar has a lovely answer. After her marriage to the painter Robert Delaunay, and the birth of their son Charles in 1911, Sonia stopped painting for more than two years. Instead she designed bedspreads, pillows and tablecloths. And a bedspread has no top or bottom. When she came back to the canvas, she kept that freedom.

So try it. Tilt your head and turn the picture upright in your mind. It still works. There is nothing in it to fall over. Just curved bands and segments of colour pushing against each other, red against blue, warm against cold. That clash is the subject. For the Delaunays, colour itself could move, and that movement could build a new kind of space.

Now find Robert's Woman with a Parasol, in the same room. Early in 1913 he took a picture with this title to Berlin, to the Der Sturm gallery, with the poet Guillaume Apollinaire. Apollinaire gave Robert's colour painting a name, Orphism. "Line is limitation," Robert wrote. Look at the woman's dress, broken into coloured patterns, very close to the designs Sonia was making to dress modern women. Both paintings came to the museum from the same collection, husband and wife side by side.

One more story in this room. Balla's crowd of Italian flags caught the eye of the choreographer Léonide Massine, who bought it straight from the painter in 1917 for a Stravinsky ballet in Rome.
