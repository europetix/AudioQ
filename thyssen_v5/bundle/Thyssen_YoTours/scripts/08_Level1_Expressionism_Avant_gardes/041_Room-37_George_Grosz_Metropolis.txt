In 1916, in the middle of a war against Britain and France, a young Berlin painter called Georg Gross decided to sound American. He added an E to his first name, so that Georg became the English George. And Gross, spelled with a double S, became Grosz, with an S and a Z.

He started this picture that December, in wartime Berlin, and finished it the following summer. The army called him up again halfway through, and for months the canvas waited. Look at what he came back to. A city street crammed with people, figures and buildings overlapping, everything soaked in red, and over it all a blazing, unreal sun. Grosz borrowed the broken planes of Cubism and the speed of Futurism, but not their optimism. This is a city heading for self-destruction. The writer Walter Mehring gave it a second title, Reminiscences of the Entrance to Manhattan, because many of Grosz's drawings for it imagined New York.

Then the painting's own story begins. In the mid-1920s a German museum in Mannheim bought it. Under the Nazis it was hung in their exhibition of so-called degenerate art and then sold off through a gallery in Lucerne, in Switzerland. A German dealer who had emigrated to New York bought it, and in the end Grosz bought his own painting back. Only later did it come to this collection.

Now find a much smaller, brighter picture in this room: Kandinsky's Murnau, a village street in Bavaria from 1908. On the right is the inn where he and Gabriele Münter always stayed. When the First World War sent Kandinsky back to Russia, Münter kept this painting. It is Kandinsky before abstraction, still looking at houses and roofs.
