In the world of Balthus, the girls always win. The boys can try anything, but they usually lose.

Here a boy and a girl sit at a table with a candlestick, playing cards in a bare, stark room. Light comes in coldly from the right. Look at the boy, his body twisted so that you see him partly from the front and partly in profile. He is ready to cheat. And look at the girl's half-hidden smile. It suggests she will win in the end.

Balthus painted it just after the Second World War, when he came back to painting with new energy. He kept away from every modern movement and looked back to the old masters, above all to Piero della Francesca, in the austerity, the geometry and the colour. X-rays show that he changed a good deal as he worked, and even painted out a hat.

Now find Max Beckmann's portrait of his wife, Quappi, in a bright pink jumper and matching hat, a cigarette in her hand. He almost finished it in Frankfurt in 1932. Two years later, the Nazis had driven him from his teaching post. He took it up again in Berlin, changed the date and, according to a friend, made her smile a little less.
