Baron Hans Heinrich Thyssen-Bornemisza spent his life choosing portraits, buying them and living with them. Until 1981, he had never sat for one.

That July he went to Lucian Freud's studio in London, near Notting Hill Gate, for the first sitting. Freud worked slowly, and the sessions were long, almost endless. The Baron kept coming back, punctually, for nearly two years. The portrait was finished in October 1982.

Freud made no preparatory drawings. He started straight on the canvas with a charcoal sketch. Then he built the face up in layer after layer of paint, some of it laid on with a palette knife. He said of his sitters, "I try to enter as far as possible into their feelings, so the picture will be about them, not me."

Now look at what he saw. The canvas is small, and he placed the Baron very close. A checked jacket, a white shirt, a dark tie. The head tips forward and the eyes look down, away from you. It's not the face of a man showing off his treasures. It's gloomier than that, more private.

And then look behind him, above his shoulder. Freud has barely sketched in a fragment of another painting. It's Watteau's Pierrot Content, which the Baron had owned since 1977, and which Freud kept pinned up on his studio wall as a reproduction. The Baron sits exactly where Pierrot sits in the Watteau, and seems to borrow his pose and his expression. You saw that Pierrot upstairs, in Room 24.

Nearby, Freud turns the scrutiny on himself. In Reflection with Two Children he looms over a mirror at his feet, and his children Rose and Ali appear tiny in the lower corner.

A collector who spent his life choosing what to look at, sitting still at last, and being looked at, in the pose of a painting he loved.
