In Ovid's poem, the god Apollo kills the boy he loves with a discus. Tiepolo has swapped the discus for a tennis ball.

He had a reason. An Italian translation of Ovid from 1561 had already turned the discus into a ball, and the court game was still the height of fashion among the nobility. Tiepolo painted this huge canvas around 1752, during his years in Würzburg, in Germany.

Look down at the floor. There's the racquet with its balls, and a third ball has rolled away to the left. Behind the onlookers you can see part of the net. Hyacinthus lies with a bruise on his cheek, looking up at Apollo. And the stone god Pan seems to have turned his head, smiling, so as not to miss a thing. In the right-hand corner, the flowers are already growing.

Walk on through Room 18 and look for Piazzetta's portrait of Giulia Lama, a Venetian painter in her own right, palette in hand. Then go straight ahead to Room 22, which has a short Extra on Crespi. The next main stop is Room 23. Until 10 January 2027, the Classical rooms 19 to 21, along the corridor to your left, hold a visiting exhibition from the Mauritshuis, which is not part of this guide.
