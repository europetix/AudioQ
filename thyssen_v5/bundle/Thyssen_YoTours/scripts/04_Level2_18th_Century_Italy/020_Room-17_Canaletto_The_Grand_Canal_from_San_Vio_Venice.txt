You can date this pair of paintings by the pavement.

Start with the view of the Piazza San Marco. Canaletto painted it from high up, where a church once stood at the far end of the square. In 1723, Venice began relaying the square to a new design. Look at the ground. The paving near the arcades is finished, but the middle is still bare. So Canaletto was painting at the very start of the work, around 1723 or 1724.

Now the Grand Canal from San Vio, one of the earliest views by him that we know. On the right, on the Palazzo Barbarigo, a woman leans out of a window and a man is sweeping a chimney. The line of palaces leads your eye down the water to the white dome of the Salute. The boats in the foreground sit in shadow and pull you in. Above it all, greenish water meets an enormous blue sky.

These two belonged to a series owned by the Princes of Liechtenstein in Vienna. Two more from the same series are back in Venice today, in the Ca' Rezzonico. Baron Thyssen bought this pair two years apart, in the 1950s.

Then step across to the two small Guardis, painted about half a century later. Francesco Guardi was Tiepolo's brother-in-law, and he started out imitating Canaletto. By around 1780 he had his own way of seeing. The land shrinks. Water and sky almost melt into each other, and the boats are just flicks of the brush.

Look for the church of Santa Lucia on the bank. If you've ever arrived in Venice by train, you've stood on that spot. The church was pulled down in the nineteenth century to make way for the railway station.

Canaletto shows you Venice as it was measured. Guardi shows you Venice as it felt.
