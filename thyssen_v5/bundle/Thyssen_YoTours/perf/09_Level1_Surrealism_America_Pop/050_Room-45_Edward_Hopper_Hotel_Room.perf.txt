[quietly] Edward Hopper once said, "What I wanted to do was paint sunlight on the side of a house." There is no sunlight here. It is night, the light is electric, and a woman sits alone on the edge of a hotel bed.

She is tired. She has taken off her hat, her dress and her shoes, and she is too exhausted to unpack. In her hands is a yellowed sheet of paper. We know exactly what it is, because Hopper's wife, Jo, kept careful notes on his paintings. It is a train timetable. She is checking the time of tomorrow's train. Jo knew the scene well. She posed for it herself, in their studio on Washington Square.

Hopper painted it in 1931, on a big canvas, just after his first real success. It was the first of many hotel rooms in his work. Now look at how he closes her in. A wall cuts across the front. A chest of drawers blocks the right. The long diagonal of the bed pulls your eye to a half-open window at the back, and outside it there is nothing but black night. [pause] And that open window turns you into the intruder, looking in on a stranger's private moment.

[wry] Two other portraits in this room keep their secrets too. Otto Dix painted his friend Hugo Erfurth, a famous society photographer, in a tweed jacket with his big dog, but without a single camera. Dix remembered the painter Max Liebermann's advice: "everything we Germans paint is a portrait."

And Picasso's Harlequin holds up a mirror and gazes into it. X-rays show that the face began as Picasso's own. He turned it into a mask, the pale face of Pierrot, the same sad clown Watteau painted, upstairs in Room 24.
