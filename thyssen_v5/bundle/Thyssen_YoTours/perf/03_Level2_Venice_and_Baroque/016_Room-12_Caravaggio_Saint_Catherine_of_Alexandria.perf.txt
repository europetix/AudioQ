[curious] The saint in this painting was a celebrated courtesan. Her name was Fillide Melandroni, and in Rome around 1598 she posed for Caravaggio as a princess and martyr.

This is the seventeenth century arriving. Caravaggio painted real people from life and set them against deep darkness, picked out by strong light. Painters all over Europe followed him.

The picture was almost certainly made for Cardinal Francesco Maria del Monte, Caravaggio's first patron. After the cardinal's heir ran into debt, it was sold, and it passed to the Barberini family. It came to this collection in 1934.

[pause] Catherine of Alexandria was condemned to be broken on a spiked wheel. Here is the wheel, cracked and splintered. Here is the sword that took her life instead, and the martyr's palm. She kneels on a costly red damask cushion, in a rich dress of blues and violets, as befits a princess.

Now look at where the light comes from. It falls from the right. That's unusual for Caravaggio, and it has led to the idea that the painting was made for a particular place, with real light coming from that side.

[warmly] And look at her face. Your eye follows the diagonals, the wheel, the sword, the turn of her body, and arrives at her gaze. A real woman of Caravaggio's Rome, looking back at you with complete composure.

Before you leave, find the wide Pietà by Jusepe de Ribera, the Spaniard who made his career in Naples. He signed it on the stone at the lower right, Jusepe de Ribera, español, with the date 1633. Here the darkness is starting to give way to colour.

Make your way to Room 13, at the end of the long gallery, with Claude's pastoral landscape and Vouet's Europa riding the white bull. Then turn into Room 14: a huge canvas where a baby is held up by one ankle.
