[warmly] In Florence, painters started with drawing. In Venice, they started with colour and light, and let the paint itself carry the feeling. This room is the Venetian sixteenth century, and its title names its heroes.

And one outsider. Before he became El Greco of Toledo, Domenikos Theotokopoulos was a painter from Crete working in Italy, soaking up everything Venice could teach him. This Annunciation, from around 1576, is one of the last versions he painted in Italy, before he left for Spain.

[amused] Look at how many teachers are in it. The elegant angel is very much in the style of Veronese. The light and colour owe a great deal to Titian. And the way the two figures are placed comes from a composition by Tintoretto in Venice. Mary sits at her prayer desk on the left, listening intently. A chequered floor runs back, with a pink curtain and a balustrade behind.

Now look up. In the cloud of light, some small angels are seen sharply from below. That's a glimpse of the painter he's about to become in Spain.

The museum owns a second, much later Annunciation by El Greco, painted in Spain. That one is on loan to The Hague until January 2027.

Before you leave, find Tintoretto's pair of large canvases, a woman on the left in each, open landscape between. In one, the widow Tamar tricks her father-in-law Judah, who finally has to admit, "She hath been more righteous than I."

Room 11 has a short Extra on Titian's late Penitent Saint Jerome. The next main stop is Room 12: Caravaggio's Saint Catherine beside her spiked wheel.
