[curious] Seventeen years separate Kandinsky's little Murnau street, back in Room 37, from this painting. Same artist. And not a single house left.

By 1925, Wassily Kandinsky was teaching at the Bauhaus, the German school of art and design, which had just moved from Weimar to the town of Dessau. That year he finished a book with a wonderfully dry title, Point and Line to Plane. In it he treated form and colour as things in their own right, with no need to describe anything. He even taught his students to match the three primary colours to three shapes: yellow to the triangle, red to the square, blue to the circle.

Now look at the painting: the bright oval of the title, and around it shapes, lines and points of colour. It could almost be a demonstration from the classroom. [pause] But the museum's text warns against reading it that way. This, it argues, is not an experiment with geometry. Like his earlier work, it carries a hidden cosmic meaning, a small universe of its own.

[reverent] Now turn to the huge, almost square canvas in this room, by the Czech painter František Kupka. It is two metres high. Kupka, like Kandinsky, believed painting could work like music, with no subject at all. He worked on two versions of this composition through the winter of 1912, and both were shown in Paris the following autumn, at the Salon d'Automne of 1913. Who painted the very first abstract picture is still argued about, and Kupka is often named among the pioneers. Stand back and let it turn.
