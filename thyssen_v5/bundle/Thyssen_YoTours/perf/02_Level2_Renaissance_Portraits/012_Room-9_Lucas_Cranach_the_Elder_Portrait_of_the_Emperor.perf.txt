[wry] Here is the Catholic emperor who ruled Spain, painted by the great painter of the Reformation. History has a sense of humour.

You've just walked through Room 8 into the German rooms. Lucas Cranach, whose Virgin with a bunch of grapes you passed next door, was court painter at Wittenberg and a personal friend of Martin Luther. And here he paints Charles V, the emperor who fought Luther's princes, in 1533.

They had met. Cranach first painted Charles as a boy of eight, a portrait now lost. Years later, after Charles defeated the Protestant princes at Mühlberg, the old painter went to plead with him for his own captured prince. One theory even says this panel belonged to a set of royal portraits that the reformers gave the emperor as a present. It's only a theory, but a tempting one.

[pause] Now look at the face. Titian's official portraits of Charles soften it. Cranach doesn't. The long face, the jutting jaw, the big nose and the full lower lip are all there. And there's almost nothing to tell you he's an emperor. Just one gold chain, made of links shaped like flames and flints, with the little golden fleece of his chivalric order hanging from it.

[amused] Two women in this room have been given the wrong names. Holbein the Elder's tiny profile, by the father of the Henry the Eighth painter, was once identified with its pair as Luther and his wife. That's now rejected. And Altdorfer's young woman was said to be Barbara Blomberg, later the mother of Charles's son, Don John of Austria. The trouble is, Barbara wasn't born until 1527.

One note for this season: Dürer's Jesus Among the Doctors, usually in these rooms, is on loan to The Hague until January 2027.
