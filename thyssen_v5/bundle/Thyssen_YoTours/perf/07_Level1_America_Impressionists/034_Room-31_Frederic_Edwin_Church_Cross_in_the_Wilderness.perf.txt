[quietly] A family had lost a child. They asked Frederic Church for a painting in memory, and in 1857 he gave them this.

There's no portrait and no angel. In the foreground stands a simple cross, hung with garlands. To its right, a waterfall drops into a still lake beneath a great wall of rock. The far peaks have almost dissolved, and over everything spreads a vast evening sky.

Church had travelled through Ecuador and Colombia, and he was about to go back. In the Andes, travellers described places along the road where the dead were laid down, each one marked with a simple cross of twigs. A scholar has linked this cross to those places. [pause] Church came to know that grief himself. Eight years later, two of his own young children died of diphtheria, and he painted two small pictures on this same theme.

He had learned his art from Thomas Cole, the painter usually called the founder of the Hudson River School, America's first real fellowship of artists. Cole's Expulsion, here in the same room, is a stage set. A stone bridge over an abyss, a waterfall, a volcano, the moon. It shows Adam and Eve being driven out of Paradise, yet neither of them appears. Eden simply glows through the arch on the right.

[warmly] Cole worried about what rapid industry was doing to America's wild places. He wrote: "We are still in Eden. The wall that shuts us out of the garden is our own ignorance and folly." That's why this room is called American Landscape and Environmental Awareness. The wilderness here is beautiful, and it's also something that can be lost.
