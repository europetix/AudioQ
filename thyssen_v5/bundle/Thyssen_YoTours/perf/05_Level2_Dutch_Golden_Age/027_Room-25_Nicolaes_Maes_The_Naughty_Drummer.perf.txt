[amused] Every parent knows this moment. The baby has finally gone to sleep, and then somebody starts banging a drum.

Nicolaes Maes painted it around 1655, soon after leaving Rembrandt's studio in Amsterdam. A mother sits by the window with her sewing on her lap. Her little boy is drumming, the baby lies asleep in its wicker cradle, and she raises a small rod at him. Light falls in from the left, warm on her reds and on the rug over the table.

[curious] Now look for the mirror. There's a man in it. In 1819 a visitor described this as The Artist's Family, with Maes himself in the mirror, his wife, her son from an earlier marriage, and their baby daughter. Much later, one scholar read it as an allegory of three kinds of life. The thoughtful man in the mirror, the active mother, the pleasure-seeking boy. Another saw a political message about Holland at the time. The museum says these readings are not mutually exclusive.

[pause] It's also, quite simply, a lesson in bringing up children, which Dutch moralists of the day counted among a mother's duties.

Beside it, Willem Kalf's still life of 1662 is the Dutch Golden Age on one tabletop. A late Ming bowl from China, a carpet from Herat, and a nautilus shell set in silver gilt. Look at the very top of the cup. A sea monster is about to swallow a tiny figure, either Jonah or Ulysses.

[lightly] And Jan Steen, the painter people compare to Molière, has painted himself with a lute, in an actor's old-fashioned costume, with a satisfied air and an ironic look. He's inviting you to the party.
