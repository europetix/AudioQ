[curious] Fragonard's most famous painting shows a young woman flying high on a swing. It's in London. This canvas is one of the first steps towards it.

He painted The See-Saw around 1750, when he was still a pupil in François Boucher's studio and had not yet been to Italy. Four children play in a wood. A girl swings up through the air, and the whole picture tilts along the diagonal of her movement. Leaves and branches spill across the canvas. The brush moves as fast as the game.

[conspiratorial] Here's the thread. This canvas and its pair, a game of blind man's buff now in Toledo, Ohio, belonged to a Paris collector, the Baron de Saint-Julien. The famous Swing in the Wallace Collection, painted some fifteen years later, belonged to him too. The pair was split up in America, and Baron Thyssen bought this half in the 1950s.

Now look along this long gallery. It's the French and English eighteenth century, running between the Dutch rooms, so you'll cross it more than once.

Boucher, Fragonard's teacher, painted La Toilette for the Swedish ambassador in Paris. A young woman ties her garter while her maid holds up a cap. There's a gold Chinese screen, and a cat plays with a loose thread. The museum calls it an invaluable document of how French rooms really looked.

[quietly] And Gainsborough's Sarah Buxton sits calmly in a garden, in a pale blue gown, painted just before her marriage. What you see is a fragment. The original canvas was cut up after a fire, and another piece shows a dog at her feet. Gainsborough earned his living with portraits, but landscape was his real vocation, and you can feel it in those trees.
