[warmly] At the front of this table lie three halved apricots, and one of them still holds its stone. It's the kind of detail that made Louise Moillon one of the finest still-life painters in seventeenth-century France.

This picture, from around 1637, is older than its neighbours in this eighteenth-century gallery by the best part of a century. But she deserves a stop of her own.

Moillon was born in Paris in 1610, into a Protestant family of painters and picture dealers. Her father ran a shop selling paintings in Saint-Germain-des-Prés, and after his death her stepfather, also a painter and dealer, went on training her.

[pause] Now read the table from left to right. A Chinese bowl heaped with strawberries, with a loose stalk and some redcurrants beside it. Behind, three artichokes and two bunches of asparagus. On the right, a dish propped up on two rough bricks holds a basket of apricots, strawberries and cherries. Above everything arches a canopy of leaves, and she has painted both sides of them, the glossy front and the paler back. The light comes from the left and lands on the apricots.

Her earliest pictures are much simpler. The table is seen from above, every fruit sits apart, and the light is flat. Here things overlap naturally, the colour is richer, and the light is focused. The museum's catalogue calls this painting a key step in that change. [wry] It's also honest enough to say that her later dated works decline. Which makes this one, from her best years, all the more precious.

People love to find sermons in still lifes. A fly for corruption, strawberries for the Resurrection. For this picture, the catalogue suggests there is probably no hidden message at all. Just spring, set out on a table by a painter who knew exactly what she was doing.
