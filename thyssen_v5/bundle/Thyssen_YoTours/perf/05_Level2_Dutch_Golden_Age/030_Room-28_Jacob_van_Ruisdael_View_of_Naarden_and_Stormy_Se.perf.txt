[warmly] Jacob van Ruisdael was eighteen, perhaps nineteen, when he signed and dated this little panel in 1647. And he was painting home ground. His grandfather had moved to Naarden, and his father and uncle were born there.

Look at the proportions. The panel is small, but the view is panoramic. He drops the horizon low, so the sky takes more than half the picture, in bands of cloud and blue. Light breaks through one cloud and runs across the fields in stripes of sun and shadow. Naarden sits in the middle, the Zuiderzee opens on the right, and far off stand the ruins of a church at Muiderberg.

[pause] Now find the sea painting, made about twenty years later. He has pushed the idea as far as it will go. The sky fills about three quarters of the canvas, and the sea is a narrow strip. Wind fills the slanting sails. A great S-shaped shadow of cloud falls across the biggest ship, and on the right, white foam breaks around a buoy.

[curious] This canvas was in London from 1817, and it was sold there twice. One scholar noticed how closely it resembles Turner's Port Ruysdael, a picture whose very name salutes the Dutchman. Turner might have seen it. Nobody can prove that he did.

The third Ruisdael here is quieter, from the early 1660s. A road winds through ripe wheat, with cattle and walkers, towards a village in the trees. It's thought to be near Naarden again, with the Zuiderzee in the distance.

[quietly] From the family town to the open water, the real subject never changed. It was always the sky.
