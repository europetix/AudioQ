 How to use this guide
 This is a walk-through audio guide to the Museo Nacional Thyssen-Bornemisza, the third corner of
 Madrid’s Golden Triangle of Art — a privately built, encyclopaedic survey of seven centuries of
 Western painting. It follows the museum’s own top-down route: you start at the top, on Level 2 in the
 thirteenth century, and walk downward through time — Level 2 (Old Masters), Level 1 (the 19th and
 20th centuries, America to Pop), and Level 0 (the Carmen Thyssen Collection). The guide is built
 around two kinds of tracks. Knowing the difference will let you move at your own pace.


 Room introductions
 Most tracks are short room introductions. Play one as you walk into a new room: it tells you what's
 there, what to look at first, and which works have their own dedicated track. In the header these are
 marked R-INTRO. They usually run between one and a half and two and a half minutes.

 Single-work tracks
 The rest focus on a single painting or sculpture. Play one when you are standing in front of the work it
 describes; in the header these are marked W-FOCUS. They usually run between two and three minutes.

 Numbers and room signs
 Every track carries a number — 001, 002, and so on — in the order you walk the tour, and the audio
 files are numbered to match, so you can simply play them in sequence. The room numbers printed at the
 top of each track come straight from the museum’s own floor plan and match the gallery signs. They are
 stable — the Thyssen is not mid-rehang — but individual works do travel (see below).

 Pauses and movement
 Each track ends with a short silent pause built into the audio. That's your moment to look, photograph if
 you want to, and walk to the next stop. When you reach a new room, play the next track. When you're
 standing in front of a marquee work, play its dedicated track. The audio always tells you what to play
 next at the end of each track. You don't have to remember anything.

 On honesty and change
 The Thyssen lends generously to exhibitions worldwide, so on any given day a particular painting may
 be off the wall, with a notice in its place — the museum says as much on its own plan. The room layout
 itself is stable, but if a work you came for is travelling, that is why. Check the live notices at
 museothyssen.org before you go. A handful of works named here also rotate with the Carmen Thyssen
 deposit.

 The visit, in shape
 T00 at the entrance → up to Level 2 for the Early Renaissance, German and Venetian painting and the
 Baroque, the Dutch Golden Age, and the 18th century to Goya → down to Level 1 for 19th-century
 America and the Impressionists, Expressionism and the avant-gardes, and America’s rise from
 abstraction to Pop → down to Level 0 for the Carmen Thyssen Collection → T-LAST on the way out. It
 runs around two and a half hours at an unhurried pace.

 Welcome to the Thyssen-Bornemisza
 Welcome. Take a breath — you've arrived at the third corner of Madrid's Golden Triangle of Art, and in
 some ways the most surprising one.

 You may have just come from the Prado, with its Spanish kings and Velázquez and Goya, or from the
 Reina Sofía, with Picasso's Guernica and the wild twentieth century. This museum is the bridge
 between them — and it fills in everything the other two leave out. Early Italian gold-ground panels. The
 Northern Renaissance. German painting. The Dutch Golden Age. The Grand Tour. Nineteenth-century
 America. German Expressionism. Russian abstraction. Pop. Where the Prado is Spanish and royal, and
 the Reina Sofía is Spanish and modern, the Thyssen is the whole story of Western painting, seven
 centuries of it, under one roof.

 And here's the remarkable part: almost none of this belonged to Spain until recently. This is a private
 collection — assembled across two generations by one family, the Thyssen-Bornemiszas,
 German-Hungarian industrialists, who set out quite deliberately to own one outstanding example of
 every chapter in the history of painting. Spain bought the whole thing in 1993, and it has been
 completing the triangle ever since.

 A word on how this tour works, because it shapes everything. The museum is built to be walked from
 the top down. You'll start on Level 2, in the thirteenth century, and as you descend through the building
 you'll move forward through time — down a floor, forward a few hundred years — until you step out
 the bottom into the present. It's one of the most satisfying walks in any museum in the world: the entire
 history of Western art, taken as a single gentle downhill stroll.

 So — to the top. Let's begin in the age of gold leaf and saints.

 The age of gold and the birth of the face
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Ground floor, Rooms 1–5 — the start of the tour and the oldest works.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Late-medieval and early-Renaissance panels: Duccio, Van Eyck, Memling, Antonello.
    LISTEN FOR
    How painting climbs out of flat gold and learns to build space, faces and the individual.


 You're at the top of the building and the top of the story — the earliest rooms, where Western painting is
 just teaching itself to walk. Take these first galleries slowly; they cover nearly two hundred years, from
 the early 1300s to about 1500, and you can almost watch art change its mind as you go.

 It begins with gold. The first panels glow with beaten gold-leaf backgrounds, because their world wasn't
 the everyday one — it was the eternal, heavenly space of saints and scripture, made for altars and
 prayer. Then, room by room, something extraordinary happens: the gold drains away and is replaced by
 sky, landscape, a real window, a real face. Artists in Italy and in the north — Flanders especially — start
 looking hard at the actual world, and at actual people.

 Watch for that shift as you move not far on. The single biggest invention in these rooms isn't a technique
 — it's the individual: the portrait of one specific human being, looking out at you across five centuries.
 The very first of them are a few steps on.

 TRACK 003 · 1                                     W-FOCUS                             222 WORDS · ~2 MIN



 Duccio, Christ and the Samaritan Woman
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 1, the gold-ground rooms — a small panel glowing with gold.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Duccio, Christ and the Samaritan Woman, 1310–11 — tempera and gold on panel.
    LISTEN FOR
    How a Sienese master cracked open the flat gold world and let real space — and a real conversation — begin.


 One of the oldest paintings in the museum — a small, jewel-like panel against a flat gold ground, with
 slender figures at a well outside a walled pink-and-cream town.

 Christ and the Samaritan Woman, painted around 1310 by Duccio di Buoninsegna, the founding master
 of the Sienese school. It's a small scene from the back of his greatest work, the Maestà, the colossal
 double-sided altarpiece made for Siena's cathedral — when that altarpiece was later broken up, predella
 panels like this one scattered into collections around the world.

 Look at what Duccio is reaching for. The background is still medieval gold, the holy non-space of an
 icon — but the town behind the figures is a real architectural place, with arches and a gate, and the
 figures themselves have weight and gesture and a quiet human exchange passing between them. Christ
 meets a woman at a well and asks her for water; she's surprised he'd even speak to her. It's one of the
 first moments in European painting where you feel a genuine conversation happening between two
 people. Duccio is standing right on the hinge between the icon and the picture — one foot in heaven's
 gold, one foot in the world. Everything else on these floors grows from this moment. The northern
 painters took the next step, close by.

 Jan van Eyck, The Annunciation Diptych
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 3 — a tiny grisaille diptych painted to look like carved stone.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Jan van Eyck, The Annunciation Diptych, c. 1433–35 — oil on panel.
    LISTEN FOR
    How Van Eyck fakes sculpture in paint so well your eye refuses to believe it's flat.


 Two narrow panels, side by side, that look at first like carved stone statues in shadowy niches — until
 you realise they're paintings, pretending.

 A diptych of The Annunciation by Jan van Eyck, the Flemish genius who, around 1433–35, did things
 with oil paint nobody had done before. The angel Gabriel on one side, the Virgin Mary on the other —
 but painted entirely in cool grey, grisaille, to imitate sculpture, complete with the illusion of stone
 niches, cast shadows, and even reflections in the polished black bases.

 This is a magic trick four hundred years ahead of its time. Van Eyck is showing off — painting is so
 powerful, he's saying, that it can perfectly counterfeit a rival art form, sculpture, and then go further,
 adding reflections and light that real stone could never hold. Get close and look at the bases: the figures
 seem to be reflected in the dark marble beneath them. Van Eyck was among the first to truly master oil
 paint — building up colour and light in thin, glowing layers — and that technical revolution, born in
 Flanders, would change everything. He makes you doubt your own eyes. That's the point. More northern
 marvels are just here.

 TRACK 005 · 3                                      W-FOCUS                              214 WORDS · ~2 MIN



 Hans Memling, Portrait of a Young Man Praying
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 3 — a young man with hands pressed together in prayer.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Hans Memling, Portrait of a Young Man Praying, c. 1485 — oil on panel.
    LISTEN FOR
    Why this half of a lost diptych once faced a Madonna — and what the sitter is really looking at.


 A small, intensely still panel — a young man in three-quarter view, his hands pressed together in prayer,
 set against a calm landscape that opens out behind him.

 Portrait of a Young Man Praying, painted around 1485 by Hans Memling, the most sought-after
 portraitist of late-fifteenth-century Bruges. The praying pose is a clue: this panel was once part of a
 larger devotional ensemble — a triptych or diptych — the young man's gaze and folded hands directed
 toward a now-separated image of the Virgin and Child.

 Memling all but invented the modern portrait formula that would rule for centuries — the sitter turned
 slightly, set not against flat gold but against a real sky and landscape that gives him weight and presence
 in the world. The face is calm, refined, a little melancholy; the sitter, never identified, was probably an
 Italian merchant living in Bruges, where Memling worked for the city's wealthy foreign community.
 And here's the hidden marvel: on the reverse of this very panel Memling painted a small vase of flowers
 — one of the earliest independent still lifes in all of European art, its irises and lilies quietly standing in
 for the purity of the Virgin. Total attention, front and back. Another tiny northern jewel is a little way
 on.

 TRACK 006 · 3                                      W-FOCUS                               198 WORDS · ~2 MIN



 Petrus Christus, Our Lady of the Dry Tree
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 3 — a miniature Madonna in a dead, thorny tree hung with letters.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Petrus Christus, Our Lady of the Dry Tree, c. 1465 — oil on panel.
    LISTEN FOR
    The strange medieval symbol behind this tiny picture, and the gold letters in the branches.


 A very small panel — easy to walk past — of the Virgin and Child standing inside the branches of a
 bare, thorny, leafless tree, with tiny golden letters hanging from the twigs.

 Our Lady of the Dry Tree, painted around 1465 by Petrus Christus, who took over as Bruges's leading
 painter after van Eyck. Those fifteen little golden objects dangling in the branches are the letter "A" —
 for Ave, the opening of the prayer Ave Maria — so the whole image is a visual rosary, a prayer you look
 at rather than say.

 The dead, thorny tree is the key. It comes from a vision in the Old Testament and stood for fallen, barren
 humanity — the world after Eden, dry and cursed. And in the middle of it, blooming where nothing
 should grow, is Mary with Christ: new life, redemption, the dead wood made to flower. It's a complete
 theology compressed into something the size of a paperback, made for private devotion in someone's
 hands. Northern painters loved this — enormous meaning packed into tiny, perfect objects. Now Italy
 answers, with two of the first true portraits in Western art, just along here.

 TRACK 007 · 5                                        W-FOCUS                               203 WORDS · ~2 MIN



 Vittore Carpaccio, Young Knight in a Landscape
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 5 — a young knight in armour in a teeming landscape.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Vittore Carpaccio, Young Knight in a Landscape, 1505 — oil on canvas.
    LISTEN FOR
    One of the first full-length portraits in Western art, and the puzzle of who he is.


 A striking full-length figure — a young man in gleaming armour, standing in a crowded landscape
 teeming with plants, birds and animals.

 Young Knight in a Landscape, painted around 1505 by the Venetian Vittore Carpaccio. It's one of the
 earliest full-length portraits in all of Western art — at a time when portraits were almost always just the
 head and shoulders, Carpaccio dared to paint a whole standing man, life-size, head to armoured foot.

 And he filled the world around the knight with meaning. Look closely and the landscape is alive with
 symbols — an ermine (a creature said to die rather than dirty itself, an emblem of purity), birds, flowers,
 a distant town, even a scroll with a Latin motto that translates as something like "better death than
 dishonour." Scholars still argue over exactly who the young man is, but the message is unmistakable:
 this is a portrait of virtue, of chivalric honour made visible. Carpaccio gives you a person and, at the
 same time, an entire moral world for them to stand in. After this, full-length portraiture was on the table
 for everyone. And right beside it hangs one of the sharpest faces in the building — just here.

 Antonello da Messina, Portrait of a Man
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 4 — a man looking straight out at you, close and alive.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Antonello da Messina, Portrait of a Man, c. 1475 — oil on panel.
    LISTEN FOR
    How a Sicilian carried the new oil technique south and made a face feel utterly present.


 A small head-and-shoulders portrait of a man against a dark ground, turned slightly toward you, with
 eyes so alert he seems about to speak.

 A Portrait of a Man by Antonello da Messina, the Sicilian painter who did something historically huge:
 he carried the northern, Flemish mastery of oil paint south into Italy. Trained in the orbit of painters who
 knew the van Eyck upheaval, Antonello fused that jewel-like northern precision with the solid,
 sculptural weight of Italian form.

 The result is a face that feels uncannily present. Where Italian portraits had often shown the sitter in flat
 profile, like a head on a coin, Antonello turns the man to a three-quarter view and lights him from the
 side, so he occupies real space and meets your eye. Every detail — the stubble, the slight asymmetry,
 the wary intelligence in the gaze — is observed, not idealised. When Antonello brought this technique
 to Venice in the 1470s, it electrified painters there; Giovanni Bellini and the whole Venetian school took
 note. You're looking at a hinge point in the history of the portrait. And now the most famous face in the
 museum, the woman on the cover, is just ahead.

    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 5 — a Florentine woman in strict profile, richly dressed.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Domenico Ghirlandaio, Portrait of Giovanna Tornabuoni, 1489–90 — tempera on panel.
    LISTEN FOR
    Why she faces sideways, the Latin verse behind her, and the young wife who died before it was done.


 The face you've seen on every poster and ticket — a young woman in strict profile, golden hair coiled
 and bound, in a gorgeous brocade and red gown, against a dark recess holding a few precise objects.

 Portrait of Giovanna degli Albizzi Tornabuoni, painted 1489–90 by Domenico Ghirlandaio, the leading
 Florentine workshop master of his day — and, not incidentally, the man in whose workshop a teenage
 Michelangelo trained. Giovanna was a young Florentine noblewoman, married into the powerful
 Tornabuoni family.

 And here is the heartbreak inside the beauty. By the time this portrait was finished, Giovanna was
 almost certainly already dead — she died in 1488, at around twenty, in childbirth. So this isn't a portrait
 of a living sitter; it's an act of memory and love, a husband's attempt to hold on to a face. Look at the
 Latin inscription on the cartellino behind her: it's a line adapted from the Roman poet Martial that says,
 in effect, "Art, if only you could also depict her character and soul, there would be no more beautiful
 painting on earth." Ghirlandaio is admitting the limit of his own art — he can give you the perfect
 surface of her, the hair and the jewels and the proud profile, but not the person who is gone. The objects
 in the niche — a prayer book, a brooch, a string of coral — speak of piety, virtue and protection. It's the
 museum's signature image, and once you know the story, that flawless profile becomes one of the most
 calmly moving things in the building. Downstairs lies Germany, and a very different intensity — this
 way to the next rooms.

 The German eye
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Rooms 8–9.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Dürer, Cranach and Holbein — the German and Northern Renaissance.
    LISTEN FOR
    The sharp, precise, slightly unsettling way Northern painters looked at the world.


 Step into the German rooms and the temperature changes. If the Italians were chasing grace and
 harmony, the German painters of the same years were after something sharper — intensity, detail, a
 slightly uncomfortable truthfulness, and a love of the strange.

 This was the world of the Reformation, of Luther and religious upheaval, and you can feel the
 seriousness of it. Two giants dominate. Albrecht Dürer — the supreme genius of the German
 Renaissance, a man who travelled to Italy to learn its lessons and brought them home filtered through a
 northern obsession with detail. And Lucas Cranach the Elder — court painter, friend of Luther, and the
 inventor of a very particular kind of cool, knowing, slightly wicked elegance, especially in his nudes.

 Between them they give you the two poles of German art: Dürer's piercing intellectual force, and
 Cranach's sly, courtly charm. Both are here at full power. Dürer first — and it's one of the strangest,
 most crowded faces-paintings you'll ever see, right this way.

 Albrecht Dürer, Jesus Among the Doctors
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 8 — a ring of grotesque faces closing in on a calm boy.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Albrecht Dürer, Jesus Among the Doctors, 1506 — oil on panel.
    LISTEN FOR
    The painting Dürer says he made in just five days — and the war of hands at its centre.

 Venice: painting becomes colour
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room 11.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Titian and El Greco — the Venetian sixteenth century.
    LISTEN FOR
    How Venice swapped Florentine drawing for pure, smouldering colour and loose paint.


 Now to Venice — and to a completely different idea of what a painting even is. In Florence, art was
 about disegno: drawing, line, design, the intellectual scaffolding underneath. In Venice, it was about
 colore: colour, light, atmosphere, paint itself laid on so you can feel the brush.

 There's a reason. Venice was a city built on water and trade, soaked in shifting light and shimmering
 reflection, awash in luxurious imported pigments. Its painters — Titian above all, with Tintoretto and
 Bassano around him — learned to build a picture out of glowing colour and loaded brushwork rather
 than hard outline. They painted flesh that looks warm, fabric you want to touch, skies that breathe.

 This Venetian revolution in colour is one of the great turning points in art; everyone downstream, from
 Rubens to the Impressionists, drinks from it. And in this same room hangs a young outsider who trained
 here before becoming the most singular painter in Spanish history — El Greco. Venice first, round the
 corner.

    Room 12.
    WHAT YOU SEE

 Cross into the seventeenth century and painting suddenly grabs you by the collar. This is the Baroque —
 the art of drama, of high contrast, of a moment caught at its most intense — and the man who lit the fuse

 His upheaval was simple and total. He painted directly from life, using ordinary people as his saints and
 sinners — dirty feet, real wrinkles, the works. And he plunged them into deep darkness, lit by a single
 raking shaft of light, so the holy moment seems to erupt out of the shadows of an ordinary room. It's
 theatre. It's a spotlight. It pulls you in close and won't let go.

 Painters across Europe were stunned, and within a generation the whole continent was painting in this
 charged, shadowed, emotional new key — from Rubens's blazing energy to the quiet candlelight of the
 north. It starts with one dark canvas, over here.

 that raw intensity pressed into the paint. From his shadows, the Baroque exploded outward; one of its
 greatest engines, Rubens, is right along here.

 Something genuinely new happens in these Dutch rooms, and it's worth pausing to feel why. The
 seventeenth-century Dutch Republic was unlike anywhere else in Europe: Protestant, so no grand
 church commissions; a republic, so no royal court; and prosperous, thanks to trade and a confident
 middle class. So who bought paintings? Ordinary citizens — merchants, brewers, shopkeepers — for
 their own homes.

 And that changed what got painted. Instead of saints and kings, Dutch artists painted the world these
 buyers lived in: their own faces, their families, their flat green landscapes under enormous skies, their
 tidy interiors, the gleam of a lemon on a laid table. Painting came down out of heaven and the palace
 and settled into the living room.

 It produced some of the most quietly perfect art ever made — and one outright genius, Rembrandt, who
 took this ordinary world and found in it the full depth of the human soul. Watch how much feeling these
 painters wring from the everyday: a face, a sky, a swept church floor. Rembrandt first — looking at
 himself, as he did all his life, close by.

 Around 1907, two young painters in Paris — Pablo Picasso and Georges Braque — looked hard at
 Cézanne's faceted world and then did something no one had dared: they smashed the single, fixed

 in these years, Braque said, that they were "roped together like mountaineers."

 It looks difficult, and it was meant to stretch you. But Cubism is the hinge of modern art — almost
 everything that follows, from abstraction to collage, runs through it. Picasso, the restless giant, first —
 not far on.

 inventing.

 about dreams. Welcome to the world beneath the rational. Dalí and the dream-painters, a little way on.

 this collection. It's among the first paintings in which he used silkscreen to print photographic images
 straight onto the canvas. (The museum is marking Rauschenberg's centenary with a special display
 devoted to this very painting.)

 Rauschenberg said he wanted to work "in the gap between art and life," and Express lives right in that
 gap. He bridged the older Abstract Expressionists and the young Pop artists — keeping their gestural
 brushwork while flooding the canvas with the mass-media imagery of modern life. The title hints at
 speed and movement, and the picture is full of it: the leaping horse, the dancers, the wheels, the figure
 descending the stair, all enlarged to a grainy, newsprint roughness and woven together so your eye
 keeps moving, never settling. You've travelled a long way from Duccio's gold leaf at the top of the
 building — from a single sacred image made for prayer to a swirling overload of media imagery made
 for a world drowning in pictures. That whole journey is the point. One more floor to go — a second,
 gentler collection waits below. This way, down to Level 0.

 A second collection, a second eye
    WHERE YOU ARE

    How a second collection, with a different taste, rounds out the museum's story.


 One more floor — and a lovely surprise to end on. Level 0 holds a separate collection with its own
 personality: the Carmen Thyssen Collection, assembled by Carmen "Tita" Cervera, the Spanish former
 beauty queen who married the last Baron and became the great champion of keeping all this art in
 Madrid.

 It began as an extension of the family collection and grew into something distinctly hers. Where the
 Baron's taste ranged encyclopaedically across all of art history, the Baroness followed her heart —
 toward landscape, toward colour, toward beauty and pleasure. So this floor is gentler and sunnier than
 the rooms above: arcadian landscapes, glowing Impressionist and Post-Impressionist scenes,
 nineteenth-century American vistas, early modern colour.

 Think of it as a second, more personal walk through some of the same centuries you've just travelled —
 a coda in a warmer key. It's smaller and easier on tired feet, and it holds at least one undisputed
 masterpiece that many visitors rank among their favourites in the whole museum. After the long descent
 through the history of everything, this is the part you can simply enjoy. The jewel of it — a Gauguin —
 is just along here.

 Paul Gauguin, Mata Mua (In Olden Times)
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Room F, the Carmen Thyssen collection — a Tahitian scene in flat, glowing colour.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Paul Gauguin, Mata Mua (In Olden Times), 1892 — oil on canvas.
    LISTEN FOR
    The paradise Gauguin invented in Tahiti, and the myth he painted over the reality.


 A radiant tropical scene — a great blue idol on the left, women in a lush Tahitian landscape, one playing
 a flute, all in deep, saturated, unreal colour.

 Mata Mua — In Olden Times — painted by Paul Gauguin in 1892, during his first stay in Tahiti. It's the
 crown jewel of the Carmen Thyssen Collection and one of the best-loved paintings in the museum.

 Gauguin had fled Europe — its cities, its industry, its art world — convinced that "civilisation" was a
 sickness and that truth and beauty survived only in places he imagined as untouched and primal. In
 Tahiti he painted not so much what he found as what he longed for: an idyllic, mythic, pre-colonial
 paradise that, in truth, had already largely vanished under European contact. The blue figure is an idol,
 the moon goddess Hina; the title looks back nostalgically to "olden times." Look at the colour — laid
 down in flat, bold, non-natural zones, the grass pink, the shadows blue — and the dreamy, decorative
 calm. This break from realistic colour and his hunger for the "primitive" made Gauguin a crucial bridge
 to twentieth-century art; Matisse and the Expressionists upstairs owe him a great deal. It's gorgeous and,
 knowing the colonial reality behind the dream, a little complicated — which only makes it richer. Two
 more highlights of the Baroness's eye are just ahead.

 John Constable and the Baroness's landscapes
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Rooms B–E, Carmen Thyssen — English skies and green country.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    John Constable and the Baroness's landscapes.
    LISTEN FOR
    How Constable made plain English weather feel monumental — and worth collecting.


 A luminous English landscape — green countryside under a big, restless sky full of moving cloud, a
 river or a mill, painted with fresh, flickering touches of light.

 A landscape by John Constable, one of the jewels of the Carmen Thyssen Collection and the heart of the
 Baroness's love of landscape painting. Constable, working in early-nineteenth-century England, is one
 of the two great English landscape masters alongside Turner.

 Constable did something quietly revolutionary: he painted the ordinary working countryside of his
 native Suffolk — the River Stour, its locks and mills and hay wagons — with a truthfulness and
 freshness nobody had brought to it before. He studied clouds like a scientist, filling the sky with weather
 you can almost feel, and he built his greens and lights from broken, flickering touches of paint that
 seemed crude to his contemporaries and prophetic to ours. When his work was shown in Paris it stunned
 the French and helped light the way toward the Impressionists you saw upstairs. This whole floor
 doubles down on the Thyssen's quiet strength — landscape — chosen by the Baroness for its light and
 loveliness rather than to fill a textbook. Let your eye rest in it. One last jewel, and then out into Madrid
 — a little further on.

 Natalia Goncharova, Fishing
    WHERE YOU ARE
    Rooms H–I, Carmen Thyssen — fishermen and nets in bold colour.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Natalia Goncharova, Fishing, c. 1909 — oil on canvas.
    LISTEN FOR
    The Russian avant-garde woman who fused folk art and modern colour into something new.


 A bold, vigorous canvas of fishermen at work — figures and nets and water broken into rhythmic,
 faceted forms, in strong, earthy, folk-bright colour.

 Fishing (also called Fishers), painted in 1909 by Natalia Goncharova, one of the leaders of the
 explosive Russian avant-garde — held here as part of the Carmen Thyssen Collection, a fitting modern
 jewel to close on.

 Goncharova matters twice over. As an artist, she fused the newest Western ideas — Cézanne's faceting,
 the Cubists' broken form, the Futurists' love of energy — with something deeply Russian: the bold
 colour, flat patterning and earthy subjects of folk art, popular prints and icons. The result, as here, is
 modern and ancient at once, sophisticated and rooted in the soil. And as a woman, she was a genuine
 leader of the avant-garde, not a footnote — provocative, prolific, internationally famous, later designing
 for the Ballets Russes in Paris. It's a fitting place to end, because it calmly rhymes with everything
 upstairs: the early-twentieth-century moment when colour and form broke free, sampled once more
 through one collector's eye. You've now seen all of it, top to bottom — seven centuries, two collections,
 one continuous story. Take a breath. Then let's step back out into Madrid — this way.

 Out into the Paseo del Arte
 And there it is — seven centuries, one staircase, one afternoon. You've walked from a Sienese altarpiece
 glowing with gold leaf all the way down to Pop Art and a lonely woman in a hotel room, and if you did
 it top to bottom, you did it exactly as the family who built this collection intended: as one continuous
 story, with no chapter missing.

 That's the quiet genius of this place. The Prado will show you the summits of Spanish and royal
 painting; the Reina Sofía will show you what Spain did to the twentieth century. But only here can you
 stand in front of Van Eyck in the morning and Pollock by mid-afternoon and feel the through-line — the
 slow, restless, endlessly inventive business of human beings trying to put the world onto a flat surface.

 Step outside onto the Paseo del Prado. You're standing on the spine of the Golden Triangle: the Prado is
 just up the boulevard, the Reina Sofía down toward Atocha, and the great green lung of the Retiro is a
 short walk east. If your feet are tired, the Thyssen's own café in the courtyard is one of the calmer spots
 in the city to sit and let it all settle.

 Whatever you do next, you've just done something rare — you've seen the whole arc, start to finish.
 Take it with you. Safe travels, and enjoy Madrid.
