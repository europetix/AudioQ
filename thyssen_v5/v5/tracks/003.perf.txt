[amused] Everyone in this painting is busy except the new mother. And even she is getting ready for dinner.

The Gospels say nothing about the birth of the Virgin Mary. The story comes from the apocryphal gospels. Around 1430, an unknown painter in Konstanz, in southern Germany, imagined it as a birth in a house of his own day.

Saint Anne sits up in bed, fully dressed, with her headdress and her halo. She's washing her hands before she eats, and a woman helps her. Another woman comes in carrying food in two covered dishes. In front, on a bench, a young woman cradles the baby, already bathed and tightly swaddled. The bed cuts across the room on a slant, and divides it into little zones of activity. The perspective wobbles a bit. Nobody seems to mind.

Now look at the right-hand edge. The panel has been cut down, and a pair of hands survives there, perhaps from someone kneeling in prayer.

Before you go, find the big Catalan panel of the two Saint Johns with a kneeling donor, painted around 1410 by an artist working in Barcelona. Until 1989 it was catalogued as the work of an anonymous master. Today it carries the name of Joan Mates.
