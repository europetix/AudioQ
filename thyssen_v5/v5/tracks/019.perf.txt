[curious] She has just turned her head, as if someone had called her name across a crowded room. And in her right hand, held between finger and thumb, is the little mask she was wearing a moment ago.

You've reached the eighteenth century. Rooms 16 to 18 are Italian painting, and most of it is Venetian. It's the Venice of Carnival and masks, of view painters working for travellers, and of Tiepolo's theatrical light.

Giambattista Piazzetta painted this small canvas in the 1720s. Over her shoulders she wears the black bautta, the cloak of Venetian women, opened just enough to show a modest neckline. She has been identified as Rosa Muziolo, a model who became the painter's wife in 1724. [pause] That may or may not be right. But the same face turns up again and again in Piazzetta's work, so painter and model clearly knew each other well. Piazzetta was usually a painter of deep shadow and heavy drama. Here he's almost tender.

[amused] For pure Venetian comedy, find Pietro Longhi's The Tickle. A young woman tickles a sleeping man's face with a feather, while two maids look on. She glances straight out at you, inviting you to keep the secret.

And Tiepolo's little oil sketch is pure tragedy. Sophonisba, a queen in Africa, lies dying beside a cup of poison. Rome wanted her as a prisoner, so her own husband sent it to her. She drank.
