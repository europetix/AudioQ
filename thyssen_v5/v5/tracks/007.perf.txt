[curious] One of the most powerful men in Europe, and he would fit inside a school notebook. This panel is only about twenty-eight centimetres tall.

Hans Holbein came back to England in 1532, after hard years in Basel. Many of his old patrons were dead or out of favour. Within a few years he was working for the king, and he was valued for one thing above all: faces that looked alive. He painted Henry's queens and possible queens, and he painted Henry.

This little portrait dates from around 1537. Henry had already broken with Rome and made himself head of the Church in England. [pause] Now look at how Holbein builds that power. The body is square to you, filling the panel. There's a sense of distance, as if you're being received. The hands sit firmly in view. One scholar spoke of the head's architectural solidity, and you can see what he meant. It's built like a wall.

[wry] Here's the twist. The Henry most of us picture, the giant figure who seems to fill a room, mostly comes from somewhere else. Holbein painted a great mural for a private room in Whitehall Palace, with Henry full-length beside his parents and Jane Seymour. It was destroyed in 1698. We know it from a copy, now in London, and the head in that copy is this head, with the same hat and the same splendid pendant jewel.

So this panel is a close-up of a lost icon. Compare it with Campin's stout man nearby, painted about a century earlier. Different worlds, the same refusal to flatter.

[quietly] Small enough to hold in two hands. Hard enough to stop you in your tracks.
