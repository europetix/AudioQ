[warmly] When Paul Gauguin sailed for Tahiti in 1891, he was looking for a world untouched by Europe. He didn't find it. What he found were the remains of an old Maori culture, already worn away by colonial rule and by the missionaries.

So he painted the world he had hoped for. Mata Mua means "in olden times". It's an elegy for a golden age that had gone, or perhaps had never quite existed in this form. The scene isn't a real place. He put it together from pieces of other pictures, like a stage set.

[pause] Now read it the way you'd read a Japanese print, from the bottom up. In front, a woman plays the flute. A huge tree trunk runs down the middle, like a hinge. Beyond it, women dance around a giant stone idol of Hina, the moon goddess. And behind everything rise mountains in pink and purple. No colour here is trying to be natural. Each one sits flat, in its own patch, against the next.

[wry] Paris was baffled. When Gauguin auctioned his work to pay for his second journey to the Pacific, nobody bought this one, even at the five hundred francs he asked. In 1984 Baron Hans Heinrich bought it at a record price, at first sharing it with a partner, and then owning it alone.

It has travelled since. It left Madrid under an export licence, and came back in 2022 as part of the lease that keeps this collection here.

[lightly] Next to it hangs Gauguin before Tahiti. A few years earlier he spent nearly four months on Martinique with the painter Charles Laval, and painted women carrying fruit to market. Look for the baskets on their heads. Edgar Degas owned that picture for the rest of his life.
