[warmly] You started this walk at the top of the building, in Room 1, with Duccio's gold. You've come down through Venice and Amsterdam, past Friedrich's dawn and Rothko's dark, to a pinball machine glowing on the ground floor.

That journey was built by two generations. Heinrich Thyssen-Bornemisza began it, buying old masters. His son, Hans Heinrich, carried it on, and in 1961 he bought his first modern paintings. Upstairs you met him in person, in Freud's portrait, sitting where Watteau's Pierrot sits. In 1993 the Spanish state bought the heart of the collection, 775 works, for this palace. And then a second collection grew beside it, the one you've just walked through, shaped by Carmen Thyssen's own eye.

[pause] So this isn't one story. It's three people's taste, a father's, a son's, and then Carmen Thyssen's, laid side by side in one building, from the 14th century to the 1970s.

If you'd like to sit with it for a while, the café-restaurant is here on the ground floor, with an outdoor terrace. And if something called you back, you can go in and out again today. The one exception is the ground-floor temporary exhibition.

Outside, you're on the Paseo del Prado, the spine of Madrid's Paseo del Arte. The Prado is a few minutes south of here, across the Plaza de Cánovas del Castillo. The Reina Sofía is further down, towards Atocha. And the Retiro park is a short walk east, if your feet would rather have trees than paintings. Yo Tours has walking audio guides for the Prado and the Reina Sofía too, built the same way as this one.

[warmly] Thank you for walking it with us. Seven centuries, two collections, one visit. Enjoy the rest of your day in Madrid.
