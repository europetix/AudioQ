[warmly] Upstairs, in Room 33, you saw Berthe Morisot indoors, with a young woman at her mirror. Here she has stepped outside, into a summer garden.

In 1890 and again in 1891, Morisot rented a house at Mézy, on the Seine, about fifty kilometres west of Paris. She came with her husband, Eugène Manet, and their daughter Julie. The house had an orchard and a terrace above the river, and the country setting pushed her towards new subjects.

[pause] This was one of them, and she went about it in an unexpected way. Morisot was one of the founders of Impressionism, a painter of the quick, fresh touch. Yet she built this picture, in the museum's phrase, in time-honoured academic fashion. She painted the river valley on its own. She painted a goat called Colette. She made drawings and a compositional sketch, and three painted versions of the reclining shepherdess.

She posed the model nude, to understand the body, but let her keep her headscarf on. Look for it. It's the one thing left of the shepherdess.

By then she admired Renoir's nudes, and Renoir came to visit her at Mézy. But a nude out in the open air also looks back to Corot, who had been her teacher. The figure here is more compact and contained than her earlier work. It's the most complete and ambitious nude she ever painted.

[quietly] And somewhere along the way the shepherdess turned into something else: a nymph, or a spring, lost in thought beside the water.

Nearby, Alfred Sisley's Flood at Port-Marly. In March 1876 the Seine broke its banks, and Sisley painted seven pictures of it, stage by stage. Here the water fills the rue de Paris.
