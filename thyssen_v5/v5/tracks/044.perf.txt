[amused] A cow is flying across this painting, and it is playing the violin. In Marc Chagall's world, that is perfectly normal. What is not normal is the journey this canvas made.

In the late 1930s, as the persecution of Jews in Europe grew into the Holocaust, Chagall, who was Jewish, began painting scenes from the Christian story, among them a Crucifixion. He started this Madonna soon afterwards. Then came the war. Fleeing the German advance, he took refuge in Gordes, a hill town in Provence, and there he went on working on the canvas, repainting parts he had already laid in. He only finished it in 1942, in New York.

[quietly] Look at her. The Virgin is enormous, dressed not in blue but in a white bridal gown, holding her child, floating near a small village. Angels sing and play around her. And the cow keeps the music going. It is a dream of peace, painted by a man on the run.

[lightly] Now find The Grey House, a smaller picture from 1917. In the summer of 1914, Chagall went home to Vitebsk, in what is now Belarus, for what was meant to be a short visit. The war and then the Revolution kept him in Russia for eight years. Here is his hometown in grey: a wooden house, a church steeple, wild clouds. To him the town was both sad and gay. On the left a small figure stands with a hand on his chest, possibly Chagall himself. His name is written on the jacket. And on the wooden fence, in Russian, is the word for idiot. Two scholars read it as a private joke: "Chagall is the village idiot."

The poet André Breton said that with Chagall, "the metaphor made its triumphant return into modern painting."
