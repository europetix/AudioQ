[quietly] For more than ten years, Claude Monet had been losing his colours. Cataracts were clouding his eyes. An operation in 1923 changed the way he saw colour, and for a long time nothing seemed to help.

Then, in the spring of 1925, it came back. He began to work intensely. That July he wrote to a friend, apologising that he couldn't see him in the mornings, because he had to paint. His sight was much better, he said, and he was very pleased with his work. [warmly] If only his lenses were better still, he would like to live to a hundred.

This is one of six pictures from that summer. It's his own house at Giverny, seen from the garden. The slate roof catches the light and turns violet. The upstairs windows are dark gaps. And two great rose trees in bloom frame the front, the left one so close that the edge of the canvas cuts it off.

[pause] Look at the brushwork. Short strokes, like commas, in far more colours than some of his earlier garden views, which drift towards a single tone. The house sits square in the middle, almost symmetrical, almost solemn. Some see it pointing straight towards the abstract painting of the new century. Monet died the following year.

[lightly] Nearby, a Spanish square. The American Childe Hassam travelled through Spain in 1910, painting in Madrid, Toledo, Córdoba, Seville and Ronda. He looked at this plaza in Ronda, it seems, from his hotel window. Back in New York, critics loved the strong light and blue sky, and praised the pictures as "artistic, not operatic". No bullfights, no castanets. Just a place, and the sun on it.
