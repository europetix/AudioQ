[curious] Natalia Goncharova once gave a speech in Moscow about Cubism. It was, she said, "a positive phenomenon, but it is not altogether new." Russia, she argued, had been doing it for centuries, in ancient Scythian stone figures and in painted fairground dolls.

That's the spirit of this painting. The museum calls her the first, in time, of the leading women artists of 20th-century Russia. On visits to her family's country estate near Moscow, she watched the peasants at work. She loved the crafts and rituals of old Russia, its icons, its popular prints, its folk art. With Mikhail Larionov she would put on an exhibition of icons and broadsheets in Moscow, as if they were the newest thing in art.

[pause] Now look at Fishing. Bright colour, emphatic outlines, flat decorative shapes. The perspective is naive on purpose. Some of that comes from Russia. Some of it comes from France, from Gauguin, whose Tahiti you've just seen in Room F, and from Matisse. She takes both and makes them her own. Upstairs, in Room 43, you met other women of the Russian avant-garde.

Later she designed for Diaghilev's productions in Paris, The Golden Cockerel and The Firebird among them.

[lightly] She keeps good company here. Picasso's Harvesters dates from 1907, the same months as his Demoiselles d'Avignon. Look for the peasant striding towards you with arms raised, and a cartwheel in the middle. And the huge canvas is Robert Delaunay's Large Portuguese. The First World War caught him and his wife Sonia on holiday in San Sebastián. They stayed in the Iberian peninsula, in Madrid and then in northern Portugal, painting its market women in that clear northern light.
