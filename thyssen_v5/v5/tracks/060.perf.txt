[warmly] Everything upstairs began with one family. This floor belongs to one woman's eye.

Carmen Cervera, the Spanish wife of Baron Hans Heinrich, was the decisive voice in bringing his collection to Madrid. When the State bought the heart of it in 1993, the family kept other works. Those became the core of her own collection, and she went on buying, above all landscape, Impressionism and German Expressionism. Since 2022 it has been leased to the Spanish state, about 330 works, until the late 2030s, with an option for the State to buy it at the end. It's hung in order, from the 17th century to the 20th, and individual works are sometimes lent or rotated.

[pause] We start in Paradise. Jan Brueghel, younger son of the great Pieter Bruegel, painted this around 1610. It's one of the earliest of the many Gardens of Eden he would paint. In his day, he and Rubens were the leading painters in Antwerp.

Now look for Adam and Eve. Don't search the foreground. They're far away in the background, two small figures in a crowded, exotic world. The real subject is the animals.

[conspiratorial] And some of them are borrowed. The horse probably comes from a lost equestrian portrait by Rubens. The lion and lioness on the left come from Rubens's Daniel in the Lions' Den. The leopards repeat two from another lost Rubens, which survives only as an engraving. It isn't theft. The two men worked side by side, and on another version of this same subject, Brueghel painted the landscape and animals while Rubens painted the figures.

[lightly] Infrared photographs show that Brueghel changed his mind about the horse and the camel while he worked. Even in Paradise, the details took some arranging.

Room B, North American landscape, has a short Extra on Bierstadt's Sundown at Yosemite. The next main stop is Room C, with Van Gogh's wide Watermill at Gennep.
