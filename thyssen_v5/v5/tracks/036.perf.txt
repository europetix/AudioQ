[amused] In the spring of 1874, a Paris critic walked round an exhibition by a group of young painters and went home sharpening his pen. One picture, by Claude Monet, was called Impression. The critic, Louis Leroy, borrowed the word for the title of a biting article in the satirical paper Le Charivari. A few days later another critic used the same word as praise, and the name stuck.

Berthe Morisot was one of the founders of that group. She showed with them almost every time they exhibited, alongside Monet, Renoir and Degas.

[warmly] Her painting here is from 1876. A young woman stands in her bedroom in a white dressing gown, in front of a tall mirror that tilts on its stand. The French call that kind of mirror a psyché, and the title plays on both meanings: a piece of furniture, and Psyche, the girl loved by Eros in the old myth. The mirror was Morisot's own. Now look at the whites and soft greys of the fabrics. When the painting was shown at the group's third exhibition, Émile Zola praised exactly those greys and whites.

[pause] Two men in this room had a very different relationship with the group. Édouard Manet never exhibited with them. His horsewoman looks straight out at you, but she is no rider. The model was a bookseller's daughter, and the riding habit was borrowed from a painter friend. Manet meant the picture for the Salon of 1883. He died that spring, and it stayed unfinished.

[curious] And then Degas. His dancer in green spins on one leg while the others are cut off by the edge, as if you were watching through opera glasses from a high side box. One of its first owners was the English painter Walter Sickert.
