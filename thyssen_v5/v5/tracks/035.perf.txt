[warmly] She married at seventeen. By the time John Singer Sargent painted her, in 1904, Millicent, Duchess of Sutherland, was thirty-seven and one of the great hostesses of her day. She ran Stafford House in London, across from Buckingham Palace, and Dunrobin Castle in Scotland.

[pause] That was only half of her. She was also a writer and a tireless charity worker for the poorest people of her time. And ten years later, when the First World War broke out, she went to work as a nurse, and France gave her the Croix de Guerre.

By then Sargent was the portraitist the British aristocracy wanted. He had left Paris after the scandal over his Madame X, settled in London, and learned to paint in the grand manner of Van Dyck and Reynolds.

Look at how he sets her. She stands full length in a shaded garden, in a green dress with a pattern of flowers, one hand resting on a fountain. On her red hair there's a tiara of laurel leaves, as if she had just stepped out of the trees. The pose borrows from Reynolds, who liked to paint duchesses as classical goddesses.

[curious] When it was shown at the Royal Academy that year, the critic Roger Fry didn't like it. Others compared it to Gainsborough and Reynolds. And the American writer Henry Adams, who saw it in Paris, wrote: "Never did any artist paint such psychology as Sargent."

Before you leave, lean in to the small still life by William Harnett, from 1879. A pipe is still smouldering on the edge of the table, among spent matches and a half-eaten biscuit. The folded newspaper even shows its date. It's a vanitas, a reminder that life is short, made not with skulls but with the things someone leaves behind when they step out of the room. [amused] One writer called the effect a magic palpability. You may want to brush the crumbs away.
