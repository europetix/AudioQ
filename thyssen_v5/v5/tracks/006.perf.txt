[quietly] Behind this young woman there's a little slip of paper, painted with a Latin verse and a date in Roman numerals. The date is 1488. That's the year she died.

Her name was Giovanna degli Albizzi. She married Lorenzo Tornabuoni of Florence in 1486, and two years later she died in childbirth. Ghirlandaio also painted her full-length in a fresco in the church of Santa Maria Novella. So was this panel painted from life, or as a memorial? Scholars disagree: one argued it came before the fresco, another saw it as a portrait made after her death.

Look into the niche behind her. A jewel with a dragon, two pearls and a ruby, for her public life. A prayer book and a string of coral beads, a rosary, for her inner life. And between them that verse, adapted from the Roman poet Martial. [warmly] "Art, if only you could reproduce the character and the spirit. There would be no finer portrait in the world."

This is the portrait room, and it shows how the portrait was born. In Italy, painters kept the strict profile, like a face on a coin. In the north, they turned their sitters towards you. Find Robert Campin's stout man, every wrinkle and bristle of stubble on show. Then Francesco del Cossa's man holding up a ring, almost full face. An Italian who has turned to meet you.

Giovanna's profile is the museum's signature image, and now you know why it feels so still.
