[warmly] Several of the Europeans in these rooms, Mondrian and Max Ernst among them, ended up in New York, fleeing the war. They found a city where American painters were ready to take the lead, and after the war the centre of modern art moved across the Atlantic.

The most famous of those Americans is Jackson Pollock. From the winter of 1947 he left the easel behind and dripped and poured paint onto his canvases with sticks, spoons and knives. [curious] "When I'm in the painting," he said, "I'm not aware of what I'm doing." The drip paintings made his name. And then, at the height of that success, he changed direction. In June 1951 he wrote to friends that he had had "a period of drawing on canvas in black."

This is one of those Black Paintings. Here the black has turned to brown, laid on straight from the tin with sticks and dry brushes, over raw, unprimed canvas. Silver paint is splashed across it. Look for the shapes that seem almost to be figures. The open, all-over web of the drip paintings has gone.

[pause] And there is a mystery. A photograph of Pollock's studio taken that same year shows this canvas next to its twin, Brown and Silver II. The two were painted side by side on one long roll of canvas and then cut apart. Neither is signed, so no one can be sure which way up they belong. You may be looking at it sideways. It is also the only Pollock in this collection.

[quietly] Nearby hangs Arshile Gorky's Last Painting. It was on his easel the day he took his own life, in July 1948. Its other title, The Black Monk, may come from a story by Chekhov.
