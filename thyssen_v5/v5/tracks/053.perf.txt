[curious] Georgia O'Keeffe spent much of her life telling people what her flowers were *not* about. [pause] This painting shows you why she had to keep saying it.

It's a white iris, painted in 1957, long after she had left New York for the light of New Mexico. There's no vase here, no stem, no garden. The flower fills the whole canvas and runs off its edges, as if you had pushed your face right into the petals. She seems to have learned that framing from the camera, from the photographers around Alfred Stieglitz in New York. Cropping the bloom so tightly turns it into something close to abstract.

For decades, critics read these close-up flowers as bodies, as sexual images. O'Keeffe always rejected that. In a catalogue text she wrote that people were seeing their own associations in her flowers, not hers. The museum suggests another way in: a meditation on creation, on how the spiritual and the material meet.

[pause] So look at it the way she asked. Follow one petal until the edge of the canvas cuts it off. You never get the whole flower. You only get what's in front of your eyes.

Now turn to the wide canvas of red, orange and yellow. It began as a memory of Texas, where she had watched cattle driven across the dry plains, kicking up dust and making a deafening noise. She painted it first thirty-five years earlier, as an upright picture. In 1954 she made it again, laid flat and wide, in the flaming colours of a sunset. [lightly] To her dealer, Edith Halpert, she wrote that the colour was "just plain color out of the tube", red and orange to lemon.

[warmly] A flower, and a plain. Get close enough, or far enough, and each one stops being a thing and becomes a feeling.
