[curious] There's a woman in this painting who didn't believe it. And her hands have paid the price.

The story comes from an early text outside the Bible, the Gospel of Pseudo-Matthew. Joseph goes off to find midwives. By the time he comes back with two of them, Zelomi and Salome, Mary has already given birth. Zelomi examines her and believes. Salome doubts, and tests the miracle for herself. As a punishment, her hands are paralysed.

Now find her, in the foreground. She stretches her stiffened hands towards the Child. And above Joseph, an angel in white is telling her that this child will heal her.

Jacques Daret painted it around 1434, as one wing of an altarpiece for an abbot's funeral chapel in Arras. He had trained with Robert Campin in Tournai, and he borrowed a great deal from his master's own Nativity, now in Dijon. Its other panels are now in Berlin and Paris, and one is lost.

[amused] Look at Joseph. He's holding a lit candle. It's a detail from the visions of Saint Bridget, where the light of the newborn Christ outshines Joseph's little flame. And there's the Child, lying on the bare ground, wrapped in golden rays. Joseph's candle is no longer needed.

[pause] One honest note. Jan van Eyck's tiny Annunciation, which usually hangs in this room, is on loan to the Mauritshuis in The Hague until January 2027.

But Van Eyck is still here, in a way. Find the small Virgin and Child by a follower of Dirck Bouts. It's built on Van Eyck's Virgin of the Fountain, with the same rich cloth behind Mary and the same low wall of stone blocks. And close by, Gerard David's Crucifixion, with its sharp oranges, greens and yellows, shows how these lessons kept travelling.

Room 4 has a short Extra on Bramantino's pale, red-eyed Risen Christ. The next main stop is Room 5, the portrait room.
