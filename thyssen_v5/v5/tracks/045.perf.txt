[curious] Before the Cubists, there is Cézanne. His Seated Man, probably his old gardener, was painted in the last years of his life, and parts of the canvas are simply left bare. Cézanne said he wanted to make of Impressionism "something solid and durable, like the art of museums." Picasso and Braque started from there, and this room hangs him at the head of the story.

[pause] Now the two partners. Braque painted his Woman with a Mandolin in the spring of 1910. He had been looking at Corot's quiet women with musical instruments, and he understood that an instrument gives a sitter the calm of a still life. The woman has broken into small planes of ochre, grey and brown. Look for the mandolin first, then the hand that holds it. The head and shoulders are almost gone.

Picasso's Man with a Clarinet came a year and a half later, after a summer working beside Braque in Céret, in the French Pyrenees. Find the shape of a pyramid, wide at the bottom. That is the man. The cylinder at the centre is his instrument, perhaps a clarinet, though one scholar thinks it is a tenora, the reed instrument of Catalan sardana bands.

[wry] Then the war turned both paintings into enemy property. The Picasso belonged to a German collector in Paris, and the Braque to the German dealer Kahnweiler. France confiscated what they owned, and in 1921 it was sold off at auctions in Paris. The poet André Breton went to the sales. He bought the Braque, and he called the Picasso "a work of fabulous elegance."

One more painter here was born in Madrid: Juan Gris. His Smoker is a wealthy American friend in a top hat and bow tie, painted at Céret two years after the Picasso. Gris used planes of green, blue, orange and red, colours the two inventors were not using at the time. When his confiscated stock came up for sale, Kahnweiler bought this one back.
