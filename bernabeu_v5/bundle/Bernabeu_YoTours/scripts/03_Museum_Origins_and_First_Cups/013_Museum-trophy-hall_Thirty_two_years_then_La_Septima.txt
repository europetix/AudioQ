Between cup number six and cup number seven, there is a gap. It lasts thirty-two years.

The sixth was won in 1966. For the next three decades, the most famous club in European football couldn't win Europe's biggest prize. Other clubs took their turn, from Amsterdam to Munich to Liverpool. Madrid kept trying, and kept coming home empty-handed.

They reached only one final in all those years, in Paris at the start of the eighties, against Liverpool. A late goal by Alan Kennedy settled it, one goal to nothing.

It's worth standing here for a moment, because this gap tells you something the shine doesn't. This club has known long years of not being good enough, and people who call Real Madrid arrogant tend to forget that. Its confidence had to be rebuilt.

The night it was rebuilt was the twentieth of May, 1998, in Amsterdam. The opponents were Juventus, playing in their third final in a row. Madrid's coach was Jupp Heynckes.

Midway through the second half, in the sixty-sixth minute, the striker Predrag Mijatović scored the only goal of the game. Thirty-two years were over.

In Spanish, the cup has its own name. La Séptima, the Seventh. Madrid fans give their European Cups names like this, the way other people name their children.

Now look at that seventh cup. It may not shine any brighter than the others, but for a whole generation of supporters it was the first one they had ever seen their club win.

Move along the row to cup number nine, won in Glasgow in 2002.
