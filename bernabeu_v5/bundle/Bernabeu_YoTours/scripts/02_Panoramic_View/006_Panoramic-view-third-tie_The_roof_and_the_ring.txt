Not one pillar in these stands holds up the roof above you. The whole thing is carried from the edges of the stadium.

According to the club, the roof rests on supports built into the towers on the Castellana side, and on two more across the stadium, on Calle Padre Damián. Between them run enormous steel trusses. The club puts the moving roof at fifty-five metres above the ground. And the company that built those trusses says the roof took somewhere between twelve and thirteen thousand tonnes of steel. With ordinary grades of steel, it says, the roof would have weighed far more.

The centre of the roof can close over the playing area, and open again. Closing takes around a quarter of an hour. When it's shut, the whole bowl is under cover.

Now bring your eyes down a little, to the band of screens hanging below the roof's inner edge. The club calls it a three-hundred-and-sixty-degree video scoreboard, a single ring running all the way around. It isn't the same height everywhere. Along the sides and corners it's six and a half metres tall. Behind each goal it rises to eleven metres. And every panel is tilted by eight degrees, so the ring isn't a straight wall at all. It's a shallow cone with the top cut off.

The club is careful here. It doesn't claim the world's first. It says only two other stadiums, both in the United States, had anything like it. It was built by the American company Daktronics.

Its first night was the Clásico against Barcelona, on the 21st of April, 2024. Real Madrid won three goals to two. The winner came from Jude Bellingham in stoppage time, and the new ring had its first great goal to show.

Now look down from the rail, at the floor of the stadium, and whatever is or isn't covering it today.
