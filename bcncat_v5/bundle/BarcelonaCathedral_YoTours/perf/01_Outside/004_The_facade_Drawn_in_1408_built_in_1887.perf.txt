The front you photograph is not medieval. It went up at the end of the 1800s, "following 15th-century plans", as the national art museum puts it.

The plans are far older than the stone. In 1408 a master from Rouen called Carlí drew the main portal. His drawing survives, in the cathedral's archive: ink on twelve sheets of parchment. Four are lost, and what's left still stands over three metres tall. It isn't on show.

The medieval builders never finished this front. Nine years after Carlí's drawing, they closed it with a plain wall, and it stayed that way for over four centuries. Why? A University of Barcelona report gives the reason: "The lack of budget in medieval Barcelona prevented this project" from being built.

In the 1880s came a competition to finish it, with one rule. Every design had to start from the medieval drawing.

[wry] Then the money. Manuel Girona, a banker who had been mayor of Barcelona, tried to raise the funds and failed. So he paid for it himself. Work began in the summer of 1887. The main front was finished three years later, on the twelfth of February, Santa Eulàlia's feast day. The lantern tower behind it was finished in 1913, by his children.

[pause] One name people expect here is missing. Gaudí built nothing here. As a young man he only helped draw a rival design, and it lost.

Now find Christ, standing on the central pillar of the door, with the apostles either side of him. Then let your eye travel out to the sides of the building. Beside this busy front, the old walls are plain and sober: buttresses and octagonal towers, and very little else.

[warmly] Barcelona waited almost five centuries for its front door.
