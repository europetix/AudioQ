Every hour you hear over the old town is struck by a bell called Eulàlia. She hangs in the tower over the Sant Iu door, the side door you've just left. Her smaller partner, Honorata, strikes the quarters. Both were cast in 1865 by Francesc Calbetó.

[conspiratorial] Honorata comes with a story. The city's own guide to the cathedral calls her the rebellious bell. In 1714, it says, during the War of Succession, King Philip the Fifth "imprisoned" an earlier Honorata and melted her down. Her crime was ringing to call the citizens to revolt.

[pause] It's a good story, and it rests on that one telling. A recent survey of the cathedral's bells lists the Honoratas lost and recast, and gives no reason. The Honorata you'll hear today is the one Calbetó cast.

There are two bell towers, eight-sided and over fifty metres high. One stands over the Sant Iu door and carries the clock. The other stands over the door from the church into the cloister. They went up after the old Romanesque bell tower came down, and the first bell was hoisted before the end of the 1300s. One tower is medieval. Its twin was finished two centuries later.

[warmly] The oldest bell in the towers was cast in 1321: a small bell, an esquella in Catalan, called l'Esquella de Prima.

In all there are about twenty bells, nearly all named after women saints. Eleven of them hang in the tower over the cloister, the number the cathedrals of the old Crown of Aragon traditionally had. [amused] One has gone by the name of the thief's bell. The survey doesn't say why.

At noon the Angelus is rung on one of the big bells, la Tomasa.
