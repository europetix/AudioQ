A cathedral roof is a fine place to be on a clear day, and a poor one in a storm. A historian of the cathedral's cloister found the dates in the city's old ceremonial records. Lightning struck the cathedral on the fifth of April 1582, and again on an August day sixty-four years later. Where it hit, and what it broke, nobody knows.

Fire nearly came from below, too. In 1606, Barcelona's public television tells, the firecrackers of Saint John's Eve, the city's midsummer night, almost burned the cathedral down.

The roof you're about to walk reopened in 2024, after works. Some two thousand square metres of tiles were redone, with new stairs, new handrails and an updated lift, and the original floor tiles were recovered. The Catalan and Spanish governments paid.

Up there you'll also be in the company of the gargoyles. In the middle of the 1400s a carver called Joan Claperós made gargoyles for the cloister below. The city counts two hundred on the roofs and pinnacles. And among the monsters, it says, there's a bull, a unicorn and an elephant.
