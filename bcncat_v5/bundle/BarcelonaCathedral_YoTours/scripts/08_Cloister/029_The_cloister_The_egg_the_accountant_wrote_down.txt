Every year at Corpus Christi an egg dances on this fountain. And we know how old the custom is because the canons' accountant wrote down the eggs.

It's called l'ou com balla, the dancing egg. An egg is set on the fountain's jet of water, where it bobs and turns, and the basin is dressed with flowers and red cherries. The cathedral says it was the first place in Barcelona to present the dancing egg, going back at least to 1636. Barcelona's popular-culture pages explain how we know. The cathedral's account books record eggs bought for this fountain, for Corpus Christi and the eight days that follow. It's popularly thought, the same pages add, that the egg stands for the Host, the body of Christ in the Eucharist.

Now look up into the little vault over the fountain. At its centre is the great keystone, Saint George killing the dragon. A study of this cloister calls him the patron of Barcelona's citizens. Seated on the ribs around him are the four evangelists. And on top of the pavilion stands a figure of Saint George.

Here's the date everyone gets wrong: 1448. It's often given for the cathedral, but it belongs to the cloister. The city's feasts for Saint George were often held right here, round this fountain. In April 1456 the Generalitat's diary noted that a public proclamation had been made through the city: Saint George's day was to be a holiday.
