Under this organ there once hung a carved Saracen's head, a carassa. The city dates the cathedral's one to the 16th century. A newspaper tells how, on three winter feasts, Saint Stephen's day, the Holy Innocents and New Year's Eve, its eyes and mouth moved, worked by strings from inside the organ. And in 1892, the same paper says, its beard was remade from three donkey tails.

"It was the organists that made their eyes move and got sweets out of their mouths", says the city's popular-culture service. That's told of the city's carasses in general, and the newspaper puts the sweets at another church.

Then the turn. By the middle of the 20th century the carasses had been taken down. One reason was the noise. Another, the city explains, was what they showed: a severed head, which came to be seen "as an apology for xenophobia and racism".

The organ above was made around 1540 by Pere Flamenc and Antoni Carbonell. Gabriel Blancafort restored it in the 1980s and 1990s: fifty-seven stops, four keyboards and pedals. His workshop calls it "the largest and most important historical organ in Catalonia". Carbonell also worked for the knights of the Golden Fleece, in the choir.

Outside this door, in the street, an inscription records the day the Gothic works began: the first of May 1298, while King Jaume of Aragon reigned.

Look up at the organ, high in its gallery over the door, and at the space below it, where the face once rolled its eyes.

A face that rolled its eyes at the feast days, and that the city now explains.

The lift to the roof leaves from the chapel beside this door, the chapel of the Holy Innocents. Join the queue there. If there's no queue, ride up and play the next stop at the top, to one side of the lift: The lift · Eulàlia strikes the hours.
