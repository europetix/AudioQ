The choir in front of you, its stalls, its painted shields, its pulpit, came through the summer of 1936 intact.

That July, as the Civil War began, churches burned across Barcelona. This one was not attacked. The Catalan government chose to protect it. The cathedral's dean has explained the choice. The government, in his words, "realized it could not protect everything, so it decided to safeguard what it deemed most important and significant, the Cathedral of Barcelona."

Nearby, the church of Sant Sever was guarded by the Mossos d'Esquadra, the Catalan police. It was one of only four churches in the old city that were not burned.

On the square outside, not everything was spared. According to one account, a lorry carrying church carvings, most of them Baroque, from the seminary's museum, was forced by the crowd to stop in front of the cathedral. Its cargo was burned. The cathedral itself was left alone.

The archive was in danger too. The Catalan government's archive service, led by Agustí Duran i Sanpere, carried it out in haste. It went first to a convent in the old town, its manuscript books to the national library, and later further still. The cathedral's own record closes the story in one line, in Catalan: "La primavera de l'any 1939, l'arxiu retorna a la Seu." In the spring of 1939, the archive came back to the cathedral.

Sant Oleguer's tomb was moved too, from the Lepanto chapel down into the crypt.

The national art museum draws the conclusion. Because the cathedral was not attacked, much of its interior has survived as it was. Look back at the choir once more, and at the church around it.
