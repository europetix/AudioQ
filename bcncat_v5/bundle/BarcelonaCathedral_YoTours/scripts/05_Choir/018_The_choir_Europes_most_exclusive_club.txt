For four days in March 1519, the most exclusive club in Europe met in these seats. This is the canons' choir, in the middle of the church, and the seats themselves are older than the paint: they were carved at the end of the 1300s. The paint came with the club.

The club was the Toisó d'Or, the Golden Fleece, an order of knights founded in 1429 by Philip the Good. Philip was a duke, not a king. An order of his own, notes a history of it on the cathedral's website, added to his prestige.

The fleece itself came from a Greek myth, the one Jason and his Argonauts sailed off to find. A pagan myth sat a little awkwardly on a Christian order. So, two years after the founding, the order's chancellor decided that a biblical origin was preferable: the fleece of Gideon, a judge of Israel.

By 1519 the grand master was the young King Charles, the future emperor Charles the Fifth, and he held the order's chapter here. It was the first time the order had met outside the lands of Burgundy and, as far as we know, the only time it ever met in Spain.

Now look at the shields painted above the upper seats. There are fifty-one coats of arms, one for every knight of the order. Only about a dozen knights actually sat under them. Charles had enlarged the order, and the new places were filled here. Among the arms are those of distant kings. Pick one shield and follow the collar of the order painted around it.

They were painted for the chapter of 1519 by Joan de Borgonya, a northern painter living in Barcelona. And the cathedral's chapter didn't pay for them. The receipts show the money came from the order's own treasurers. The order paid for its own coats of arms. An art historian who studied these stalls writes that when Charles visited, he saw a choir very like today's.
