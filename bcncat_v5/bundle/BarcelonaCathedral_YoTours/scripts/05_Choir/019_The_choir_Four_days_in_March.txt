On the fifth of March 1519, at about three in the afternoon, a procession set out for the cathedral. It was the first day of the knights' chapter, and they were coming for the vespers of Saint Andrew, their order's patron. Trumpets, clarions, sackbuts and shawms played them in.

An account written at the time tells what happened inside, in Spanish. "And then the singers of the chapel, and the great organ, sang very solemnly vespers and compline." That wasn't the organ over the Sant Iu door. That one came about twenty years later. Charles heard the organ that came before it.

The four days had their own order. One day for the patron saint. One for the order's dead brothers, with a Requiem. And on the last morning, the eighth of March, a Mass of Our Lady.

The order brought its own tapestries for the occasion, and hung them around these stalls. They told the story of Gideon and his fleece, the fleece the order had chosen. A music historian notes that they changed the very sound of the choir. Look at the backs of the stalls and picture them hung with Gideon's story, and the knights seated below, each under his own arms.

But the church had already seen a funeral that month. On the second of March, during the funeral rites for the emperor Maximilian, Charles's grandfather, a catafalque stood here. The royal chapel sang the vespers of the dead from a platform beside the door of the high altar. The trumpets were silent, and the bells tolled twice a day.

Charles was still in Barcelona that July, when the news came that he had been elected emperor.
