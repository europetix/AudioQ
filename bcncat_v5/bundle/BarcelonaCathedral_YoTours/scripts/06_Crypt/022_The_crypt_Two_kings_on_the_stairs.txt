In July 1339 two kings came down these steps. With them came their queens, a cardinal and bishops, and they came for a saint. King Peter the Ceremonious wrote in his own chronicle that he, the king of Mallorca, the cardinal, the archbishop and several bishops carried her body with their own hands.

Who was she? Eulàlia, by tradition a girl of thirteen martyred in 304, is Barcelona's own saint. Her relics had been hidden, the story goes, when the Moors came. In 877 Bishop Frodoí found them at Santa Maria de les Arenes, the church that is today Santa Maria del Mar. Four and a half centuries later, the kings brought them to this tomb.

You'll often read that this tomb is alabaster. It's marble, not alabaster: white marble, partly painted and gilded.

Look closely. Round the edge of the box, in Gothic capitals, the tomb writes its own history, and it names the kings of Aragon and Mallorca. On the front of the lid it shows its own inauguration: the king and four bishops, as the cathedral describes the scene. The box stands on eight columns of different shapes. If you're down beside it, look straight up. The keystone overhead shows the Virgin and Child crowning Eulàlia.

Behind the tomb, if you can make it out, is a much older stone, a plaque from the ninth century. Its Latin text begins with these words. "Here rests blessed Eulàlia, martyr of Christ, who suffered in the city of Barcelona." It goes on to name the governor, Dacian, and the bishop who found her, Frodoí.

Two masters made this place. The crypt is thought the finest work of Jaume Fabre, a master builder from Mallorca. The tomb was carved by the workshop of a sculptor from Pisa, almost certainly Lupo di Francesco.

Barcelona's public television calls this crypt, where her remains lie, the most sacred place in the cathedral.
