There's a saint in this painting wearing spectacles. On the left, Saint Jerome reads his Bible, a pair of reading glasses on his nose, his lion asleep at his feet. Find the spectacles. On the right kneels the man who paid for the painting: unshaven, tired under the eyes, his hat in his hand.

He was Lluís Desplà, canon and archdeacon of this cathedral, a humanist and a collector of antiquities. Just outside, beside the cathedral, he also rebuilt the old archdeacon's house.

The painting is the Pietà of Canon Desplà, by Bartolomé Bermejo, and it's dated 1490. The Virgin, still young, in mourning, sits on the ground. Across her lap lies the body of her dead son, and she looks down at him.

Now look along the bottom of the frame for the painter's signature, in Latin capitals: "OPUS BARTOLOMEI VERMEJO CORDUBENSIS". The work of Bartolomé Bermejo of Córdoba. It's dated the twenty-third of April, Saint George's day, the saint over the cloister fountain.

Bermejo was a nickname. It means reddish: the red one. His real name was Bartolomé de Cárdenas, and he was born in Córdoba. The National Gallery in London says "it seems likely that he was a converso", a Jew converted to Christianity. That, the national art museum thinks, very possibly forced him into a wandering life. This is his last known work. When he died, nobody knows.

Now look past the figures. The national art museum calls the background "an extraordinary landscape". On the left, a storm breaks over the cliffs. On the right, the sun rises over a city, perhaps Jerusalem. There's a farmhouse with a palm tree, and a horseman riding alone. And in the foreground, among the plants, the small creatures: lizards, butterflies, beetles. The same museum calls it "a real botanical and animal universe".

A saint in spectacles, a patron on his knees, and a painter who signed with a nickname.
