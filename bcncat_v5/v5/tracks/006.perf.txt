Most cathedrals put their lantern tower over the crossing, near the altar. Here it's over your head as you walk in. This is the cimbori, the lantern tower, and inside it rises forty-one metres.

In 1421 the master builder, Bartomeu Gual, and a carpenter made an eighteen-day journey to Valencia, to see how they had built their tower over the crossing. Back home, the money seems to have run out. [amused] So they put a temporary roof on the unfinished tower. The stopgap lasted almost five hundred years, into the 1900s.

Why over the door at all? The bay by the door is the longest in the church. It was built that length to carry the tower.

[pause] Now look along the nave. The church is laid out simply: three aisles, one apse, and a walkway behind the altar. The side aisles rise almost as high as the centre. The keystones at the top of the centre are twenty-six metres above you.

The city says the work took more than a hundred and fifty years, and never interrupted the services. They started at the far end, behind the altar, and worked their way towards this door. 

[curious] Look up at the ribs of the vault, to the carved bosses where they meet. There are over two hundred of these keystones in the church. They were found to be painted when they were cleaned in 1970. Pick one out above you, and look for the colour.

Now find the chapel close to the main door, on the right as you face the high altar: the chapel of the Christ of Lepanto. Stop at its doorway. If Mass is on, don't go in. You can hear this from the door: The Lepanto chapel · The Christ older than its battle.
