Thirteen white geese live in this cloister. There should be thirteen, anyway. [amused] Count them, if they'll keep still long enough.

Why thirteen? The cathedral answers with a careful phrase: "according to tradition". The geese represent, it says, the age of Saint Eulàlia when she was martyred. That's the girl of thirteen in the legend, Barcelona's own saint. The city goes further. Its pages speak of "the thirteen white geese of Santa Eulàlia, one for each of the agonies she suffered, as well as for the years of her life."

[pause] Under the legend, there is a fact. In the cathedral's own words: "The presence of the geese in the Cloister has been documented since the 16th century." Geese have lived here for at least four hundred years.

Why geese, though? Nobody knows for sure. Some say they were watchmen. Some say their whiteness stands for purity. And a folk tale, repeated on Barcelona's public television, makes Eulàlia a goose-girl, which is why, it says, there are geese in the cloister. Each of these is a guess. None of them can be proved.

Today they're well looked after. The cathedral describes daily cleaning, a pond whose water is constantly renewed, a diet prescribed by a vet, and regular check-ups.

[warmly] Find them now, on the water or on the grass beside the pond, and watch them for a moment.

Thirteen geese, four centuries, and nobody wrote down why.

Walk on to the cloister's door to the street with a Pietà carved above it, the Pietat door. Stand near it, where you can also see a pillar's carved capital: The cloister · A Bible on the capitals.
