In the chapter's old book of the choir there's one line, in old Catalan, that sums up this choir: "Anglada was sent … to Bruges to buy oak for the said choir." Anglada is Pere Sanglada, the Barcelona sculptor in charge. He travelled all the way to Flanders to choose the wood himself.

[warmly] You're in the canons' choir, the cadirat, the choir stalls, set in the middle of the nave, in front of the crypt. Here the canons sang the hours, enclosed in the middle of the church.

Work began in the 1390s, under Bishop Ramon d'Escales. Sanglada directed the upper stalls, carved mainly in Flemish oak, and he didn't carve them alone. The Catalan encyclopedia's art history calls the stalls, in essence, a team effort, with a workshop of carvers around him. On the fifth of December 1399 they were formally declared finished.

Sanglada's hand turns up elsewhere in this church. He carved the alabaster figure of Sant Oleguer, the bishop who lies under the Lepanto crucifix near the main door.

The lower row of seats came half a century later. A carver called Macià Bonafè won that job, against a rival, Joan Claperós.

[pause] Now look at the upper row, the tall seats at the back. Follow the carving on the seat backs and along the armrests, one seat to the next. That is the work of Sanglada's team, in oak that came from Bruges.

If you're on the full tour, find a seat whose folding ledge is raised, and look at the carving underneath: The choir · Extra · Under the canons' seats. Otherwise, the next main stop is above the seats, at the row of painted shields: The choir · Europe's most exclusive club.
