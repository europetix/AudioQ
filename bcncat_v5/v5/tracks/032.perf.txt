[wry] This chapel is older than the cathedral it now belongs to. Bishop Arnau de Gurb founded it, and it was finished in 1268, thirty years before the Gothic works began.

It's often called the last surviving piece of the Romanesque cathedral. It isn't. It was part of the bishop's palace, as his own chapel, and joined the cathedral's buildings only in 1431, when the cloister reached it. And Santa Llúcia came later. It was first dedicated to Our Lady and all the holy virgins.

[curious] If the door is open, look along its single nave, under a plain, slightly pointed barrel vault. The founder lies inside, his figure on a slab over four columns. When his tomb was opened, it held the top of a gilded crozier, and his burial silks. Those silks are now scattered among museums in Madrid, Granada, Cleveland and New York.

The chapel's other door opens onto the street, Carrer de Santa Llúcia. Its capitals show the Annunciation, the Visitation, and two animals facing each other. The Catalan encyclopedia's Romanesque survey gives its verdict on that carving, in Catalan: "molt mediocre", very mediocre.

[warmly] On her day, the thirteenth of December, people come here to venerate her relics. She protects eyesight. And every December since 1786, a Christmas fair named after her has filled the square outside.

If you're on the full tour, look for the room called Jewish memory, in the old archive: The cloister · Extra · Jewish memory. Otherwise, the next main stop is the Chapter Hall museum, next to this chapel. Go in and find the painting of the dead Christ across his mother's lap: The Chapter Hall · The saint in spectacles.
