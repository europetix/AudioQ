[warmly] When Gaudí came here, this was a bald hill. The Catalan heritage service describes the chosen site as one "with almost no vegetation", stony and very uneven. Most of the green you see came later.

Below lay a city that already had "over half a million inhabitants", as the park's history puts it. The old town of Gràcia, just below the park, had been joined to Barcelona in 1897.

[pause] At the foot of the stairway you heard that one more rule waited up here. The park's history says the height and placement of the houses were set "so that they could not block the view of the sea". The city history museum's guide adds the sunlight. No house was allowed to block the view, or the sun.

The square was made for looking. The park still calls it "A viewpoint over the city". In the city history museum's story for schoolchildren, told in Catalan, people would come up to the central square "to take the fresh air and watch the city".

[curious] So do as they planned. Spain's tourist board calls this one of the best views of the city, "from which you can even see as far as the sea". Look out past the roofs to the horizon, for the flat line of the Mediterranean.

On a clear day, in the Catalan Tourist Board's words, the view is "framed by the towers of the Olympic Village and the blue Mediterranean": two tall towers by the sea.

[wry] And the buyers this view was protected for? Most of them never came. The city history museum's guide says the bourgeoisie "preferred the central Eixample", or houses in Sant Gervasi and on the new estate at Tibidabo, where the building rules were looser. Few wanted a hill this hard to reach.

[lightly] The rules kept this view for buyers who never came. It ended up belonging to everyone.

Stay at the bench, and look up at the hill above the park, with three crosses on its top: The Nature Square · Three crosses instead of a chapel.
