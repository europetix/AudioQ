When Gaudí came here, this was a bald hill. The Catalan heritage service describes the chosen site as one "with almost no vegetation", stony and very uneven. Most of the green you see came later.

Below lay a city that already had "over half a million inhabitants". The old town of Gràcia, just below the park, had been joined to Barcelona in 1897.

At the foot of the stairway you heard that one more rule waited up here. The park's history says the height and placement of the houses were set "so that they could not block the view of the sea". The city's history museum, in its guide, adds the sunlight. No house was allowed to block the view, or the sun.

The square was made for looking. The park still calls it "A viewpoint over the city". In the story the city's history museum tells schoolchildren, in Catalan, people would come up to the central square "to take the fresh air and watch the city".

So do as they planned. Spain's tourist board calls the park one of the best views of the city. Look out over the roofs, as far as the day allows. On a clear day, the same board says, "you can even see as far as the sea": find its flat line on the horizon.

The Catalan Tourist Board says the view is "framed by the towers of the Olympic Village and the blue Mediterranean": two tall towers by the sea.

And the buyers this view was protected for? Most of them went elsewhere. The museum's guide says the bourgeoisie "preferred the central Eixample", or houses in Sant Gervasi, or the new estate at Tibidabo, where the building rules were looser. Few wanted a hill this hard to reach.

The rules kept this view for buyers who never came. It ended up belonging to everyone.

Stay at the bench, but turn away from the city, and look up at the hill above the park, with three crosses on its top: The Nature Square · Three crosses instead of a chapel.
