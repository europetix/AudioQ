This is a bench, a railing and a drain at once. The park calls it an undulating bench of broken tile, "acting as a balustrade". Barcelona's water museum adds that it "has a water collection system". Even the bench catches the rain. And this whole square is the roof of the hall of columns.

Who made it? The park's pages credit Jujol alone. Other sources credit both men. Gaudí shaped the bench. Its skin of broken tiles is largely Jujol's work, the same young assistant who coloured the suns in the hall below you.

It's about a hundred metres long, by the measure of the conservators who restored it. It was made from prefabricated modules, sections like a kit, and then dressed in tile. It was finished in 1914, the year the works on the estate stopped.

Now look closely along the top of the backrest. Bassegoda described "incisions and colors made by Josep Maria Jujol" there, and along a line halfway up the back. Find a mark scratched into the tile. Among Jujol's marks are prayers to the Virgin Mary, engraved into the ceramic. The conservators note that its inner face is ceramic and porcelain, its outer face glass.

There's a favourite story about this bench, and even local television has told it. Gaudí, it goes, sat a workman, naked, in wet plaster to get the curve. It's a great story, and nobody has ever found proof of it.

What is true: the backrest curves to fit your back. Lean into it.

Conservators have come back again and again since a major restoration around 1990. Once, every backrest was photographed, one by one, so the repairs would stay true to the original.

If you're on the full tour, there's a short Extra to play here on the bench, about the words hidden in it: The Nature Square · Extra · Words in the tiles. Otherwise, the next main stop is right here. Stand at the bench and look out over the city: The Nature Square · The view.
