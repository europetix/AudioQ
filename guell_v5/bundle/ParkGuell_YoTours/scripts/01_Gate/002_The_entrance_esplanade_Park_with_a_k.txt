Güell's estate was never meant to be open to the city. The city's history museum, in its guide to Gaudí's Barcelona, describes it in Catalan as "a private estate, closed in by a wall, with a porter's service". This is that wall. The two gatehouses of the porter's lodge stand either side of the gate.

Look along it. In the park's own words, it's "rustic stone topped with ceramic tiling and medallions bearing the name of Park Güell". Those medallions are the first clue to what Güell had in mind.

Find one of them and read it. Park, with a k. Güell spelt it in English on purpose. In the park's own history, he wanted to recreate the British residential parks: an English word for an English idea, set into a Catalan wall.

The same museum's dossier for schools puts the idea in a single line. The Park "began to be built in 1900 as a garden city". A garden city on a hill above Barcelona, with a wall round it and a porter at the door.

The hill wasn't empty of history, even then. Next to this gate, outside the park, is an old spring, the Font del Carbó. Around it, in 1875, the first modest building on this hillside began, a quarter of a century before Güell.

So before you climb a step, the place tells you what it hoped to be: a British idea, walled and guarded, above Barcelona.

Now walk over to the gates themselves, the iron gates in the middle of the wall. Stand to one side, out of the way of people leaving, and look at their ironwork: The Carrer d'Olot gate · A glove shop window.
