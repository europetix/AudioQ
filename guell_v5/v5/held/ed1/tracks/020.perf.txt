[warmly] The park's own entry for this square opens with five plain words: "A viewpoint over the city."

The rules for buyers, as the city's history museum sums them up, said in Catalan that a buyer "may not block the view of the sea". This view was written into the contract.

When Gaudí came here, this was a bald hill. The Catalan heritage service describes a site "with almost no vegetation", stony and very uneven. Everything green you see came later. Barcelona then had over half a million people, and the city has been climbing the hill ever since.

[curious] The park's highest point, a hundred and eighty-two metres above the sea, is the hill of the three crosses, the Turó de les Tres Creus. The park says it was once called "Ore Hill or the Hill of Mines and Caves". A chapel for the estate was meant to stand up there. On a plan of 1903 its outline survives, labelled "Capelya". That same year the architect Bonaventura Bassegoda wrote in a Barcelona newspaper, in Catalan, that the chapel "would protect the whole colony, looking down on it".

[pause] It was never built. Instead, the park says, three stone crosses went up, "which simulated the Calvary". Where the chapel should have been, three crosses.

The next stop is the Portico of the Washerwoman. Find the old iron door at the edge of the square that once led into the gardens of Casa Larrard. Follow the path beyond it, through the pines, to a portico of stone shaped like a breaking wave: The Portico of the Washerwoman · The wave.
