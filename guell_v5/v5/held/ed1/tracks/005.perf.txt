[curious] Before any house, Gaudí built the roads. Work began in 1900 with three kilometres of road to link the plots. Within three years the first viaducts were up.

On the fourth of January 1903, Gaudí led a party of Catalan architects round the works, "on Mr Güell's behalf", as the Gaudí scholar Joan Bassegoda tells it in Catalan. At the top of the hill one of them, Salvador Sellés, read out a description of the works, "checked beforehand by Gaudí". It's the nearest thing we have to Gaudí's own account of the park.

[conspiratorial] Sellés described the caves on the hill as "shady openings in the ground, as if dug as burrows by some creature of troglodyte times". The party went in by torchlight. Bassegoda adds that the caves were artificial, dug out for stone.

Gaudí planned three viaducts like this, five metres wide, for carriages climbing from the Carrer d'Olot entrance to the top of the estate. The first postcards called them the bottom, middle and top bridge: the Pont de Baix, del Mig and de Dalt. Their sloping columns and vaults are rough stone from the hill itself. Road on top, shelter underneath, and, in the park's words, "buttresses and retaining walls for the mountain".

Now find the leaning columns. This bridge curves, "supported by two rows of inclined columns forming a parabola that includes the central vault". Small columns along the top hold up planters as a railing.

[pause] Why the lean? A university engineering study of this viaduct found the tilt is not just for show. The columns lean because the weight leans, and the forces stay inside the stone.

[warmly] Bridges, made of the hill they hold up.
