[curious] It began with a shop window. At the Paris Exhibition of 1878, Eusebi Güell stopped in front of a display case made for a glove maker, Esteve Comella. The park's own history says the partnership "began when Güell saw a window display that Gaudí had planned" for him.

Güell was born in Barcelona, the son of Joan Güell i Ferrer. He studied there, in France and in England, and he never forgot England. With Ferran Alsina he built up the cloth works that became the Colònia Güell. Then came cement. His Asland company opened some of the first Portland cement works in Catalonia. Güell made the very material modern Barcelona was built from.

He was a city councillor, then a senator, and president of the Centre Català. [amused] And this textile baron also wrote a book on microbiology, in French, published in Paris.

In 1886 Güell asked Gaudí to build his new house, the Palau Güell. In 1900 he gave him this hill to plan. The park's own history says the relationship was "not simply that of an artist and his patron, but a real story of friendship".

The city's history museum sums up his aim in Catalan: the Park "began to be built in 1900 as a garden city".

Now look at the wall. It's rustic stone, topped with ceramic tiling, and set with medallions bearing the park's name. Find one and read it. *Park*, with a k. Güell spelt it in English, after the residential parks of Britain.

[pause] Then look at the gates. Their iron is shaped like palm leaves, and they weren't made for the park at all. They came from Casa Vicens, a house Gaudí had built years earlier.

[warmly] So even the gate is a transplant, and the name on the wall is English. Before you climb a step, the place tells you what it hoped to be: a British idea, built by Catalans, on a bare hill above Barcelona.

Now turn to the two gatehouses either side of you and stand between them, where you can see both roofs: the tall tower with its cross, and the mushroom: The entrance esplanade · Two gatehouses.
