[curious] This garden was meant to be somebody's house. The park says so plainly: what we now call the Austria Gardens "was one of the zones to be used as plots in the estate". Remember the sixty plots? This is what one of them looks like with no house on it.

When the estate became a public park, the city used this ground as a municipal plant nursery. Its name, the Jardins d'Àustria, came from "a donation of trees from Austria in 1977".

[pause] From the centre of these gardens, the park says, "the two houses that were built in Eusebi Güell's time can be seen". Two houses, from all those plots.

One belonged to the estate's first buyer. On 24 July 1902, the Gaudí scholar Joan Bassegoda records, a plot was sold to Martí Trias i Domènech, the Güell family's lawyer. His house, by the architect Juli Batllevell, was built between 1903 and 1906. In the park's words, it "continues to be a private residence and is still owned by the same family": the descendants of the lawyer and his wife, Anna Maxenchs. The city, though, has moved to buy it compulsorily. For now, the path from Casa Trias up to the top viaduct is closed for works.

[warmly] Look across the gardens and picture a house here among the trees, with its own garden around it.

Casa Trias is a private home, so we leave it in peace. The other house you can see from here has a stranger story.

Stay in the gardens and find, among the trees, the pink house with the pointed tower: The Austria Gardens · The house Gaudí didn't design.
