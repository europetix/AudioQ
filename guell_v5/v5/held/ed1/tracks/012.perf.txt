[curious] Each of these columns hides something down its middle. The scholar María-Belén García Ávila described how they were made. Ready-made moulds were filled, as she wrote in Spanish, with "a concrete made of lime mortar with rubble and stones". A wooden roller stood upright in the centre. When the concrete set, the roller was pulled out. And the hole it left "served as a drainage conduit to the cistern".

So these are drainpipes. Eighty-six columns hold up this hall, Greek in style, with the outer row leaning inwards against the rules of classical building. They were built as plumbing too.

Follow the rain. The great square above has a sandy surface, which, in the words of the La Pedrera foundation, "allows water to filter down into the cistern below". The rain soaks through the square, gathers in the hollows of the little domes in the ceiling, and runs down inside the columns. In the park's words, a conduit "collects the rainwater that filters down from the square, sending it on to an underground tank". A large one, under your feet.

Barcelona's water museum says the tank "was built to supply water to the park and the houses in the garden city", and to water its gardens. And when it was full, the overflow came out of the dragon's mouth.

The Gaudí scholar Joan Bassegoda called the cistern "an extraordinary architectural space, which is totally unknown to the public". It isn't open to visitors. In March 2026 the city announced a plan to switch Gaudí's rain system back on for watering the gardens, including the tank under this hall, long out of use.

[warmly] Now look down the wider aisle you're facing. Where columns were left out, the park says, the gaps "simulate three naves, like a great church". Then look at the column feet, at their white skirts of trencadís, broken-tile mosaic. García Ávila notes that this is the base "to which Gaudí applied white trencadís".

[pause] A Greek temple, a church nave, and underneath it all, a working drain.
