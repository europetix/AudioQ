[curious] The most famous man who ever lived on this estate lived in a house he didn't design.

The house began as an advertisement. The works contractor, Josep Pardo i Casanovas, put it up as a show house, a model home to tempt buyers. In the park's words, it was "built according to the plans of Gaudí's friend and collaborator, Francesc Berenguer i Mestres". The Sagrada Família's pages on the house call him, in Spanish, Gaudí's "friend and right hand".

But Berenguer had no architect's title. So, the same pages explain, Gaudí signed the plans. The Gaudí scholar Joan Bassegoda put it in Catalan: "plans by Francesc Berenguer, signed Gaudí". [pause] So if anyone tells you that Gaudí designed his own house here, it isn't so. Gaudí signed it. Berenguer drew it.

No other buyer ever came. In 1906 the Gaudí family bought it themselves, and Gaudí moved in with his father, Francesc Gaudí i Serra, and his niece, Rosa Egea i Gaudí.

[quietly] His father died that October. His niece died six years later. Gaudí stayed on alone, until 1925, a year before his own death, when he moved into his workshop at the Sagrada Família. Nearly twenty years in this house, as the park's historians count it. Bassegoda wrote of a glazed window here that looks out to the Calvary on the hilltop, "which Gaudí wanted always to have in view".

Later, a couple who admired him, the Chiappos, made it known as the Casa Gaudí. It has been a museum, the Casa Museu Gaudí, since 1963. Its garden holds iron railings from Casa Vicens and Casa Milà: salvaged Gaudí, like the palm-leaf gates at the entrance, which came from that same Casa Vicens.

[warmly] Now find it among the trees: the pink stucco walls and the pointed tower. It's a museum today, and this guide stays outside. Gaudí's home, Berenguer's design, and Gaudí's name on the plans.
