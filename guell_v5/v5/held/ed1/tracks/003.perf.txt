[curious] The most photographed roofs in the park are made of rejects. A restoration study found the broken tile came, in Spanish, "mostly from surplus and defective stock from ceramic factories". Cheap, and easy to fit to curves.

Gaudí didn't invent *trencadís*, this broken-tile mosaic. Catalans and Valencians had dressed walls in coloured tile since the Middle Ages, and Mudéjar builders set broken ceramic into them. Gaudí first used it in the 1880s and made it his signature.

The park calls the pair the Porter's Lodge. One was the porter's office, "with a waiting room and telephone booth". The other was the caretaker's home, "a modest dwelling", now the city history museum's Casa del Guarda.

Look up at the roofs. The tall tower is a hyperbolic paraboloid that narrows as it rises, topped by a four-armed cross. The other house is oval, and its flue ends in what the restoration study calls "the mushroom-shaped hat". Yet the mushroom seems to have been an afterthought: the plans Güell and Gaudí signed in 1904 don't show it.

[pause] The cross isn't the first one. The original was destroyed in 1936, at the start of the Civil War. The one put up after the war looked quite different. Today's is a copy, made from photographs of the original.

[lightly] Tour guides love to say these are the gingerbread house from Hansel and Gretel. A Palau de la Música programme notes the opera was staged at the Liceu in 1901, in a Catalan version by the poet Joan Maragall, a friend of both men. Did Gaudí have it in mind? Nobody can show it.

[warmly] Factory seconds, a fairy tale, a copied cross.

If you go inside the Casa del Guarda, the caretaker's house with the mushroom on its roof, there's a short Extra to play at its door. The next main stop is up among Gaudí's first roads. Go to the Marianao entrance, the visitors' gate on the Carrer d'Olot side, without going out, and take the steps and ramps from just inside it up to the Low Viaduct, the Pont de Baix, and stop on it: The Low Viaduct · Bridges made of the hill.
