[warmly] You're sitting on a bench, a railing and a drain at once. The park calls it an undulating bench of broken tile, "acting as a balustrade". The Museu de les Aigües, a museum of water, adds that it "has a water collection system". Even the bench catches the rain.

Who made it? The park's pages credit Jujol alone. Other sources credit both men, and that's the honest version. Gaudí shaped the bench. Its skin of broken tiles is largely the work of his young assistant, Josep Maria Jujol. He's the same man who coloured the suns on the ceiling of the hall below you.

It's about a hundred metres long, by the measure of the conservators who restored it. It was made from prefabricated modules, sections like a kit, and then dressed in tile. It was finished in 1914, the year the works on the estate stopped.

[curious] Now look closely along the top of the backrest. The Gaudí scholar Joan Bassegoda described "incisions and colors made by Josep Maria Jujol" there, and along a line halfway up the back. Find a mark scratched into the tile. Among Jujol's marks are prayers to the Virgin Mary, engraved into the ceramic. The conservators note that the inner face is ceramic and porcelain, and the outer face pieces of glass.

[amused] There's a story that tour guides love, and even local television has told it. Gaudí, it goes, sat a workman, naked, in wet plaster to get the curve. It's a great story, and nobody has ever found proof of it.

What is true: the backrest curves to fit your back. Lean into it.

Conservators have come back again and again since a major restoration around 1990. Once, every backrest was photographed, one by one, so the repairs would stay true to the original.

Gaudí's curve, Jujol's colour, and the rain running through both.

There's a short Extra you can play here, sitting on the bench: the words hidden in it, and how much of it is original. The next main stop is at the parapet, where the bench looks out over the city: The Nature Square · The view.
