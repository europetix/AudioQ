So: a bald hilltop, bought by Güell in 1899, and a plan.

The park's history counts "some 60 triangular-shaped plots". Each was to have, in the words of a restoration study, "sewerage, water, gas, electricity and telephone". A telephone, on a bare hill, at the start of the twentieth century. The plans promised shared services too. The city's history museum lists them: "a porter's lodge, a market, a central square, a chapel". You've seen the lodge. The market and the square are up the stairway.

[pause] Then came the rules. You could build on only a sixth of your plot. The rest stayed garden. The museum's notes for older pupils add more, in Catalan. No industry was allowed, and nothing that "could disturb the neighbours' peace". Every buyer had to pay a fee for the upkeep of the shared park.

[amused] The museum's story for schoolchildren goes further. A buyer "will have to pay a fine of 50 pesetas if he cuts down or uproots a tree". And his plans had to be "supervised and approved by Güell himself". One more rule, about the view, waits for you up on the square.

The buyers mostly never came. The first plot went to Güell's friend, the lawyer Martí Trias. A show house went up to tempt others. Only two houses were ever built for the estate. The park's historians blame poor public transport, and rules "designed so that the architecture would blend into nature". Those rules, as a reviewer sums them up, "worked against the project". The park's history is blunt: "A lack of buyers led to the works being abandoned in 1914."

Sixty plots. Two houses.

Ahead of you is the great stairway, with its dragon halfway up. One thing before the check: past this point the route runs one way, and you can't come back this way. Once you're through, stop at the foot of the first flight, out of the way of the steps: The Dragon Stairway · A bus shelter before buses.
