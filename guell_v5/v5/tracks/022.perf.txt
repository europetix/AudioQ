Each column in this hall, the Hypostyle Room, hides something down its middle. The scholar María-Belén García Ávila described how they were made. Ready-made moulds were filled, as she wrote in Spanish, with "a concrete made of lime mortar with rubble and stones". A wooden roller stood upright in the centre. When the concrete set, the roller was pulled out. And the hole it left "served as a drainage conduit to the cistern".

So all eighty-six of these Greek columns, the leaning outer row included, are drainpipes. They were built as plumbing too.

Follow the rain. The great square above has a sandy surface, which, in the words of the La Pedrera foundation, "allows water to filter down into the cistern below". The rain soaks through the square and runs down inside the columns. The bench sits on top of the colonnade along the front. There, the park describes a conduit that "collects the rainwater that filters down from the square, sending it on to an underground tank". A large one, under your feet.

Barcelona's water museum says the tank "was built to supply water to the park and the houses in the garden city". And when it was full, the overflow came out of the dragon's mouth.

The Gaudí scholar Joan Bassegoda called the cistern "an extraordinary architectural space, which is totally unknown to the public". It isn't open to visitors. In 2026 the city announced that Gaudí's old rain tanks, this one included, would be restored, so that the park's rainwater can be collected and used again.

[warmly] Now look down the wider aisle you're facing. Where columns were left out, the park says, the gaps "simulate three naves, like a great church". Then look at the column feet, at their white skirts of broken tile.

Look up at the ceiling of little domes, and stand under one of them, away from the big round discs: The Hypostyle Room · The young man on the ceiling.
