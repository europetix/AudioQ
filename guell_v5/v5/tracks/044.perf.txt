[curious] The estate nobody wanted to buy became a place almost everybody wanted to see. Before the limits came in, the city estimated about nine million visits a year to the heart of this park. Nine million, onto the steps, the square and the bench you've just seen. Look across at the two gatehouses, and the crowd between them.

So the city started counting. "Access to Park Güell has been regulated since 2013 to prevent tourist overcrowding," the park says. One of its aims is that the park stays "an urban park, a place for the community".

The neighbours were part of the story from the start. When charging began, in October 2013, a Catalan news agency reported that neighbours had been complaining about the crowds. At one point, neighbourhood associations even signed a manifesto against closing the park off. Two years later, the head of one residents' association told a local broadcaster, in Catalan: "For years I couldn't photograph the iguana." He meant the dragon.

Since 2020 the controls have covered the whole twelve hectares of the historic park.

People who live in the city, and the neighbours above all, can still come in free.

In 2025 the park counted more than four million paying visitors, and more than half a million free visits by local residents.

[pause] Where does the money from all those visits go? The park's press release on one big round of works said they "have been funded by the return from the park's tourist regulation".

[warmly] And the repairs are slow, patient handwork. You've seen it today: the Suns taken down in pieces and put back, the bench photographed backrest by backrest, the dragon's mended foot.

[wry] Barcelona still employs people to glue Gaudí back together, piece by piece.
