[wry] In 2015 a zoologist looked hard at this creature and decided to settle the matter. Bayard Brattstrom, an emeritus professor of zoology, wrote to the New York Review of Books. To his eye, it appears to be "either a garden lizard or the North African rainbow lizard". And he was firm on one point: "It is not a crocodile as some have said."

The critic Martin Filler wrote the reply. Gaudí's power, he said, lay in "his inventive imagination, rather than any strict fidelity to this or that prototype". In other words, stop counting the scales.

Bassegoda looked for meaning instead of species. An English press note quotes him: Gaudí's dragons "are taken from mythology and history, and reflect the ideas of Count Güell". For Bassegoda, those ideas were Catalanism, mythology and religion.

And it is not alone. By one photographer's count, Barcelona has nearly four hundred dragons, on its buildings, fountains and parks.

[pause] Now look back down at it from here. See how the tiles follow the body, the spine, the open mouth. A dragon to most visitors, a lizard to a zoologist, and a dragon or salamander to the park.

Climb to the top of the stairway, where the last flight runs in under the hall of columns. Stop by the curved stone bench there, shaped like a little Greek theatre, and look for a tripod that seems made of snakes: The Dragon Stairway · Delphi?
