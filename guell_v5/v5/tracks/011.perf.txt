[curious] This Greek temple was built to be a market. The park describes it modestly, as a hall "which could be used as a market place for the estate". Its Catalan name is the Sala Hipòstila, the hall of columns. The rules for the buyers of the sixty plots promised them shared services, and a market was one of them. This is it.

The park gives the number exactly: "86 striated columns inspired in the Doric order". Guidebooks love to round that up to a hundred. [lightly] The real figure is eighty-six, and the park has counted.

They are Greek columns, done Gaudí's way. Each is fluted, like a temple column, and each stands about six metres tall. Thick for their height, they are squat on purpose.

[pause] Then comes the rule-breaking. In the park's words, "The outermost ones slope in an undulating movement clearly contrary to the rules of classical composition." The scholar María-Belén García Ávila, writing in Spanish in 2023, put it more bluntly: they "lean strongly into the hall". They work like buttresses, pressing in against the weight above them.

The front of this hall has had troubles of its own. When the hall was studied in 1986, a report found its front settling. Part of it stands on rock, and part of it does not.

This hall went up in the busiest years of the works, when the estate still expected its buyers to arrive. A market, under a temple's roof, for families who never came.

[warmly] Now find a spot where you can see the outer row side-on. Follow one column from its foot to its top, and then the next one along. The tilt is real, and it's deliberate. A market hall in Greek dress, quietly breaking the Greek rules.

Walk in among the columns. Stop where you can see down one of the wider aisles, where columns were left out: The Hypostyle Room · Columns that are drainpipes.
