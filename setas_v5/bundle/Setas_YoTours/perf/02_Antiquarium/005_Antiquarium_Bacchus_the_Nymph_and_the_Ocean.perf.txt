[lightly] Wine, water and the open sea. The names in this part of the Antiquarium sound like the guest list for a Roman dinner.

The first is the Casa de Baco, the House of Bacchus. Bacchus was the Roman god of wine. Close by is the Casa de la Ninfa, the House of the Nymph. In ancient myth, nymphs were the spirits of springs, rivers and woods.

Then comes the Patio del Océano, the Ocean Courtyard, with a three-metre mural. And there is a place with a stranger name, the Hospitium de los Delfines, the Hospitium of the Dolphins. Hospitium is a Latin word for hospitality, or for taking in guests. It shares its root with our own word hospitality.

[pause] What exactly went on in each of these places is a question for the labels, not for guesswork. Read them as you go. They tell you what each floor or wall shows, and whether you're looking at an original or a replica.

These houses were not frozen in one moment. The Roman city kept changing here for centuries, rebuilt in phase after phase, long after its first mosaics were laid. [quietly] Then the Roman layers end. The next one you'll see belongs to Moorish Seville, centuries later and much nearer the street.

Walk on to the Moorish house, the casa almohade, and look for its label. The next stop in the Antiquarium is The Almohad house.
