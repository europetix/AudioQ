[curious] Some of the oldest things found under this square were Roman tanks, filled with fish remains. To see why, it helps to face the river.

With the Giralda on your left, you're facing west. That is the direction of the Guadalquivir, the river that runs past the old city on its way to the Atlantic. Whether or not the water shows between the roofs from where you stand, it's out there.

[warmly] Across the river, on its far bank, lie the rooftops of Triana. Its trade for centuries was pottery: the regional tourism office calls it the district's most important activity. And beyond it all rises the Aljarafe, the high ground west of the city.

For the Romans, this city was Hispalis, and the river made it a port. Ships came up the river from the sea. One academic study by a University of Seville archaeologist is titled "Hispalis, Roman port of Baetica". Baetica was the Roman province of the south.

[pause] Now the find. Under this square, archaeologists uncovered a fish-salting works from the first century. A corridor, floored with small Roman bricks, led to five tanks. Four have been excavated. When the works was abandoned, they were filled with rubbish, mostly compacted fish remains. Judging by that fill, they went out of use somewhere between the years 70 and 120.

Was garum made here, the famous Roman fish sauce? It's tempting to say so. The careful answer is that this was the kind of works that produced salted fish and fish sauces like garum. Either way, it belonged to a city that lived by its river.

[quietly] Potters across the water, fish salters under your feet. Long before the convent, the market or the parasols, the river had already put this square to *work*.
