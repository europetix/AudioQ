[curious] The architect of the Setas has said that one of his starting points was not a mushroom at all. It was the cathedral, less than a kilometre from where you stand.

That way is south. The tall tower is the Giralda, and the cathedral stands beside it.

The Giralda began as a minaret. Work on it started in 1184, for the Great Mosque of the Almohads, the same Moorish Seville as a house found under this square. When King Fernando the Third took the city in 1248, the mosque became the cathedral, and the minaret became its bell tower. [pause] [reverent] It rises close to a hundred metres to the tip of its weathervane. The Setas are close to thirty metres high. The tower is more than three times as tall.

The cathedral itself came later. In 1401 the cathedral chapter voted to pull down the old mosque and build a new church. What they built is vast. UNESCO calls it the largest Gothic building in Europe, and lists it as World Heritage, together with the Alcázar and the Archive of the Indies.

Now, the link. Mayer has spoken of the cathedral's stone vaulting as one of his references. The Museum of Modern Art in New York keeps a model of the Setas, and it describes the design as a democratic "cathedral without walls".

[lightly] Take a moment with that idea. Inside the cathedral, stone vaults close out the sky. Up here the vaults are timber, and the sky comes straight in.

[warmly] Two cathedrals, then, centuries apart. One of stone, with walls. One of wood, without.

Now find a spot on the walkway facing west, toward the river: stand with the Giralda on your left. The next stop is The river and the Aljarafe.
