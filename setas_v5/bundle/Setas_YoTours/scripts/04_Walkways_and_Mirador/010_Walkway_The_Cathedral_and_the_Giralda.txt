One of the ideas behind the Setas is in view from here. It's the cathedral, less than a kilometre from where you stand.

That way is south. The tall tower is the Giralda, and the cathedral stands beside it.

The Giralda began as a minaret. Work on it started in 1184, for the Great Mosque of the Almohads, the Moorish rulers of the city. This was the same Moorish Seville whose remains lie under this square. When King Fernando the Third took the city in 1248, the mosque became the cathedral, and the minaret became its bell tower. It rises close to a hundred metres to the tip of its weathervane. The Setas are close to thirty metres high. The tower is more than three times as tall.

That weathervane has a name, the Giraldillo. It's a bronze figure of Faith, holding a banner and a palm, cast in the 1560s. From here it looks tiny. In fact it's about three and a half metres tall. It stands for the triumph of Christianity over Islam, on top of a tower that began as a minaret.

The cathedral itself came later. Early in the 1400s, the cathedral's clergy voted to pull down the old mosque and build a new church. What they built is vast. UNESCO calls it the largest Gothic building in Europe, and lists it as World Heritage, together with the Alcázar and the Archive of the Indies.

Now, the link. The Museum of Modern Art in New York keeps a model of the Setas. The museum describes the design as a democratic "cathedral without walls".

Inside the cathedral, stone vaults close out the sky. Up here the vaults are timber, and the sky comes straight in.

Two cathedrals, then, centuries apart. One of stone, with walls. One of wood, without.

Now find a spot on the walkway facing west, toward the river: stand with the Giralda on your left. The next stop is The river, Triana and the Aljarafe.
