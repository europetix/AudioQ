Some of the oldest things ever found under this square were Roman tanks, still packed with fish. To see why, it helps to face the river.

With the Giralda on your left, you're facing west. That is the direction of the Guadalquivir, the river that runs past the old city on its way to the Atlantic. Whether or not the water shows between the roofs from where you stand, it's out there. And beyond it lies the Aljarafe, the high ground west of the city.

For the Romans, this city was Hispalis, and the river made it a port. Ships came up the river from the sea. One academic study by a University of Seville archaeologist is simply titled "Hispalis, Roman port of Baetica". Baetica was the Roman province of the south.

Now the find. Under this square, archaeologists uncovered a fish-salting works from the first century AD. A long corridor, floored with small Roman bricks, led to five tanks, each about three metres across. When they were dug out, the tanks were still full of compacted fish remains. Judging by what filled them, they went out of use somewhere between 70 and 120 AD.

Was garum made here, the famous Roman fish sauce? It's tempting to say so. The careful answer is that this was the kind of works that produced salted fish and fish sauces like garum. Either way, it belonged to a city that lived by its river.

Long before the convent, the market or the parasols, the river had already put this square to work.
