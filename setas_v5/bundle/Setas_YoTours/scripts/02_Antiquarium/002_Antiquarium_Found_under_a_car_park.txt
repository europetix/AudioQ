In the 1990s, Seville planned a car park under this square. What it got instead was the Romans.

The plan was to remodel the Plaza de la Encarnación with an underground car park. But the ground here kept giving up its past. First came a small test dig in 1991, paid for by the market traders. Then a rescue dig in 1998, when the car park was planned. Then the big excavation, from 2003 to 2009, its archaeology directed by Fernando Amores. The car park was never built. What they uncovered is what you're about to walk through.

This is the Antiquarium, the museum of the remains under the Setas. It opened together with the Setas, and it covers nearly five thousand square metres. The city calls it Seville's most important surviving Roman site.

The remains span some twelve centuries. The oldest go back to the emperor Tiberius, in the first century. The latest are from the 1200s. The Roman houses lay deepest. A house from Moorish Seville came much later, and much nearer the surface.

Remains of a Roman fish-salting works were found here too. Their story comes later, when you can look toward the river.

The space was designed as carefully as the dig. Its architect, Felipe Palomino, wanted a membrane wrapped around the ruins, and he made it of glass. The glass can be clear, or frosted, or anything in between. Almost no daylight reaches down here. There is just one area of direct natural light. So the lighting comes in two separate systems, one for the architecture and one for the remains.

Walkways carry you over the excavation, with remains underfoot and behind glass. Read the labels as you go. This was a neighbourhood, where people lived and worked for century after century.

A car park that was never built became a window into Roman and Moorish Seville.

Walk on along the walkway in the Antiquarium, and play the next stop at the first good view down into the remains: Seville stacked up.
