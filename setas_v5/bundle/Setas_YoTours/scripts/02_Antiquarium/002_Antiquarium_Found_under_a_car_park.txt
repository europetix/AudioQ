In the 1990s, Seville wanted somewhere to park. It dug under this square, and it found the Romans.

The plan was to remodel the Plaza de la Encarnación with an underground car park. The digging ran into Roman Seville, and the car park was never built. Instead, the excavations went on, on and off, for more than a decade, the later ones directed by the archaeologist Fernando Amores. What they uncovered is what you're about to walk through.

This is the Antiquarium, the museum of the remains under the Setas. It opened together with the Setas on 27 March 2011, and it covers nearly five thousand square metres. The city calls it Seville's most important surviving Roman site. It is run by Seville City Council.

What makes it unusual is the span of time. The remains here cover some 1,200 years of the city's life, from about the time of the emperor Tiberius, in the first century, to the 1200s. In the ground, the Roman houses lay deepest. A house from Moorish Seville came much later, and much nearer the surface. You'll meet both.

Remains of a Roman fish-salting works were found here too. Their story comes later, when you can look toward the river.

Inside, walkways carry you over the excavation, with remains underfoot and behind glass. Take your time, and read the labels as you go. Most of what's here is houses and streets. This was a neighbourhood, where people lived and worked for century after century.

A car park that was never built became a window into Roman and Moorish Seville.

Walk on along the walkway to the first place where you can look down into the excavation and see how deep it goes. The next stop in the Antiquarium is Seville stacked up.
