Wine, couches and leftovers. Keep an eye on the labels as you walk. Some of them read like the plan for a Roman dinner party.

Start with the Casa de Baco, the House of Bacchus, the Roman god of wine. Romans dined lying on couches, in a room called the triclinium. In this house, the main mosaic of the dining room showed the triumph of Bacchus. With him was Ariadne, the princess he married in the myth. The study of these floors says one version, with Ariadne riding in his chariot, was long the most common in Roman southern Spain. When you find the mosaic floor of the Casa de Baco, look at its corners for the drinking vessels.

Then the couch. Look for the Casa del Sigma, the House of the Sigma. Sigma is a Greek letter, and in Roman times it was often written like a C. The name passed to a curved dining couch, where guests lay in a half-circle around the food. If the label speaks of a stibadium, that's the same curved couch.

Now the strangest mosaic of the evening. Hospitium is Latin for hospitality, or for taking in guests. In the Hospitium de los Delfines, the Hospitium of the Dolphins, the study describes a rare motif set in a geometric pattern: an unswept floor. Food scraps, scattered as if a feast has just ended. Which party it was, nobody can know. The study says that's exactly what wakes up your imagination.

Two more names to watch for. The Casa de la Ninfa, the House of the Nymph, a spirit of springs and rivers. And the Patio del Océano, the Ocean Courtyard, where the city's tourism office lists a three-metre mural. Among the mosaics found here, the study counts many sea creatures: dolphins, fish and water birds.

The guests are long gone. Their dinner party was set down in stone.

Keep walking and reading the labels in the Antiquarium. Next, look for a mosaic hung upright on a wall, with two birds at its centre. Play the next stop when you find it, or as you walk on: The two birds.
