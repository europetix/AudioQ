Seville was an important Roman city, yet it has kept surprisingly few Roman mosaics. The best group it has is down here.

The Romans called the city Hispalis. Under this square stood a quarter of its houses, and they were never finished in one go. The archaeologists traced phase after phase of building, over five or six centuries, starting in the first century.

They found forty-seven floors, thirty-three with mosaics. Guadalupe López Monteagudo wrote the specialist study of them. When it came out, only about a dozen were on show. Some lie on the floor where they were found. Others hang upright, as panels. A review of her study puts it plainly: in a city where few have ever been found, these are unmatched for quality and variety.

The house to find is the Casa de la Columna, the House of the Column. Look for its Medusa, the snake-haired Gorgon of Greek myth. In the old stories, a single look from her turned people to stone, until the hero Perseus cut off her head.

So why would anyone want her underfoot? Because that stare could work for you, too. Romans put Medusa's head on their floors as a kind of guard, a face to turn bad luck away. The specialist study calls this one a warning and protective Medusa.

If you find her, notice her setting. She sits inside a geometric pattern. Then look at her face. The same study notes that she looks unhappy. Perhaps she knows how her story ends.

Along the way, the labels give other house names too. The Casa Romana, the Roman House. The Casa de las Basas, the House of the Column Bases. The Casa de la Noria, the House of the Water Wheel. They are modern names, not Roman ones. And the labels tell you which pieces are originals, which are replicas, and which survive only in photographs taken during the dig.

For a city that kept so little of its Roman floors, this is where Hispalis still shows its face.

Walk on through the Roman houses, and play the next stop in the Antiquarium as you go: Bacchus and the dinner party.
