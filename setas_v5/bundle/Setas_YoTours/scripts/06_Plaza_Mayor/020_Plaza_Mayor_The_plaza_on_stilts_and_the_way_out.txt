You've been below the street, and you've been up on the parasols. This square, in the middle, is the part of the Setas that belongs to everyone.

The Plaza Mayor is one of the four layers of the design: under the parasols, open to the sky, and made for events. Its architect, Jürgen Mayer H., hoped the Plaza de la Encarnación would become, in his words, "the new contemporary urban centre".

Mayer went further. He called the whole thing "an urban, democratic, open cathedral that is held together by the people and the life in the center of the city."

Not everyone in Seville agreed with him at first. But a year after the opening, the Setas was a finalist for the European Prize for Urban Public Space.

Look around you. The people were always the point.

Below you, the market carries on. And after sunset, when the Aurora light show is running, the timber above you lights up.

To leave, follow the signs down to the street, by the stairs or the escalators. From there you can visit the market, too. If you're on the full tour, there's one last short Extra for after you leave: The fig trees and the Roman columns.

You started below the street, beside the remains of Roman and Moorish Seville. You climbed to the highest point of the parasols, and you've finished here in the middle, where the city meets.
