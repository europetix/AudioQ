By the lifts, you heard how the old market hall came down in 1973. Its traders were promised a temporary home. Three years, at most. They waited thirty-seven.

Right below you, at street level, is the Mercado de la Encarnación, the Incarnation Market. When the old hall came down, the stallholders moved into makeshift stalls nearby.

The square they left behind became a car park. Then the archaeologists came, and then the architects. Each time, the market waited.

It moved back in at the end of 2010, about three months before the square you're standing on opened above it. The architect had kept its place in the plan. The market went at street level, with this square on top of it, like a roof you can walk on.

Fishmongers and greengrocers still trade down there. A study published by Mercasa, Spain's state food-markets company, calls it one of the oldest municipal markets in Andalusia, and in all of Spain.

And there's a neat symmetry under your feet. Two thousand years ago, the Romans were salting fish on this spot. Today, the fish is sold fresh.
