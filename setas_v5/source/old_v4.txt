About This Tour
WHAT THIS IS
A walking audio tour through one of the most ambitious works of contemporary architecture in
southern Europe — the Setas de Sevilla, locally known as Las Setas (The Mushrooms). Built
2005-2011 on the site of the old Mercado de la Encarnación in Seville's medieval centre,
designed by German architect Jürgen Mayer H., engineered by Arup, constructed by Sacyr. Six
interconnected wooden parasols 28.5 metres tall, 150 metres long, 70 metres wide, made of
approximately 3,400 micro-laminated Finnish pine Kerto LVL modules — marketed as the world's
largest wooden structure. Four levels: the Antiquarium below (Roman and Almohad ruins
discovered during construction in 2003, preserved in place); the food market at ground level; an
elevated public plaza; and a 250-metre rooftop walkway with 360-degree views of central Seville
at 28 metres up. The structure opened to controversy (€102 million final cost vs. €50 million initial
estimate; a trademark dispute over the original Metropol Parasol name) and has since become
one of Seville's most visited attractions. Most travelers visit Seville once. This tour is built so that
one visit feels like enough — and so the memories last.

HOW TO USE IT
Twenty tracks. About 65-70 minutes of audio across the whole visit. Each track is intentionally
short, 3-4 minutes, because the Setas is a rooftop experience first — the view itself deserves
your attention, and the audio is there to give you context between sections, not to compete with
what you're looking at. Plan two hours total for the whole site if you want to do the Antiquarium
plus the walkway in one visit. Plan three hours if you also want to come back for Aurora, the
evening light show. The tour follows the natural visitor route: start at street level, descend into the
Antiquarium archaeology below, return to ground level, ascend through the market and elevated
plaza to the walkway, then back down. Each track opens with a Stop and Look note telling you
where to stand. Pause anytime. Your standard ticket includes two visits within 48 hours — so a
daytime visit and an evening Aurora return cost the same as a single ticket.

Welcome
★ ANCHOR



  STOP & LOOK
  Stand in Plaza de la Encarnación, at the base of the wooden structure. Look up. The canopy
  stretches 150 metres in one direction and 70 in the other, supported on only six concrete columns.
  The ground floor is open - you can walk under it without a ticket. Find a spot in the shade and pause

Welcome. Take a breath.

The thing above you was not supposed to look like this.

In two thousand four, the city of Seville held an architectural competition. The brief was simple -
design something to fill a hole in the middle of the old quarter. The hole had been a market for
seventy years. Then a car park for thirty. Then a building site for seven. Seville needed someone
to close it.

Sixty-five architects from around the world submitted proposals. A jury that included the
Japanese architect Toyo Ito and the Swiss architect Jacques Herzog picked the most daring, the
most expensive, the most controversial of all sixty-five. The winning design was by a German
architect from Berlin named Jürgen Mayer. It called for six gigantic wooden parasols,
twenty-eight metres tall, made of three thousand four hundred individual timber modules glued
together, hovering over the medieval centre of one of the oldest cities in Spain.

Seven years of construction, multiple delays, a hundred and two million euros, and one Roman
archaeological site discovered in the middle of it all later, the parasols opened to the public in
twenty eleven.

Locals immediately renamed them Las Setas. The Mushrooms.

The name stuck so completely that in twenty thirteen, when the city discovered that Mayer
himself had trademarked the original name Metropol Parasol and would charge for its use, the
council officially adopted the people's name. Today the structure is the Setas de Sevilla. The
Mushrooms of Seville. There is no other building in Europe with a more affectionately ridiculous
official name.

Here's how we'll walk it. Twenty tracks. About seventy minutes of audio across the whole
experience - but here's the important thing. You do not need to listen to me while you're up on
the walkway looking at Seville. The view deserves your eyes. The audio is there to give you
context before you go up, and to pull pieces together as you move between the levels. Most of
what's worth seeing here is best experienced in silence.

TRACK 02 / 20

A Convent, a Market, a Car Park, a Hole
CONTEXT



  STOP & LOOK
  Still at street level in Plaza de la Encarnación. Look around the square. The buildings on the
  perimeter are eighteenth and nineteenth century - tile-fronted, four or five storeys, balconied. The
  Setas occupy where the centre of the square used to be open. This was empty ground for nearly forty
  years.


The square has been a hole for most of living memory.

Before that, it was a market. And before that, a convent. And the layers go back further than that
- we'll get to that in a moment.

In the sixteenth century the Augustinian order built a convent here called the Convento de la
Encarnación - the Convent of the Incarnation. That's where the plaza gets its name. The convent
stood for almost three hundred years. It was demolished in the nineteenth century during one of
the great waves of Spanish anticlerical reform, the Mendizábal disentailment of eighteen
thirty-six, when the Spanish state confiscated monastic properties across the country.

What replaced it was a market. In eighteen forty-two, the architect Melchor Cano - a major figure
in nineteenth-century Sevillian architecture - designed the Mercado de la Encarnación. The
market opened with more than four hundred stalls selling fresh produce, meat, fish, and bread. It
became the central food market of the entire city. Sevillanos lived around it, shopped at it, met
there, gossiped there. For one hundred and thirty years, the rhythm of life in this neighbourhood
was the rhythm of the market.

In nineteen seventy-three, the market was demolished.

The reasons were a slow accumulation. The stalls for vegetables and fish had developed serious
structural problems. The Tamarguillo flood of nineteen sixty-one had damaged the foundations.
And the first supermarkets had begun to appear in Seville - the supermarket model that was
about to transform European food shopping was undermining the central market everywhere it
touched.

So the market came down. The traders were moved to temporary facilities nearby - facilities that
were supposed to last three years. They lasted thirty-seven. The site of the original market
became a car park. An underground car park, paving the surface as a flat plaza on top.

The car park was supposed to be temporary too. It lasted from nineteen seventy-three to two
thousand three. Thirty years of a flat asphalt plaza in the middle of one of the most beautiful old
cities in Europe.

TRACK 03 / 20

The 2003 Discovery
CONTEXT



  STOP & LOOK
  Still at ground level. Walk over to the entrance of the Antiquarium - it's on level minus one,
  signposted, accessed from inside the structure on the side facing the eighteenth-century buildings.
  You can see ruins through plate-glass windows before you even buy a ticket. Don't go in yet. Stand at
  the glass.


This is where the project went wrong, and then went right.

The diggers had started clearing the old car park in two thousand three. The plan was
straightforward: remove the existing structure, deepen the foundations, build a new underground
car park, lay a new market on top, and rebuild the square. Nobody on the construction team
expected to find anything historical.

Then someone hit masonry.

The masonry was Roman. A clean line of dressed stone, an early imperial pattern, with a section
of mosaic floor still in place beside it. The construction crew called in the archaeologists. The
archaeologists started digging. What had looked like a quick foundation job turned into one of the
most significant urban archaeological excavations in modern Spanish history.

By the time the digging was done they had uncovered eleven separate areas of remains. Seven
Roman houses with mosaic floors, columns, and wells. Streets between the houses. Industrial
fish-salting tanks - the Romans of southern Spain made an enormous business out of garum, the
fermented fish sauce that was their version of soy sauce. And on top of the Roman city, the
foundations of an Almohad house from the twelfth or thirteenth century, complete with the
housing for a noria, a vertical waterwheel.

The earliest finds dated to the first century before Christ. The latest dated to the thirteenth
century. Roman, Visigothic, early Islamic, and Almohad - over a thousand years of continuous
urban occupation, sitting four to five metres below the surface of a twentieth-century car park.

This is not unusual for Seville. The whole city sits on top of itself. Seville is two thousand four
hundred years old as a settlement. Before the Romans called it Hispalis, the Phoenicians had a
trading post here. Before the Phoenicians, the Tartessians. The historic centre is a sandwich of
urban layers, each one built on the rubble and the foundations of the last.

But the size of what they had found here was significant. And the city had a choice to make.
Demolish the ruins and build the car park as planned. Or rebuild the entire project around the
archaeology, preserving everything in place.

TRACK 04 / 20

The Antiquarium
★★ HERO



  STOP & LOOK
  Buy your Antiquarium ticket - it's separate from the Setas walkway ticket, around two and a half
  euros, free with a Real Alcázar ticket. Take the stairs or lift down to level minus one. You'll come out
  into a low, dim, atmospheric space, with glass walkways running over the ruins. Stand at the first
  walkway and just look down for a minute.


You are now four to five metres below the modern street.

Above you - in the equivalent height of a small two-storey building - is the elevated plaza, the
market, the wooden canopy, the walkway, and the open sky. Below you is the Seville of the
Romans.

The space is enormous. Four thousand eight hundred and seventy-nine square metres of
archaeological hall, plus another five hundred and fifty of approach walkway. Almost five
thousand square metres of preserved ancient city, in place, with the walls and floors arranged
exactly as they were found. The architect of this underground space was Felipe Palomino. He
designed the surrounding membrane - a vast hanging curtain of glass that wraps around the
entire archaeological hall, lit from above and behind, so that the ruins seem to glow softly without
obvious light sources. The effect is deliberately atmospheric. Many visitors describe it as feeling
like a temple, or like the bottom of a slow river.

You can walk above the ruins on raised glass walkways. The total length of walkways inside the
Antiquarium is about four hundred metres. From the walkways you look down onto Roman
mosaic floors, Roman wells, Roman streets, the foundations of Roman houses, the fish-salting
tanks, and the later Almohad house on top.

The temporal span here is unusual. Most ancient sites in Europe represent one civilisation.
Pompeii is Roman. Stonehenge is prehistoric. The Forum in Rome is Roman. But the
Antiquarium spans more than thirteen centuries on a single site. The earliest layers are
first-century before Christ - Republican Roman, before the empire even existed. The Roman
houses you can see are mostly second to fifth century AD. Then there's a gap - Visigothic
remains exist but are less visible to a casual visitor. Then the Almohad house from the twelfth
and thirteenth centuries, when Seville was a major city of the Western Islamic world, capital of an
empire that stretched from southern Spain across North Africa to Senegal.

All of it preserved in place, all of it visible at once, all of it sitting underneath six wooden
mushrooms that locals named at a coffee in twenty eleven.

TRACK 05 / 20

The Mosaic Houses
OBJECT



  STOP & LOOK
  Walk into the main hall of the Antiquarium. Find the Casa de la Columna - it's the largest single
  Roman house on display, with a clear pillared patio at the centre and a mosaic floor running around it.
  Stand at the glass railing.


This is the Casa de la Columna. The House of the Column.

It dates from the fifth century after Christ. By Roman standards that's late - the western Roman
empire was collapsing in this period - but the house was substantial. Look at the pillared patio at
its centre. The marble pedestals are still in place where the columns once stood. The house was
built around this courtyard in the typical Roman pattern - rooms opening off a central open-air
space, with the family living around the courtyard and using it for light, air, and drainage.

The mosaic floor around the patio is the centrepiece. You can see laurel wreath motifs - the
curved garland of leaves that Roman emperors wore as crowns and that athletes wore at the
Olympic games. You can also see diadem motifs - the simple circular crown of bands wrapped
around the head, similar to the laurel in meaning. Both designs were popular in the later Roman
empire, when Roman patrons were increasingly drawing on Greek visual vocabulary to signal
cultural sophistication.

Move on through the hall and you'll find the Casa de Baco. The House of Bacchus, the Roman
god of wine.

This one is smaller but more visually striking. The mosaic floor has a central image of Bacchus,
surrounded by an ornamental border. The corners of the mosaic show elaborate drinking vessels
- kraters and kantharos cups - the kind of fine pottery used at Roman dinner parties for serving
wine. The house belonged to someone who wanted dinner guests to know that wine was being
taken seriously. The Romans of southern Spain produced enormous quantities of wine for the
empire - Hispania Baetica, the Roman province that contained Hispalis, was one of the major
wine exporters of the western Mediterranean. The Casa de Baco is, in a quiet way, a portrait of
that economy.

Both houses are from the late Roman period. Both were destroyed and abandoned - not in a
single catastrophic event, but through the slow urban contraction of the fifth and sixth centuries,
as the western empire fragmented and the population of Hispalis declined. The Visigothic and
early Islamic periods reused some of the foundations but built less densely. By the time the
Almohad period arrived in the twelfth century, parts of the Roman city had been buried for half a
millennium.

TRACK 06 / 20

Medusa, the Kissing Birds, and the Laurel Wreath
OBJECT



  STOP & LOOK
  Continue through the Antiquarium. Find the wall-mounted reconstructed mosaic of two facing birds
  with their beaks touching - that's the kissing birds, the symbol of the museum. Nearby on the floor
  you'll find a circular Medusa mosaic with the snake-haired goddess at the centre. Stand near them.


The kissing birds are the symbol of the Antiquarium.

Originally a floor mosaic, the museum has reconstructed them on a wall so visitors can see them
at eye level. Two birds face each other, beaks touching. The image is simpler than the larger
figural mosaics, almost abstract - flat colours, geometric border, a clear visual gesture. The birds
are doves, or possibly partridges - the species is not certain. The mosaic dates from the third or
fourth century AD.

What it meant in the original house, nobody knows for sure. It could be a domestic decorative
motif. It could refer to a particular Roman myth about devoted bird couples. It could just be
beautiful - the Romans, like every other ancient civilisation, sometimes made beautiful things for
no greater reason than beauty.

The Medusa mosaic is more famous mythologically but less rare as an image.

Medusa was one of the Gorgons - a monster with snakes for hair and a gaze that turned people
to stone. In Greek and Roman myth she was killed by the hero Perseus, who used her severed
head as a weapon, since the petrifying gaze still worked after death. The image of the Medusa
head, the gorgoneion, was used throughout the Roman empire as an apotropaic motif - a symbol
meant to ward off evil. You see Medusa heads on Roman shields, on temple doorways, on
jewellery, and on house floors. Walking over a Medusa mosaic at the entrance to a Roman
house was understood to bring protection from misfortune.

This particular Medusa is laid out on the floor. The face is at the centre, the snakes radiate
outward in a circular border, and a square frame contains the whole composition. The colours
are the standard palette of Roman floor mosaic - black, white, red ochre, terracotta, occasionally
yellow.

The laurel wreath motif you saw in the Casa de la Columna also appears in smaller mosaics
throughout the Antiquarium. The laurel was sacred to Apollo and was used both for athletic
victory and for imperial coronation. By the late Roman period it had become a generic decorative
motif - the equivalent of decorative gilt edging in nineteenth-century European interiors. You see
it everywhere in late Roman house decoration.

TRACK 07 / 20

The Almohad House of the Waterwheel
OBJECT



  STOP & LOOK
  Find the section of the Antiquarium where the masonry style changes. You'll see brick rather than
  dressed stone, smaller and lower walls, and a deep square or circular shaft - the housing for the
  noria, the vertical waterwheel. Stand near it.


This is the Casa de la Noria. The House of the Waterwheel.

It is Almohad, twelfth to thirteenth century - about seven hundred years younger than the Roman
houses next to it.

The Almohads were a Berber dynasty from North Africa who conquered the western Islamic
world in the twelfth century. At their peak they controlled most of Morocco, all of Andalusia, and
parts of what is now Algeria and Tunisia. Their capital alternated between Marrakech and Seville.
Their architectural legacy in Seville is enormous - the Giralda, the cathedral's bell tower, was
originally the minaret of the Almohad great mosque, built in the eleven hundred and eighties. The
Torre del Oro on the river is Almohad. Parts of the Real Alcázar palace are built on Almohad
foundations.

This domestic house is a quieter piece of the same period. It's built in brick rather than the
dressed stone the Romans used. The walls are lower and the rooms smaller. The defining
feature is the housing for the noria - a vertical waterwheel, powered by an animal or by flowing
water, used to raise water from a well or cistern up to the level of a domestic garden or fountain.

Norias were essential to Islamic urban life in Andalusia. The medieval Islamic cities of the Iberian
peninsula were famous across the Mediterranean for the sophistication of their water systems -
aqueducts, cisterns, canals, fountains in private courtyards, public baths. The noria was one of
the technologies that made this possible. Walk through any garden of the Real Alcázar and the
water that runs through the channels was once raised by something like the wheel that used to
turn here.

The house itself was a relatively modest urban residence. Not a palace - just a comfortable home
for a merchant or middle-ranking official in twelfth or thirteenth century Seville. The same kind of
person who, if they had lived a thousand years earlier on the same plot, would have owned the
Casa de Baco above.

The Almohad presence in Seville ended in twelve forty-eight, when the Castilian king Fernando
III conquered the city. Most Almohad public architecture survived the conquest - the Christians
reused the mosque as a cathedral, the minaret as a bell tower. But Almohad domestic
architecture was largely demolished and rebuilt over the following centuries. This house survived

TRACK 08 / 20

The 2004 Competition
CONTEXT



  STOP & LOOK
  Back at street level, in Plaza de la Encarnación. Step back from the structure so you can see all six
  parasols at once. Notice where the supports come down to the ground - there are only six of them,
  two of which are the larger concrete cores housing the elevators.


The competition that produced this structure took place in two thousand four.

The city of Seville's redevelopment agency, called EMVISESA, ran an international architectural
competition for the redesign of Plaza de la Encarnación. The brief was complicated. Whatever
the winner designed had to: rebuild the food market that had stood on the site until nineteen
seventy-three; create a new public plaza; preserve the Roman and Almohad archaeology that
had just been discovered; and create some kind of contemporary architectural signature that
would put this neglected corner of Seville back on the cultural map.

Sixty-five proposals arrived from architects around the world.

The international jury was led by Spanish architects but included two of the most prominent
international names in the profession - Toyo Ito, the Japanese architect who later won the
Pritzker Prize in twenty thirteen, and Jacques Herzog, of the Swiss firm Herzog and de Meuron,
which had designed Tate Modern in London and the Bird's Nest stadium in Beijing.

The winner was a German architect named Jürgen Mayer H. He was forty years old, based in
Berlin, and had not yet built any project at this scale. His proposal was titled Metropol Parasol. It
called for six enormous wooden parasols, twenty-eight metres tall, arranged in a loose grid
above the square, with a market underneath, an elevated plaza at the level of the parasol bases,
a rooftop walkway running through the canopies, and the Antiquarium preserved at basement
level.

The jury chose it because it was the most ambitious. The most expensive too - the initial budget
was fifty million euros. The most engineering-intensive. The most uncompromising in its
commitment to the archaeology underneath. Of the sixty-five entries, it was the one that took the
constraint of having to float above the Roman city most seriously and turned it into the central
idea of the design.

It was also the riskiest. Nobody had ever built a wooden structure this size before. The materials,
the engineering, the construction process, the maintenance, the safety - all of it had to be
invented as the project went along. The jury knew this. They chose it anyway.

The engineering challenge is the next track.

TRACK 09 / 20

Inspiration
OBJECT



  STOP & LOOK
  Step back further if you can - across the square to one of the cafés on the perimeter. Look at the
  canopy as a whole, with its undulating roof line and the way the six parasols merge at the top into a
  single connected mass. Try to forget that it's wood for a moment and just look at the shape.


Jürgen Mayer was open about where the design came from.

Two inspirations, both Sevillian, both anchored in places you can walk to in twenty minutes from
here.

The first was the Cathedral of Seville. Specifically, the vaulted ceilings of the cathedral nave. The
cathedral is a fifteen-century late Gothic structure - the largest Gothic cathedral in the world by
area - built between fourteen thirty-four and fifteen seventeen, on the foundations of the Almohad
mosque. The vaults rise to forty-two metres at the highest point. They are made of stone ribs that
branch outward from columnar piers, splay across the ceiling, and meet other branches halfway,
forming a continuous interlocking pattern of structural curves.

Stand under the cathedral vaults and look up, and you'll recognise what Mayer was doing here.
The Setas are an inverted cathedral. The same branching structural pattern, the same sense of a
canopy held up by columns, the same play between mass and lightness - but built outdoors in
wood instead of indoors in stone, and inverted so that the branches spread above your head into
open sky instead of containing you inside a closed religious space.

The second inspiration was a tree. Or rather, a grove of trees. Three minutes' walk from here, in
a small square called Plaza Cristo de Burgos, there are several enormous mature ficus trees -
common Spanish name ficus de Bengala, the same species you find in tropical street plantings
across the world. The ficus in Plaza Cristo de Burgos are over a hundred years old, with trunks
two metres thick and canopies that span the entire square.

Walk under them and look up. You'll see exactly the geometry of the Setas. The branches spread
laterally, interlocking with their neighbours, casting a dense dappled shade over the ground. The
canopy is generous, sheltering, and feels organic in the way that only natural growth can. The
Setas are an architectural ficus tree. Six of them. Made of three thousand four hundred pieces of
Finnish pine.

The pairing is significant. A cathedral and a tree. The two great forms of canopy that medieval
Spain knew - the human one and the natural one. Mayer combined them into a single
architectural gesture.

Three thousand four hundred timber modules. Six vertical supports. Twenty-eight metres tall.
One hundred and fifty metres long. The largest wooden structure in the world.

The engineering was done by Arup, the London-based engineering firm responsible for some of
the most ambitious structural projects of the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries - the
Sydney Opera House roof, the Centre Pompidou in Paris, the Bird's Nest stadium in Beijing.
Arup's role here was to take Jürgen Mayer's architectural vision and figure out how to actually
build it.

The first decision was the material. Mayer wanted wood - the cathedral-vault inspiration would
only work if the canopy felt warm and organic, which steel could not have provided. Arup's
engineers looked at the available high-performance timber products and settled on Kerto LVL -
laminated veneer lumber. Specifically Kerto Q, a grade engineered for high structural loads.
Each Kerto module is made of thin layers of Finnish pine veneer, glued together under pressure
with alternating grain directions, producing a wood-composite material that is far stronger than
solid timber of equivalent dimensions.

The Kerto came from a Finnish company called Metsä Wood. The modules were manufactured
at Metsä's factory in Aichach, Germany. Each one was custom-shaped - three thousand four
hundred individual pieces, no two exactly the same, each precisely cut to fit a specific position in
the canopy.

The modules were glued together with a custom polyurethane resin designed to withstand the
high summer temperatures of southern Spain. The exterior was coated with a two-component
polyurethane weather sealant. Finished assembly began on site in two thousand nine, after two
years of prefabrication in Germany.

The six-support constraint - imposed by the archaeology below - was the structural problem. Six
supports for a hundred-and-fifty by seventy metre canopy is extreme. The canopy effectively
cantilevers outward from each support for thirty to forty metres in multiple directions. Arup solved
it by making two of the central supports into concrete-and-steel cores that carry most of the
vertical load and house the lifts, while the other four are pure timber-and-steel composites that
mainly resist the lateral and rotational forces.

The Setas were controversial from the start. Some of that controversy was earned. Some of it
was the inevitable cost of doing something new in an old city.

The first controversy was money. The initial competition brief in two thousand four specified a
budget of approximately fifty million euros. By the time construction was complete in twenty
eleven, the final cost had risen to one hundred and two million euros - more than double the
original estimate. The cost overruns came from a combination of factors. The Roman
archaeology discovery in two thousand three forced a major redesign and additional preservation
work. The engineering challenge of the wooden canopy required custom solutions that nobody
had built before. There were construction delays that added years to the timeline. And the city's
contracting process, like many large public projects, was not always efficient.

For Sevillanos, the cost was a political issue. A hundred and two million euros is a significant
sum for any European city. The construction overlapped with the start of the global financial crisis
in two thousand eight, and Spain was hit harder than most countries. Public spending on a single
architectural project, however ambitious, attracted criticism while the country was cutting
services elsewhere.

The second controversy was the name.

The project was called Metropol Parasol throughout the design and construction phase. That
was Jürgen Mayer's chosen name, and it was used in all the architectural press, all the awards,
and all the official communications. When the structure opened in twenty eleven, Sevillanos
almost immediately renamed it Las Setas - The Mushrooms - because of the obvious shape.

In twenty thirteen, the city discovered that Jürgen Mayer had personally trademarked the name
Metropol Parasol and would charge for its commercial use. Tourist guides, postcards, T-shirts,
merchandise - all of it would have to pay royalties to the architect. The city was not amused.
They officially adopted the people's name. Setas de Sevilla became the structure's legal
designation. The architect's name was, in effect, retired by his own client.

The third controversy was aesthetic. Some Sevillanos hated the structure from day one. A
wooden modernist canopy in the middle of a sixteenth and seventeenth century neighbourhood
looked like architectural vandalism to traditionalists. The opposition has softened over time but
never disappeared. You'll find Sevillanos who, fifteen years after opening, still won't walk through

palace still in use anywhere in Europe. King Felipe the Sixth uses the upper apartments when his
family visits. Almohad foundations, Pedro the First's fourteenth-century Mudéjar masterpiece,
Habsburg additions, Bourbon gardens. Game of Thrones used parts of it for the Water Gardens
of Dorne in twenty fourteen. Plan two and a half to three hours.

Third - Plaza de España. Fifteen minutes south, past the cathedral, in Maria Luisa Park. Built for
the nineteen twenty-nine Ibero-American Exposition - the same year as the Poble Espanyol on
Montjuïc in Barcelona, by complete coincidence. It's one of the most photographed semicircular
plazas in the world. Best at sunset.

Fourth - the Museo de Bellas Artes. Second-most-important fine arts museum in Spain after the
Prado. Murillo, Zurbarán, Valdés Leal, and the great Sevillian baroque painters in the building
they were painting for. Quieter than the cathedral, never crowded, two hours.

Fifth - Triana. Cross the Puente de Triana bridge from the cathedral side. The traditional
working-class neighbourhood of old Seville, home of flamenco, ceramics, bullfighters, and
sailors. Have dinner at one of the riverside restaurants and watch the sun set behind the Giralda
