[warmly] In 2004 Seville asked architects from around the world what to do with a square that had been a car park and then a dig for some thirty years. Sixty-five entries came in.

The winner was Jürgen Mayer H., from Berlin. His project was called Metropol Parasol: a huge shade over the square, standing on a handful of trunks, with a walkway across the top.

[amused] He didn't set out to build mushrooms. But Sevillanos called it las Setas anyway. The nickname stuck, and even the architect came round to it.

So where did the shapes come from? Mayer has said he took cues from the cathedral's vaulting and from the great shade trees of a neighbouring square. The city names them as the giant fig trees of the Plaza del Cristo de Burgos. Trees and vaults, then, not mushrooms.

[pause] As you step out, the timber is all around you. It was engineered with the firm Arup, and it's laminated veneer lumber made in Finland: thin sheets of wood glued into big panels. The pieces were milled in Bavaria and trucked to Seville, some 3,400 of them. And every beam wears a waterproof skin of polyurethane a few millimetres thick.

[curious] But it isn't all wood. The lift you just rode runs inside a concrete trunk.

You may have heard a bigger claim. Long billed as the largest wooden structure in the world, it is certainly one of the largest. Its engineers say "one of the largest timber structures ever built".

[warmly] A competition, a nickname nobody planned, and thousands of pieces of Finnish wood. That's what's all around you now.

Walk on along the walkway until the Giralda, the cathedral's tall tower, is in clear view above the rooftops. The next stop is The Cathedral and the Giralda.
