[curious] *Architect* magazine once called this the world's largest building held together primarily by glue. And in Seville, glue has an enemy: the summer sun.

The engineers were Arup, who also worked on the Sydney Opera House and on Beijing's Bird's Nest stadium. Wherever two timber panels cross around you, there's a joint, and there are more than three thousand of them. Inside each, steel bars are glued into the wood. The timber makers say about thirty-five thousand holes were drilled for those bars, by hand.

The glue is epoxy, a hard resin. The polyurethane is only the skin, a waterproof coat a few millimetres thick.

The question was heat. Scientists at Germany's Fraunhofer institute for wood research simulated what the sun would do to the joints. Dirk Kruse, of the institute, put it plainly: "the temperatures in the adhesive could potentially reach almost 60 degrees." Sixty Celsius: right at the limit the glue was rated for.

[pause] The fix sounds like baking. After gluing, the parts were heated again, a step called tempering, so the resin set harder. Every glued joint was baked a second time to toughen it.

It wasn't the only scare. By one account, in 2007 the engineers warned it could not be built as drawn, and it was largely re-engineered. Mika Kallio, of the timber firm Finnforest, later said: "It is perhaps the most complex timber structure ever built."

[warmly] The biggest single panel is sixteen and a half metres tall. And every one is still holding on, glue and steel, all around you.
