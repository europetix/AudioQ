[curious] Of all the gods and monsters found under this square, the museum chose two birds as its emblem.

Somewhere along the way, look for a mosaic hung upright on a wall, with two birds at its centre. It was pieced back together and set on the wall, so you see it standing up.

[amused] Some people call them the kissing birds. Kissing, or just keeping each other company? You decide.

The study of these mosaics describes a panel with two birds on a crater, the big bowl Romans mixed their wine in. It may be the same mosaic. That panel comes from the Hospitium de los Triclinia, the Hospitium of the Dining Rooms. [pause] If the label matches, look for more. On that panel, the birds sit in a small square at the centre of a geometric design. Around it, two borders are laid to look like moving water, and smaller zigzag panels add to the ripple. See if the water seems to move.

Why birds at all? Romans liked mosaics that brought nature indoors, plants and animals, especially in the parts of a house people walked through. What one image meant to the family who lived with it, the study admits, always eludes us.

[warmly] Out of all of it, the museum picked the gentlest image to stand for the whole.

Walk on, and look for the label for the casa almohade, the Almohad house. Play the next stop in the Antiquarium there: The Almohad house. If you passed it earlier, play that stop now, before you leave the Antiquarium.
