[lightly] Wine, water and the open sea. As you walk on through the Roman houses, keep an eye on the labels. Some of the names sound like the guest list for a Roman dinner.

Look for the Casa de Baco, the House of Bacchus. Bacchus was the Roman god of wine. Look, too, for the Casa de la Ninfa, the House of the Nymph. In ancient myth, nymphs were the spirits of springs, rivers and woods.

Two more names are worth watching for. The Patio del Océano is the Ocean Courtyard, and the city's tourism office lists a three-metre mural there. The other has a stranger name, the Hospitium de los Delfines, the Hospitium of the Dolphins. Hospitium is a Latin word for hospitality, or for taking in guests. It shares its root with our own word hospitality.

[pause] What exactly went on in each of these places is a question for the labels, not for guesswork. They tell you what each floor or wall shows, and whether you're looking at an original or a replica.

These houses were not frozen in one moment. The Roman city kept changing for centuries, rebuilt in phase after phase, long after its first mosaics were laid. [quietly] Above the Roman layers lies Moorish Seville, centuries later and much nearer the street.

Keep reading the labels as you walk. When you reach the one for the casa almohade, the Almohad house, play the next stop in the Antiquarium: The Almohad house. If you passed it earlier, play that stop now, before you leave the Antiquarium.
