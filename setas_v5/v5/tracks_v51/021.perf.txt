[curious] Ask where the parasols came from, and the city points to a group of old trees nearby.

Jürgen Mayer H. spoke of the cathedral's vaulting, and of big trees on a neighbouring square. The city names those trees: the fig trees of the Plaza del Cristo de Burgos. The operator calls them centennial, more than a hundred years old. Stand under them, look up into the branches, and see whether the parasols look familiar.

They need looking after now. A local report calls them singular trees, and the city has been giving them specialist care to keep them stable and safe.

[pause] Roman Seville also survives above ground nearby, in the Alameda de Hércules, the old promenade. There stand two Roman columns, from the age of the emperors Trajan and Hadrian, and probably from a temple. They were set up in the 1570s, on the orders of the Count of Barajas. On top are two statues: Hercules, the city's mythical founder, and Julius Caesar.

[warmly] Under the Setas, Roman Seville was dug out of the ground. At the Alameda, the city stood a piece of it back up, more than four centuries ago.

Both are nearby: the Plaza del Cristo de Burgos for the fig trees, and the Alameda de Hércules for the columns. Thank you again for listening, and enjoy the walk.
