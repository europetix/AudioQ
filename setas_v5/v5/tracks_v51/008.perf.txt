[curious] This square exists because of Napoleon's brother.

It takes its name from the Convent of the Incarnation, the Encarnación, a nunnery founded here in the late 1500s. The nuns stayed for more than two centuries. Then came the French. During the occupation, in 1810, Joseph Bonaparte's government ordered the convent pulled down to open up a square.

A square is rarely empty for long in Seville. Soon, traders had a wooden market here. Then the city built a proper one, a permanent market hall designed by the architect Melchor Cano, which went up in the 1830s. For well over a century, this was Seville's central market. By the operator's account, it had more than four hundred stalls, and it was the first known food market in the city.

[pause] It didn't last. Around the middle of the last century, a new traffic artery sliced off part of the market. In 1973 the rest was demolished. The operator gives three reasons. There were structural problems in the fish and vegetable stalls. There was damage from a flood of the Tamarguillo, a stream on the edge of the city. And the first supermarkets had arrived.

The traders were moved out to temporary stalls, and for decades the square was a fenced, dusty car park. Later, the city planned an underground car park beneath it. The digging ran into Roman Seville, and that plan was dropped. What they found is in the Antiquarium, beside these lifts.

[wry] So the gap that Napoleon's brother cleared has been filled again and again. First by a market, then by parked cars, then by archaeologists, and finally by the Setas.

And the traders? Their own story waits for you at the end, above the market.

[warmly] The lift you're about to take rises through every chapter of it, from below the old streets to high above the rooftops.

Take the lift up. Start the next stop as the doors close. It carries on as you step out onto the walkway: Mushrooms of wood.
