[lightly] Wine, couches and leftovers. As you walk on through the Roman houses, keep an eye on the labels. Some of them read like the plan for a Roman dinner party.

Start with the Casa de Baco, the House of Bacchus, the Roman god of wine. Romans dined lying on couches, in a room called the triclinium. Here, the main mosaic of that dining room showed the triumph of Bacchus, with Ariadne, the princess he married in the myth, beside him. One scholar of these floors says it was the favourite Bacchus scene in Roman southern Spain for a long time. [pause] When you find the Bacchus floor, look at its corners for the drinking vessels.

Then the couch. Look for the Casa del Sigma, the House of the Sigma. Sigma is a Greek letter, and in late Roman times it was often written like a C. The name passed to a curved dining couch, where guests lay in a half-circle around the food. If the label speaks of a stibadium, that's the same curved couch.

[amused] Now the strangest mosaic of the evening. In the Hospitium de los Delfines, the Hospitium of the Dolphins, the scholar describes a rare motif set in a geometric pattern: an unswept floor. Food scraps, scattered as if a feast has just ended. Hospitium is a Latin word for hospitality, or for taking in guests. Which party it was, nobody can know. The scholar admits that's exactly what wakes up your imagination.

Two more names to watch for. The Casa de la Ninfa, the House of the Nymph, a spirit of springs and rivers. And the Patio del Océano, the Ocean Courtyard, where the city's tourism office lists a three-metre mural. The sea runs all through these floors. The scholar lists dolphins, fish and water birds.

[quietly] The guests are long gone. Their dinner party is still on the floor.

Keep walking and reading the labels in the Antiquarium. Next, look for a mosaic hung upright on a wall, with two birds and a wine bowl. Play the next stop when you find it, or as you walk on: Two birds and a wine bowl.
