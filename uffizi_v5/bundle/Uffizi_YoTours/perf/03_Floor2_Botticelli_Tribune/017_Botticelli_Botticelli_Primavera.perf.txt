[curious] Nobody is quite sure what this painting means. The museum itself calls its meaning a mystery.

Botticelli painted it around 1480, on poplar, and it's larger than the Birth of Venus. By the end of that century it hung in a Medici house on Via Larga, home of the heirs of Lorenzo di Pierfrancesco, a cousin of Lorenzo the Magnificent. It sat above a lettuccio, a chest with a high back. Later it moved to the Medici villa at Castello.

[pause] Read it from right to left. On the right, the wind Zephyr bursts through the trees and seizes the nymph Chloris. Look for the flowers at her lips. Beside her stands the same nymph, transformed into Flora, goddess of spring, in her flowered dress. In the centre, a little set back, is Venus. Above her, Cupid, blindfolded, aims his arrow of love. On the left, the three Graces dance in a ring. And at the far left, Mercury, with his helmet and winged sandals, reaches up with his staff to touch a cloud.

[lightly] They stand in a grove of orange and laurel trees. Now look down at the meadow, thick with flowers. At least forty-two different plants have been identified there, twenty-eight of them flowering, perhaps copied from a herbal.

So what is it about? The museum's own reading is a celebration of love, peace and prosperity. Some have suggested a Medici wedding, but that is only a possibility.

[warmly] Since the rehang, it has new company. Two of Botticelli's Madonnas hang either side of it, of the Rose Garden and of the Cherubim. And across the passage, the Birth of Venus looks straight back at it.

Turn round and go through the passage to the painting that faces this one: the Birth of Venus, the goddess on her shell. Room A13 comes after it.
