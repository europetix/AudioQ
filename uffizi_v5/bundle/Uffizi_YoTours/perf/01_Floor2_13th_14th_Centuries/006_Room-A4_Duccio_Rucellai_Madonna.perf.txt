[conspiratorial] For about three hundred years, this was thought to be a Cimabue. It isn't.

In 1285 a confraternity called the Laudesi ordered a great altarpiece from a painter from Siena, Duccio di Buoninsegna. They met in the church of Santa Maria Novella to sing the praises of the Virgin. Centuries later, Vasari described it and gave it to Cimabue. The name stuck to the wrong man.

Then the paperwork surfaced. In 1854 the scholar Gaetano Milanesi realised that an old published document was the actual contract, made out to Duccio. Many still refused to believe it. The contract was published again, the argument went on, and only an exhibition here at the Uffizi in 1937 closed the debate for good. [pause] And the nickname? From the late fifteen hundreds it hung in the chapel of the Rucellai family.

It's the largest painting on wood from the thirteenth century known today. Six angels kneel around the throne, three on each side, and they seem to float in the gold. Small roundels of saints run round the frame. Near the bottom, look for two figures in black and white: Saint Dominic and Saint Peter Martyr, because Santa Maria Novella was a Dominican church.

[lightly] Now find the gilded edge of the Virgin's dark mantle and follow it down. It doesn't run straight. It curls and ripples the whole length of her figure, a line drawn for the pleasure of drawing it. Cimabue never painted an edge like that. It's a Sienese delight in line. Even the Child's red cloth is woven with gold.

[warmly] Three centuries under another man's name, and Duccio's signature was in that line all along.
