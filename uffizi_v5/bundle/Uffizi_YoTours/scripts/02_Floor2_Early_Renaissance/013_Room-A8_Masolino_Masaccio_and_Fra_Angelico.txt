In this room the gold begins to come down, and a new kind of space comes in. It starts with two painters who were both called Tommaso.

Masolino and Masaccio painted the Saint Anne with the Virgin and Child together, around 1424, for the church of Sant'Ambrogio, according to Vasari. Its old name, Sant'Anna Metterza, means Anne "in third place": grandmother, mother and son, one above the other. Vasari gave the whole panel to Masaccio. Today the stern Saint Anne is given to Masolino, and the Virgin and Child to Masaccio. Look at that Child. He's solid and heavy, almost a little Hercules. Masaccio was in his early twenties. He died at twenty-six.

Fra Angelico, a Dominican friar, takes you somewhere gentler. His Glorification of Mary is crowded with more than forty saints, each one an individual. Christ isn't crowning his mother. He's adding a precious stone to her diadem. Its predella was separated from it more than four centuries ago, and was reunited with it here in 2024. Look too for his Madonna from Pontassieve, of 1435, where the Child's pale skin almost glows.

And Paolo Uccello? Vasari tells that he sat up all night over his perspective. When his wife called him to bed, he answered, "What a sweet thing this perspective is."
