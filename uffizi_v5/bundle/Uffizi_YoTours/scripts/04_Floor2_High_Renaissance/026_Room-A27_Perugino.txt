Around 1500, few painters in Italy were paid more than this one. And Vasari tells us that late in life he was mocked for painting the same thing again and again.

He was Pietro Vannucci. People called him Perugino, after the city of Perugia, though he was born in the small town of Città della Pieve. In the mid 1490s he painted these two altarpieces for the church of San Giusto alle Mura, by the city walls. The church did not survive. It was pulled down in 1529, ahead of a siege.

Look at the Pietà first. Mary holds her dead son. John supports him on one side, and Mary Magdalene prays on the other. Nobody screams or tears their clothes. Everyone is still, under the grey stone pillars of a calm, measured portico.

Then the Oration in the Garden. Christ kneels on a rock and turns toward an angel holding a cup. Below him, three apostles have fallen asleep. And far behind, soldiers are coming, led by Judas, in two neat, matching groups.

That balance was his great gift. If Vasari is right, it also became his trap.

But one young painter studied it closely. Perugino was traditionally Raphael's teacher, and he was certainly his model. Remember these soft faces and quiet landscapes when you reach Raphael, a few rooms on.
