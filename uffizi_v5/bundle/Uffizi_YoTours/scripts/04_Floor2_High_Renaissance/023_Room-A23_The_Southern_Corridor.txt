This is the shortest corridor in the gallery, and the one with the best view. Take a minute at the windows.

That's the Arno below you, and just along the river, the Ponte Vecchio, with its little shops hanging over the water. Since 1593, when Grand Duke Ferdinando moved the butchers out, those shops have belonged to goldsmiths. Now look along the top of the bridge. That long run of windows is the Vasari Corridor, the Medici's raised private passage. It links Palazzo Vecchio, this building, the bridge and the Pitti Palace on the far bank.

In the small hours of the 4th of November 1966, the Arno broke its banks and flooded the city. Very early that morning, the head of the museums came here with curators and attendants. They carried works up to the higher floors. And they took the paintings out of the Vasari Corridor, which, in the museum's words, seemed "on the edge of collapse together with Ponte Vecchio".

The red stone she-wolf in the middle is carved from porphyry. And the marble head called the Dying Alexander is really a sea god, one of the museum's rare Greek originals.

The rooms you walked past, A17 to A21, normally show painting from beyond the Alps and from fifteenth-century Siena. They are closed while they are rearranged.

Turn into the long Western Corridor. The first doorway on your left, the side away from the courtyard windows, is Room A25: look for an altarpiece of four saints under a pink and green loggia.
