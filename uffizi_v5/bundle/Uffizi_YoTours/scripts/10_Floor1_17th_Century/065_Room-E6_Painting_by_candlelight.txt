In this room, the light doesn't come in through a window. It comes from inside the pictures.

Gerrit van Honthorst was a Dutch painter from Utrecht. Early in the seventeenth century he went to Rome, and there he saw what Caravaggio had done with darkness. He took that hard spotlight and made it warmer and more intimate: candles, lamps, faces glowing in the night. The Italians gave him a nickname for it, Gherardo delle Notti, Gerard of the Nights. You've already met his name once, on the Caravaggio that a collector bought thinking it was his.

His Adoration of the Child was painted around 1619 or 1620. There is no candle in it at all. The light pours out of the newborn's body. It softens the Virgin's face as she leans over him. It catches Joseph, leaning on his stick, and two angels bending over the manger. Everything outside that small circle sinks back into the night.

Grand Duke Cosimo the Second bought some of Honthorst's works in 1620, and this was probably one of them. So it may have reached the Medici almost as soon as it was painted.

Caravaggio used darkness to shock. In this room it is used to hold people close together.
