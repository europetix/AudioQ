When this painting reached Florence, it caused a stir. According to the museum, it was denied the honour of being shown in the Gallery. Today it hangs at the heart of it.

The story is from the Bible. Judith, a young Jewish woman, saves her besieged town by going into the enemy camp. After a banquet, the Assyrian general Holofernes falls into a drunken sleep, and she kills him. As the Book of Judith puts it, "The Lord has struck him down by the hand of a woman."

Look at how Artemisia Gentileschi stages it. This is no graceful heroine holding a tidy sword. The general lies across the bed, his head gripped by the hair. Judith braces her arms and works the blade into his neck. Her maid leans on him to hold him down. And the blood spurts so hard it stains Judith's own dress.

Artemisia was the daughter of the painter Orazio Gentileschi, who worked in Rome. In 1612 a court in Rome convicted Agostino Tassi, a painter who worked with her father, of raping her. During the trial she herself was tortured to test her word, with cords wrapped round her fingers and pulled tight. She held to her account. "I have told the truth and I always will, because it is true." Many people read that ordeal into this picture. Perhaps. But it was her painting, not her suffering, that made her name.

She spent seven years in Florence, and she became the first woman admitted to the city's Academy of Drawing. She agreed the fee for this canvas with Grand Duke Cosimo the Second. He died in 1621, soon after it was finished, and the money stalled. To get paid, she needed help from her friend Galileo Galilei.

Two women working together, and a painter who refused to look away. Florence wasn't ready to hang it. The world has caught up.
