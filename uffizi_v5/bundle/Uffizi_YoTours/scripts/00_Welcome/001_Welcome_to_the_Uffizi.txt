The most famous art gallery in Florence began life as an office block. The word Uffizi simply means "offices".

Around you is the Piazzale degli Uffizi, the long stone courtyard between the two wings, with statues of famous Tuscans standing in the niches. In 1560, Duke Cosimo the First de' Medici began this building to a design by Giorgio Vasari, to bring the offices of the Florentine state under one roof. Vasari and Cosimo both died in 1574. Cosimo's son Francesco and the architect Bernardo Buontalenti finished it.

Francesco filled the top-floor loggia with ancient statues and paintings, a private gallery for himself and his court. Over the next century, a select few visitors from outside the court were let in. Then, on the 24th of June 1769, Grand Duke Peter Leopold opened it to the public. The museum calls it one of the world's first museums created with a modern concept.

Why is it all still here? After the last Medici grand duke died, his sister, Anna Maria Luisa, made a Family Pact with the new ruling house of Lorraine. Nothing was ever to be taken away from Florence and the State. The collections would stay, in the pact's words, for the ornament of the State, the benefit of the public, and to attract the curiosity of foreigners. Which, today, means you.

This guide comes in three lengths. The full tour takes about two and three-quarter hours, the essential version about two hours, and the highlights about an hour and a quarter. Whichever one is on your phone, play it in order. The numbers may jump, and every stop tells you where to go next. We start on the second floor and finish on the first.

Go in at the museum entrance and climb the Granducal staircase all the way to the second floor. There is a lift beside it. At the top, walk through the vestibule, Room A1, into the long corridor lined with marble statues: Room A2, the Eastern Corridor.
