In 1802, the most admired woman in Europe was packed up and carried off to Paris. She stands in this room. In 1816, she came home.

Bernardo Buontalenti built the Tribune in the early 1580s for Grand Duke Francesco the First, as a place for his jewels and treasures. It's an octagon, because eight was the number that draws near to Heaven. And Francesco planned it as a whole cosmos of the four elements.

Look up at the dome. It's set with 5,780 pieces of mother-of-pearl from the Indian Ocean. That's water. The red velvet walls are fire. The lantern on top is open to the winds, so it's air, and a weathervane up there is linked to a wind rose painted inside. The lantern is a sundial too, catching the sun at the solstices and equinoxes. And the floor, a great flower of coloured marbles, is earth. Visitors view it from the doorways, to protect it.

Now the goddess. The Medici Venus is a marble from the late Hellenistic world, shielding herself with her hands, a small dolphin beside her. Ferdinando de' Medici, then a cardinal in Rome, bought her there. Later she came to Florence, with the two Wrestlers and the kneeling Knife Grinder you see beside her. The French took her for the Musée Napoléon. After Napoleon fell, the Treaty of Vienna sent her home.

One small deception. The base is signed by a Greek sculptor, Cleomenes of Athens. The museum's experts say the signature did not originally belong to this statue.

For two centuries this little room was the high point of the Grand Tour. Titian's Venus of Urbino hung near her for years. You'll meet her downstairs.

Walk on along the Eastern Corridor to its end. The rooms after the Tribune, A17 to A21, are closed while they are rearranged, so walk past them. Turn the corner into the short corridor with windows over the river: Room A23, the Southern Corridor.
