On the back of this little canvas, an old inscription gives the painter's nickname: "Michelangelo of the Bambocciate".

Michelangelo Cerquozzi worked in Rome. A bambocciata was a small scene of everyday life, a fashion named after a painter from the north nicknamed Il Bamboccio. Here, in the 1640s, he turns the same eye on himself.

He's at his easel in a bare studio. In front of him an old man, stripped to the waist, kneels at a lectern. He's a model, posing as Saint Jerome. Now look round the room. A marble bust lies on the floor, there to be copied. A brazier of glowing coals warms the bare room. And on the wall hangs a painting of the Good Thief on his cross. He liked ordinary Romans as models, the way Caravaggio did.

It's a self-portrait that shows you not just a face, but a working day.

The next rooms follow the same thread: C4 is called Showing the Artwork, and C5 reaches the early eighteenth century.
