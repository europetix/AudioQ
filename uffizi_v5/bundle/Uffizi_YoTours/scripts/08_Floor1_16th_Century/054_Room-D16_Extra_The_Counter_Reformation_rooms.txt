In 1563, at the Council of Trent, the Catholic Church decided that religious pictures had a job to do.

The Protestant Reformation had attacked images. The Church answered by defending them, on one condition. Sacred art had to be clear, decent, and able to teach and move ordinary believers. Room D18, the Room of the Counter-Reformation, is given to the religious painting of those decades.

But step into D17 first, because the Medici court kept another kind of picture for itself. Alessandro Allori painted his Hercules on a small sheet of copper, for Francesco the First. The nine Muses crown the hero for rescuing them from the Giants, who lie beaten on the ground. Pictures like this were riddles for a few learned eyes. Look at the Muse with pearls threaded through her curls. That smooth, pale skin is the lesson of Allori's teacher, Bronzino.

In the same room, Jacopo Ligozzi's Fortune balances on a globe. A crown, a sceptre and books tumble down. Coins slide into a glass vase, and some of them turn into butterflies. Ligozzi later led the decoration of the Tribune upstairs.

Come back into Room D16, and go into the narrow room next door, D19, through the doorway near the far end of their shared wall. Look for the sign for the Vasari Corridor meeting point.
