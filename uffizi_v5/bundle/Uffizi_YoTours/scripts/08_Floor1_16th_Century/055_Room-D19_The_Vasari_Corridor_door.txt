Somewhere beyond this narrow room, a corridor runs out of the museum, above the streets, across a bridge and into a garden on the far side of the river.

This is where visits to the Vasari Corridor begin. Duke Cosimo the First ordered it in 1565 for the wedding of his son Francesco to Joanna of Austria, and Giorgio Vasari built it in just five months. It runs for about 750 metres. It starts at Palazzo Vecchio and passes through this building. Then it crosses the Arno along the top of the Ponte Vecchio and looks down into the church of Santa Felicita. It ends by the Buontalenti Grotto in the Boboli Gardens, beside the Pitti Palace. It let the grand dukes move between their home and the seat of government without setting foot in the street.

You've already met some of its former residents. From 1973 to 2016, part of the self-portrait collection hung in the Corridor, seen by very few. It closed that year and reopened in December 2024, stripped back to its plain original walls and its seventy-three windows.

Today, more than fifty Roman portrait busts stand in the stretch above the Ponte Vecchio.

The Corridor is visited separately, by reservation, in accompanied groups that start from the meeting point in this room. It runs one way only, to the Boboli Gardens, so you can't come back through it to the museum. This guide stays in the galleries.

Room D20, the Venetian Chapel, opens off this room near its corridor end. Look for Lotto's Susanna and the Elders. Don't go through the Vasari Corridor door.
