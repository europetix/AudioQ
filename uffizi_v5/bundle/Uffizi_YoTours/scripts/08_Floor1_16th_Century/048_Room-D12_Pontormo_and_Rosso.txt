Two young men trained in the same Florentine workshop, Andrea del Sarto's. Between them, they broke nearly every rule it taught.

Jacopo Carucci was born in 1494 in Pontorme, near Empoli, and took the village as his name, Pontormo. Giovanni Battista di Jacopo, a year younger, was called Rosso, the redhead. They are among the first painters of what we now call Mannerism: elegant, unsettling, deliberately strange.

Until the seventh of February 2027, three of Pontormo's paintings from this room are on loan to an exhibition in Rome.

Rosso's altarpiece here shows what the fuss was about. In 1518, the prior of the hospital of Santa Maria Nuova ordered it for the altar of a Catalan widow, buried in the church of Ognissanti. When he saw it, he was far from pleased. Vasari reports that he called it sketchy, and said the saints looked like cruel, desperate devils. The picture was sent off to a country church. Rosso sued him for the money he was still owed.

Now look at Saint Jerome, gaunt and scowling. You can guess what upset the prior. Then look lower, at the two child angels on the steps of the throne, lost in their book. Rosso could be tender too.

Studies under the paint found another change. Two of the saints were repainted for the new church: one now has a stone on his forehead, the martyr Stephen.
