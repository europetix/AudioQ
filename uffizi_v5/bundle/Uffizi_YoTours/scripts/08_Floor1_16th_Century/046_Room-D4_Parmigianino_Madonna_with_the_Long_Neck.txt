The contract gave Parmigianino five months to paint this altarpiece. He took the rest of his life, and still didn't finish it.

A noblewoman, Elena Baiardi Tagliaferri, ordered it in 1534 for a church in Parma. He died in 1540, at thirty-seven, with the panel still in his studio. When it was set up on its altar, a line in Latin was added at the base of the column. In English it reads, "Adverse destiny prevented Francesco Mazzola from Parma from completing this work". An apology, painted into the picture.

The Virgin is stretched like a figure in a dream. The column beside her may be the key. An old hymn to the Virgin says, "Your neck is like a column", a sign of her purity.

The Child sprawls across her lap, enormous and fast asleep. That sleep looks ahead to his death on the cross. And look at the angel on the left holding up a tall urn to the Virgin. On it, the museum explains, is a reflection of the Crucifixion.

Then drop your eyes to the bottom right. A tiny man is unrolling a scroll. That's Saint Jerome. He turns towards Saint Francis, who was meant to stand beside him. Of Francis, Parmigianino painted one foot. Look for it.

Vasari tells us that in his last years Parmigianino was lost in alchemy. Whether or not that's fair, the picture stopped where he stopped, and that single foot is all there is of a saint.
