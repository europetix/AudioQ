 How to use this guide
 This is a room-by-room audio guide to the Uffizi Gallery, following the museum's own Classic
 itinerary: the whole second floor, from the birth of Renaissance painting to the High Renaissance, then
 down to the first floor for the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries. The guide is built around two kinds of
 tracks. Knowing the difference will let you move at your own pace.


 Room introductions
 Most tracks are short room introductions. Play one as you walk into a new room: it tells you what's
 there, what to look at first, and which works have their own dedicated track. In the header these are
 marked R-INTRO. They usually run between one and a half and two and a half minutes.

 Single-work tracks
 The rest focus on a single painting or sculpture. Play one when you are standing in front of the work it
 describes; in the header these are marked W-FOCUS. They usually run between two and three minutes.

 Numbers and room signs
 Every track carries a number — 001, 002, and so on — in the order you walk the tour, and the audio
 files are numbered to match, so you can simply play them in sequence. The museum room is printed at
 the top of each track (ROOM A9, ROOM D23, and so on) and matches the gallery's own wall signs, so
 you always know exactly where you should be standing.

 Pauses and movement
 Each track ends with a short silent pause built into the audio. That's your moment to look, photograph if
 you want to, and walk to the next stop. When you reach a new room, play the next track. When you're
 standing in front of a marquee work, play its dedicated track. The audio always tells you what to play
 next at the end of each track. You don't have to remember anything.

 On honesty and change
 Museums move things. As this guide was prepared, the Uffizi kept a live list of works temporarily off
 display — out on loan, or away for restoration — and individual rooms close for maintenance from time
 to time. A few of the second-floor rooms were also between rehangs. The tour names what should be in
 each room as the museum itself lists it, but check the live notices page at uffizi.it before you go, in case
 a particular work is travelling.

 The visit, in shape
 T00 at the entrance → up to the second floor for the 13th and 14th centuries, the Early Renaissance and
 the Tribune, Renaissance beyond the Alps, and the High Renaissance → down to the first floor for the
 sixteenth century and then the seventeenth → T-LAST on the way out. This follows the museum's own
 Classic itinerary (groups A, D and E) and takes a little over two hours at an unhurried pace.

 The Uffizi — Before You Go In
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In the Piazzale degli Uffizi, the long narrow courtyard between the two wings of the building, before you join
    the entrance queue at Door 1.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A long, narrow stone courtyard running down toward the Arno, framed by two matching wings of arches and
    columns, with statues of famous Tuscans set in the niches along the piers. The river closes off the far end.
    LISTEN FOR
    What this building actually was, how the gallery came to be, how this guide works, and the shape of the
    Classic route you're about to walk.


 Welcome to Florence, and welcome to the Uffizi. Before you go in, stand here in the courtyard for a
 moment, because the building itself is the first thing worth understanding.

 Look at the name. Uffizi means offices. Cosimo the First de' Medici had this built in the fifteen-sixties,
 to a design by Giorgio Vasari, to house the administrative offices and magistrates of the Tuscan state —
 all under one roof, the two long wings you see on either side of you, joined at the far end by the river. It
 was a government building. The art came later. Cosimo's son, Francesco the First, turned the top floor
 into a private gallery for the Medici treasures, and around fifteen eighty-one he had Buontalenti build a
 small octagonal jewel-box of a room, the Tribune, to show off the very best of them. For two centuries
 this was a private collection you needed permission to see. Then, in seventeen sixty-nine, it opened to
 the public — one of the first museums in the modern sense anywhere in the world. And when the last of
 the Medici died, the family pact signed by Anna Maria Luisa left the entire collection to the city of
 Florence on one condition: that it could never be sold, never broken up, never leave the city. Which is
 why it's all still here.

 How this guide works. It's built from two kinds of tracks. Room tracks orient you the moment you walk
 into a new room — what's there, what to look at first. Work tracks are for standing in front of a single
 painting. Each track tells you what to play next, so you can just walk and listen. Every track is labelled
 with the museum's own room code, so you can match the audio to the signs on the walls.

 The shape of the day. You'll climb to the second floor and walk the Renaissance more or less in the
 order it happened — the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries first, then the early Renaissance and the
 Tribune, then the High Renaissance of Leonardo, Michelangelo and Raphael. Then you come down to
 the first floor for the sixteenth century, and finish with the seventeenth — Caravaggio, Rembrandt. It
 runs a little over two hours if you don't rush.

 One practical thing: book a timed entry slot before you come, or the queue can swallow an hour. When
 you're through the ticket check and you've reached the top of the staircase on the second floor, play the
 next track.

 The Eastern Corridor — The Spine of the Gallery
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In the long Eastern Corridor: a tall, straight gallery with windows down one side and the painting rooms
    opening off the other, ancient statues lining the walls.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    A corridor running the full length of one wing — a painted, vaulted ceiling covered end to end in delicate
    'grotesque' decoration, ranks of ancient Roman marble busts and statues along the walls, and tall windows
    looking over the courtyard toward the Arno.
    LISTEN FOR
    What these corridors are, the ceiling worth tipping your head back for, and how to use the corridor as you go.


 Before you dive into the painting rooms, look at where you're walking, because the corridor itself is one
 of the oldest parts of the museum and one of the easiest to march straight through without noticing.

 This is the Eastern Corridor, one of three that Vasari ran around the U of the building in the
 fifteen-sixties. He designed them as long, uninterrupted perspectives, and the Medici quickly filled them
 with their collection of ancient sculpture — the marble emperors, gods and athletes you can see ranked
 along the walls, many of them brought from Rome. For centuries this corridor of antiquities was the
 gallery; the rooms full of paintings opened off it as a kind of bonus.

 Now tip your head back. The entire vaulted ceiling is covered in what's called grotesque decoration —
 painted in the fifteen-eighties, a fantasy of scrolls, masks, little creatures, garlands and medallions,
 named after the ancient Roman wall-paintings that had recently been dug up underground, in 'grottoes.'
 It runs unbroken for the whole length, and most visitors never look up at it once.

 Through the windows on one side you get your first views down into the narrow courtyard and out
 toward the river. Use the corridor as your reset between rooms — step back into it, take a breath, look
 up, then turn into the next painting room. The first one is just off the corridor. Turn into it and play the
 next track.

 Thirteenth-Century Painting — Where the Story
 Starts
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In the first painting room off the corridor, hung with large gold-ground panels — Madonnas and painted
    crucifixes from the twelve and thirteen hundreds.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Devotional panels on wood, their backgrounds solid gold leaf: enthroned Madonnas, a large painted Cross or
    two, ranks of small saints. The figures are frontal, elongated, outlined in dark line, with little sense of weight
    or of real space.
    LISTEN FOR
    What this kind of painting was for, why it looks the way it does, and exactly what to carry into the next room.


 You're at the beginning, in every sense, because the gallery is arranged so that you walk forward
 through time — and this is the oldest room of all.

 Look at what these paintings have in common: gold. The backgrounds are solid gold leaf, beaten thin
 and burnished, and the figures stand against it without any landscape, any room, any sky. The Madonnas
 face you straight on, long and narrow, their features drawn in firm dark outlines, their robes folding in
 stylised gold lines rather than real cloth. Nobody here has much weight. Nobody quite stands in a space
 you could enter.

 That isn't failure — it's the whole intention. This is painting inherited from Byzantium, from the
 Greek-speaking Christian East, and its job was never to show you the visible world. It was to open a
 window onto an unchanging heaven: the gold is divine light, not afternoon sun, and the stillness is the
 stillness of eternity. These were objects to pray in front of, lit by candles, glowing in the dark of a
 church.

 Hold three things in your eye as you leave: the flat gold, the frontal pose, the absence of weight.
 Because next door, three enormous paintings of exactly this subject are going to take that inherited
 tradition and start pulling it apart — and you'll only see how radical that is if you remember what it
 looked like here first. The next room is just ahead. Walk in, and play the next track.

 Cimabue, Duccio, Giotto — the Room of the
 Maestà
    WHERE YOU ARE
    The large room where three monumental altarpieces face each other across the floor — one on each of three
    walls, each well over three metres tall.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Three versions of the same subject — the Virgin and Child enthroned in majesty, a 'Maestà' — by three
    painters within about a single generation: Cimabue (right), Duccio (left), and Giotto (centre, in the place of
    honour). Gold grounds, angels, prophets beneath the thrones.
    LISTEN FOR
    Why these three are deliberately staged together, and the order to take them in so the change is visible.


 This is one of the most carefully arranged rooms in the whole building, and one of the most rewarding,
 because it isn't just a display — it's an argument, staged in paint.

 Three paintings, all the same subject: the Virgin and Child enthroned in majesty, what's called a Maestà.
 All three are enormous, all three sit on gold, and all three were made within roughly thirty years of one
 another, around twelve eighty to thirteen ten. Hung together like this, they let you watch painting
 change its mind in real time.

 On the right is Cimabue, the great old master, working at the very summit of the Byzantine tradition you
 just saw next door. On the left is Duccio, the founder of the Sienese school — still steeped in gold and
 elegance, but loosening it. And in the centre, in the place of honour, is Giotto, the painter who broke the
 rules: whose Virgin has real bodily weight, whose throne has real depth you feel you could walk into.
 Everything you'll see on this floor for the rest of the morning grows out of the shift that happens
 between these three walls.

 There's a separate track for each painting. Take them in the order that makes the change visible:
 Cimabue first, on the right, then Duccio on the left, then Giotto in the centre last. Go and stand in front
 of the Cimabue, and play the next track.

 Cimabue — Santa Trinita Maestà
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of Cimabue's Maestà, on the right-hand wall — a towering panel close to four metres tall, so step
    back and look up.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    The Virgin enthroned with the Child on her knee, against gold. Four angels stack symmetrically up each side
    of an elaborate inlaid throne; beneath it, in arched openings, four Old Testament prophets hold scrolls.
    Tempera and gold on a single great wooden panel, about 1280.
    LISTEN FOR
    What belongs to the old world here, the one thing reaching toward the new, and the story that links it across
    the room.


 Start with Cimabue, painted around twelve eighty for the church of Santa Trinita — and look at it as the
 most traditional of the three, because that's exactly what makes it the right place to begin.

 Almost everything here belongs to the old Byzantine manner. The gold ground. The strict symmetry.
 The angels stacked up the sides in tidy tiers, more like a repeating pattern than a crowd of living beings.
 The Virgin herself is long and slender and weightless, her robe ridged with fine lines of gold — those
 gold striations standing in for the way light would fall, rather than real, observed shadow. Cimabue was
 the most celebrated painter in Florence in his day, the summit of a way of working centuries old.

 But look closer, at three things. First, the faces — there's a softness, a gentle shading around the eyes
 and cheeks, a reach toward something more human than the tradition strictly required. Second, look
 down at the base of the throne, at the four prophets in their arched niches: Cimabue is trying to make
 that architecture turn and recede, to seat them in a real structure with depth. It doesn't fully work — the
 space is more diagram than room — but the ambition is there. Third, look at the throne itself, at how he
 tilts its sides inward to suggest it stands in space; it's a first, halting attempt at the very thing Giotto,
 across this room, will solve completely.

 Cimabue is often called the last great painter of the old world. There's an old story, told by Vasari, that
 he was the teacher who found a shepherd boy sketching sheep on a rock and took him on — and that
 boy was Giotto, whose painting hangs across this room. Whether or not it happened, the room is hung to
 tell exactly that story: the master, and the pupil who would overtake him. Now turn to the left-hand
 wall, to Duccio's Madonna, and play the next track.

 Duccio — Rucellai Madonna
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of Duccio's Maestà, on the left-hand wall — the largest of the three panels, with six angels framing
    the throne.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    The Virgin enthroned, the Child raising his hand in blessing, six kneeling angels holding the sides and corners
    of a slender Gothic throne draped in gold-edged cloth. The frame carries small roundels of saints. Tempera
    and gold, dated 1285 — the largest 13th-century panel in the room.
    LISTEN FOR
    How the Sienese answer differs from the Florentine one, the detective story of its authorship, and the detail
    that is pure Duccio.


 This is the Rucellai Madonna, painted in twelve eighty-five by Duccio di Buoninsegna — and it carries
 a small detective story. It was commissioned by a religious confraternity for a chapel in the church of
 Santa Maria Novella, and for centuries everyone assumed it was a Cimabue. Only in the twentieth
 century did scholars settle that it was Duccio's — a reattribution that reshaped our whole understanding
 of both men and of the Sienese school he founded.

 It's the biggest panel in the room, and the most graceful. Where Cimabue is grave and architectural,
 Duccio is a master of line. Look first at the throne: instead of a heavy structure, it's a slender, delicate
 thing, and six angels kneel around it, leaning in to hold its sides and corners as if it weighed almost
 nothing — three down each side, their wings overlapping in a soft rhythm. Then look at the edge of the
 Virgin's deep blue cloak: it's trimmed with a flowing band of gold that ripples and curves down the
 whole length of the figure. Follow that gold hem with your eye — it's drawing as music, a line that
 exists for sheer beauty. No Florentine would have lavished such care on a decorative edge.

 And look at her face: softer than Cimabue's, more inward, almost sad — a real human tenderness inside
 all the formality. Notice, too, the little roundels of saints set into the frame, like a border of jewels
 around the whole image. This is the Gothic line at its most refined: beauty offered as a form of devotion.
 Siena would follow this road of elegance and colour for the next century, while Florence took the other
 one — which is the road the painting in the centre of the room is about to choose, head-on. Turn to the
 Giotto in the centre, and play the next track.

 Giotto — Ognissanti Maestà
    WHERE YOU ARE
    In front of Giotto's Maestà, in the centre of the room, in the place of honour — face it straight on so you can
    read the throne.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    The Virgin enthroned, larger and more solid than the figures around her, on a Gothic throne built like real
    architecture that recedes into depth; angels and saints kneel and stand in front and to the sides, overlapping,
    taking up room; light models the bodies. Tempera and gold, about 1310, from the church of Ognissanti.
    LISTEN FOR
    The single change that earns Giotto the title 'father of Western painting,' and how to see it in ten seconds.


 And here is Giotto, painted around thirteen ten for the church of Ognissanti. Now that you've looked
 hard at the other two, you can see in about ten seconds why this one changed everything.

 Giotto's Virgin has a body. Look at how she sits: you sense knees and a lap under the white robe, a real
 weight pressing down onto a real seat. Her chest is solid; you can almost feel her breathe. Look, too, at
 how the thin fabric of her dress pulls across her knees — Giotto is observing how cloth behaves over a
 real form, not drawing a pattern. Then look at the throne: it isn't a flat gold shape, it's a piece of Gothic
 architecture, with a canopy and steps and sides that genuinely turn and recede, a structure you could
 imagine walking around. And look at the angels and saints: instead of stacking up the sides like
 Cimabue's, the front ones kneel with their backs partly to us, overlapping each other, crowding into a
 shared space — they take up room. One angel hands up a crown; another offers flowers. They behave
 like people in a place.

 That is the whole revolution, standing in one panel. Giotto threw out the assumption that a sacred image
 had to be a flat, timeless pattern, and began painting holy figures as if they occupied the same physical,
 weighty world we do. Two centuries later Vasari credited him with bringing painting back to nature
 after the long medieval winter, and standing between these three walls you can feel that's no
 exaggeration — you can almost date the birth of modern painting to this one panel. Everything ahead,
 on this whole floor — perspective, anatomy, real bodies in real rooms — starts from the decision Giotto
 makes right here. When you've let it land, walk on to the next room, where Siena answers back in colour
 and gold. Play the next track at the doorway.

 and tempestuous of all the great Venetian painters, and he was proud of it.

 He reportedly pinned a motto up on the wall of his studio, promising to combine the drawing of
 Michelangelo with the colour of Titian — the two supreme and supposedly opposite ambitions of Italian
 art, the Florentine line and the Venetian glow, claimed at a single stroke by one restless Venetian. What
 he actually delivered was speed, energy and pure theatre: enormous canvases painted at lightning pace,
 figures flung and tumbling through deep, shadowy, rushing space, sudden flashes of light tearing across
 the dark, and daring, vertiginous viewpoints that pitch you headlong into the middle of the action rather
 than letting you watch from a calm distance. He worked so fast and took on such vast commissions that
 rivals grumbled he was undercutting them; the energy was part temperament, part business.

 Look here for that restless movement and stagey, dramatic lighting, and for the rough, rapid, almost
 slashing energy of his brush, so different from Titian's mellow richness. The serene balance of the High
 Renaissance is long gone now, replaced by something that already feels like the coming Baroque storm.
 Take it in — this is painting as performance — then carry on. Take your time, then play the next track in
 the room ahead.

    Why Veronese is the painter of Venetian splendour, his run-in with the Inquisition, and what to enjoy.


 Paolo Veronese is the painter of pure Venetian splendour, and this room is a feast — quite literally,
 because he loved to stage even the most sacred subjects as glittering, well-dressed banquets.

 Where Tintoretto gives you drama and engulfing shadow, Veronese gives you light, silver, rose and
 gold, and sheer worldly magnificence: grand classical architecture, shimmering silks and velvets,
 handsome aristocratic crowds, faithful dogs and musicians, everything bathed in a cool, pearly, even
 daylight. He painted the good life of the richest city in Europe and calmly lifted it to the level of the
 divine. That love of luxury once landed him in real trouble: hauled before the Inquisition to explain why
 he had filled a painting of the Last Supper with dogs, dwarfs, drunkards and German soldiers, he was
 ordered to remove them — and instead simply renamed the enormous picture Feast in the House of Levi
 and carried serenely on, an early artist quietly insisting on his own freedom.

 Look here for that unmistakable shimmer of fabric and light, the soaring painted architecture, the festive
 crowd, and the cool silvery key of his colour, quite unlike anyone else's. Enjoy the magnificence — this
 is the high noon of Venetian painting, the last great blaze before the century turns — then move on
 toward the last of the Venetian spaces. Whenever you're ready, the next track picks up in the room that

 Caravaggio — the Shock of the Real
    WHERE YOU ARE
    The Caravaggio room — darker walls, dramatic spotlighting — holding several of his works and paintings by

    WHAT YOU SEE
    The drama of the early Baroque: Caravaggio's Medusa, painted on a shield, his Sacrifice of Isaac, and nearby

 You've reached the seventeenth century, and the most revolutionary painter since Giotto: Michelangelo
 Merisi da Caravaggio. Stop and reset your eye, because everything is about to get darker, harsher and
 far more real.

 Caravaggio threw out idealisation altogether. Where the Renaissance gave you perfect bodies in clear,
 even light, he gave you real people — dirty feet, weathered faces, models pulled off the street —
 slammed by a single harsh beam of light out of deep, engulfing shadow. That violent contrast of light
 and dark is called tenebrism, and it made his paintings hit like a blow. He was as notorious in life as in
 art: a brawler who killed a man and died on the run, all before forty. And he changed European painting
 overnight; a whole generation of 'Caravaggisti' across Italy and the north copied his drama.

 This room and its neighbours hold the proof. There are tracks for the two paintings to give your time to
 here — his Medusa, painted on a shield, and Artemisia Gentileschi's ferocious Judith. Nearby hangs his
 Sacrifice of Isaac, too. Start with the Medusa. Play the next track in front of it.

    LISTEN FOR
    Why it's painted on a shield, the impossible moment it captures, and whose face it might be.


 This is Caravaggio's Medusa, and the first strange thing about it is what it's painted on: not a flat panel
 but a convex wooden parade shield, curving out toward you. Caravaggio used that curve as part of the
 illusion — the head seems to bulge and lunge into your space, leaping off the surface, so the object that
 should protect you instead attacks.

 In the myth, anyone who looked directly at the Gorgon Medusa was turned to stone, until the hero
 Perseus beheaded her by using his polished shield as a mirror, so he never met her gaze head-on.
 Caravaggio paints the most impossible instant of all: the very moment of decapitation. Look at the face
 — the mouth wrenched open in a full scream, the eyes flung wide with shock, the brow knotted in
 disbelief. Blood jets in thick, dark ropes from the severed neck, painted with a butcher's accuracy. The
 hair is a writhing nest of green snakes, each one alive, twisting, hissing in different directions. And here
 is the unbearable cleverness of it: Caravaggio catches her in the split second when she is already
 beheaded but not yet dead — still conscious, still horrified, watching her own death arrive. It's a scream
 frozen at the very edge of extinction.

 There's a long tradition that the agonised face is Caravaggio's own, that he studied himself in a mirror
 and painted himself as the monster — a private, dark joke entirely in keeping with a man who lived
 violently and died on the run. The shield was a diplomatic gift, sent to the Medici in Florence, a
 dazzling show-off piece designed to astonish a court that loved both art and weapons. It works on you
 exactly as intended: revulsion and wonder in the same instant. Stand close and let the convex surface do
 its trick, watch the head seem to swell toward you, then turn to Artemisia's Judith and play the next
 track.

 This is Artemisia Gentileschi's Judith Slaying Holofernes — and it is one of the most powerful paintings
 in the entire gallery, by one of the very few women able to build a major artistic career in the
 seventeenth century.

 The biblical story is a heroic one: the widow Judith saves her besieged people by going to the tent of the
 enemy general Holofernes, letting him drink himself into a stupor, and beheading him in his sleep.
 Many male painters had treated it almost decorously — a serene, beautiful Judith holding a tidy sword,
 the deed already politely done. Artemisia paints the act itself, in progress, as brutal physical labour.
 Look at how the two women work together: Judith leans in with her whole body weight, sleeves pushed
 up her strong forearms, both hands driving the blade as she saws through the neck, her face set with
 concentration rather than triumph. The maidservant — young and powerful, not the usual old crone —
 pins the thrashing Holofernes down with both arms across his chest. Blood jets upward in arcs and soaks
 into the white sheets in dark rivers. Nothing is prettified; everything is weight, force and effort.

 Artemisia learned her dramatic light and shadow — the tenebrism — from Caravaggio's circle and from
 her own father, the painter Orazio. But the raw conviction here is entirely her own. Her early life was
 hard: as a young woman she was raped by a colleague of her father's, and endured a public trial —
 recorded in surviving court documents — during which she herself was tortured with thumbscrews to
 test whether her testimony was true. Viewers have long read that ordeal into the unflinching violence of
 her Judiths, two women overpowering a powerful man with their bare hands. Whether or not you read it
 that way, look at the sheer physical conviction of it, the refusal to look away or to make the horror
 beautiful. It is the work of a painter who knew exactly what she was depicting. When you're ready,
 move to the next room for Caravaggio's Bacchus, and play the next track.

 Painting by Candlelight
    WHERE YOU ARE
    A darkened room of nocturnal scenes lit by candle and lamp — the painters who carried Caravaggio's drama
    across Europe.
    WHAT YOU SEE
    Seventeenth-century 'night pieces': scenes lit by a single hidden candle or lamp, by the international followers
    of Caravaggio — among them the Dutchman Gerrit van Honthorst, known in Italy as Gherardo delle Notti,
    'Gerard of the Nights.'
    LISTEN FOR
    How one painter's idea became a European craze, and what to look for in the dark.


 This deliberately dimmed room is a tribute to influence — to how completely Caravaggio's drama
 spread across the whole of Europe after his death, carried by a generation of painters who turned his
 shadows into a speciality all their own.

 These are night pieces: scenes lit by a single candle, lamp or torch, the flame often half-hidden behind a
 hand or an object, so that the warm light seems to bloom out of the painting itself and model the faces
 gathered around it in glowing gold and deep, velvety shadow. The master of the genre here is the
 Dutchman Gerrit van Honthorst, who worked for years in Italy and so delighted in these effects that the
 Italians affectionately renamed him Gherardo delle Notti — Gerard of the Nights. Look for the trick of
 the concealed light source, for fingers that glow translucent red where the flame shines through them,
 for the intimate hush of figures leaning together around one small fire. It's Caravaggio's harsh tenebrism
 turned tender, warm and domestic.

 These painters, the Caravaggisti, spread that style from Rome to Utrecht to France, making the dramatic
 dark the international language of the early Baroque. Enjoy the warm gloom — let your eyes adjust to it
 — then move toward the very last room of the tour. The next track is in the room that follows — start it
 when you arrive.

 This is the last room of the tour, and a fitting place to end — with the giants of the seventeenth-century
