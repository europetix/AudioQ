[quietly] She's only just arrived. The wind is still blowing, the roses are still falling, and her hair hasn't settled.

This is Venus, goddess of love and beauty, coming ashore on the island of Cyprus. She was born from the foam of the sea, and here she stands on a giant scallop shell. On the left, the wind Zephyr blows her in, wrapped together with a companion, perhaps the breeze Aura. On the right, a young woman hurries to cover her with a cloak scattered with flowers. She may be the Hora of spring, or one of the Graces.

A near-life-size nude goddess was still a rarity in Florence in the 1480s. The subject was perhaps suggested by the poet Agnolo Poliziano. The painting is often read through the Neoplatonic ideas of the Medici circle. [pause] But Botticelli also borrowed. Venus's modest pose comes from ancient statues. And the pair of winds is based on a Hellenistic carved gem that belonged to Lorenzo the Magnificent.

Now notice the surface, pale and matte. This is painted on canvas, not on wood. That wasn't a daring first: canvas was widely used in fifteenth-century Florence for decorative pictures in noble houses, and it helps explain that soft, chalky look. Vasari saw it in 1550 in the Medici villa at Castello, beside the Primavera.

[warmly] Now the two are together again, facing each other across the passage, in new airtight cases instead of the old glass screens. Either side of Venus hangs a round Madonna. Look from the goddess to the Virgin of the Magnificat, and compare the faces. They are strikingly alike.

This room has a short Extra on the round Madonnas beside the Venus and on the Calumny. The next main stop is Room A13, through the doorway at the end of the Botticelli rooms: the big Flemish triptych with the kneeling family.
