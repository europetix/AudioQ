[wry] Botticelli's story in these rooms begins with a job that was never supposed to be his.

In 1469 the merchants' court of Florence, the Tribunale di Mercanzia, ordered a set of seven Virtues for its hall on Piazza della Signoria. The commission went to Piero del Pollaiolo. But one of the men in charge, Tommaso Soderini, steered one panel to the young Botticelli. Pollaiolo protested, and Botticelli was allowed this single figure. The museum calls the protests understandable.

Find her: Fortitude, the young woman wearing armour over her graceful dress, with a ruler's sceptre in her hand. She's dated 1470, Botticelli's first documented work. Her body is long and sinuous, her face languid and melancholy. You'll meet that face again. Even the wood is different. Pollaiolo's Virtues are on cypress, hers is on poplar. Compare Pollaiolo's Charity: a young mother feeding a child, a small flame between her fingers.

[pause] These rooms have been newly rehung, and the Virtues are back in their nineteenth-century gilded frames. From here the Botticellis follow his life in order. The sequence opens with his Adoration of the Magi. The Primavera and the Birth of Venus face each other across a passage, in two jewel-box rooms. And it ends with his Calumny, from the Florence of Savonarola.

The next Botticelli room has a short Extra on his Adoration of the Magi, the painting full of Medici faces. The next main stop is the Primavera: the wide, dark orange grove with nine figures, a little further on. Stay in the Botticelli rooms.
