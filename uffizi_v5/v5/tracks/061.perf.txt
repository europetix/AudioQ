[lightly] After so many rooms of paint, here is a space with hardly any paintings at all. That's rather the point.

The Uffizi calls the Verone a covered loggia on the Arno. Its walls are a very pale, elegant green, and it gathers ancient and Renaissance statues. Three of the biggest came from the Villa Medici in Rome, the family's grand house in that city. This is a good place to rest your eyes, and your feet.

Look for Silenus cradling the infant Bacchus. In the myths, Silenus was the old companion and teacher of the wine god, usually shown drunk and cheerful. Here he is something gentler: a foster father holding a baby. The museum presents the statue under a single word, fatherhood. The Sicilian sculptor Jacopo del Duca made this version between 1571 and 1574, copying an ancient Roman original.

[warmly] Keep that baby in mind. In a few minutes you'll meet him again, grown up, in a painting by Caravaggio, holding out a glass of wine.

[pause] Behind you now is the whole sixteenth century: Lombardy, Ferrara and Bologna, the Florentine court of Bronzino, and Venice with Titian and Veronese. Ahead is the seventeenth century, and a painter who changed everything with a beam of light and a great deal of darkness.

At the end of the Verone, turn left along the corridor. The first room, E4, is Caravaggio's. Look for the round shield with Medusa's screaming head.
