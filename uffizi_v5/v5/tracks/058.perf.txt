[warmly] Long before the Venus of Urbino, Titian painted this young woman. She looks out of the dark, around 1517, with a few spring flowers in her hand.

Her hair gave a colour its name. That copper-blonde, falling loose over her shoulders, is the shade the world still calls Titian red. Her white robe, cut in an ancient style, is slipping off her right shoulder. In her hand are violets, rosebuds and jasmine.

She is called Flora, the nymph who became the bride of Zephyr, the west wind. You may remember her from Botticelli's Primavera upstairs. But the museum reads her less as a goddess than as an image of beauty itself, modest and sensual at the same time. Venetian painters loved this kind of picture. They are known simply as the Beauties.

[amused] She also travelled. Around 1635 she was in Amsterdam, in the collection of a Spanish ambassador. He sold her to an archduke of Austria. Then in 1793 the imperial gallery in Vienna exchanged her with the grand dukes of Tuscany, and she came to Florence. Engravers had been copying her since the sixteenth century.

From here, the next main stop is Room D26, Veronese. Go through Room D24 and Room D25, the Tintoretto room, and into D26. Look for the tall portrait of a father with his hand on his young son's shoulder.
