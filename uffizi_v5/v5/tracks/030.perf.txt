[curious] Nobody knows who ordered this painting, or where it first hung. It reached the Uffizi in 1867, from a small church just outside the city gates. What we do know is that Leonardo was about twenty, and still working in Verrocchio's studio.

The scene is the Annunciation. The angel Gabriel kneels on the left and holds out a lily. The Virgin, on the right, looks up from her book. Between them is an enclosed garden, the old symbol of her purity, in front of a palace.

Now look the way Leonardo looked. Start with the angel's wings. They aren't the usual decorative feathers. The museum says they're based on the wings of a mighty bird of prey. Then the lawn under the angel, full of separate flowers, each one observed. Then the far distance, past the dark trees, where everything fades into a pale, misty blue.

[pause] Look at the marble lectern in front of the Virgin. It copies a design by his master Verrocchio, inspired by the tomb of Piero de' Medici, known as Piero the Gouty, in the church of San Lorenzo. A real Florentine monument, slipped into a sacred scene.

[lightly] Then the famous problem. Look at the Virgin's right arm, reaching for the book. It seems too long. Was the young painter careless? Perhaps not. The museum suggests a viewpoint from the right, because the panel once hung over a side altar. So take a few steps to your right, and look again. See whether the arm settles.

He was already testing how our eyes really see. In his early twenties, he was asking questions no one around him thought to ask.
