[curious] For more than thirty years, this was not a famous Caravaggio. It was a piece of parade equipment in the Medici armoury, and nobody had written down who painted it.

In 1598 Cardinal Francesco Maria del Monte, Caravaggio's patron in Rome, gave the painted shield to Grand Duke Ferdinando the First. It went into the Uffizi armoury without any attribution. Only in 1631 was it recorded as a work by Caravaggio. And it was put on show as part of a suit of Persian armour, worn by a mannequin sitting on a wooden horse. [amused] Picture walking into the armoury and meeting that rider, with this head on his arm.

It was a well-chosen gift. Medusa was the Gorgon whose gaze turned people to stone. The hero Perseus cut off her head, and the goddess Athena set it on her shield, so its power would freeze her enemies. The Medici liked the image as a sign of their own military strength.

[pause] Now look at the face. Caravaggio has painted the moment between life and death. The head is already cut off, and she is still alive enough to be horrified by it. Her mouth is wide open. Her eyes stare. Blood pours from the neck, and the snakes in her hair are still writhing. He painted it on canvas stretched over a wooden shield, so the head sits on the curve and seems to push out toward you.

[conspiratorial] There's a long tradition that the face is Caravaggio's own, studied in a mirror. Nobody can prove it. But remember how Perseus survived. He never looked at Medusa directly, only at her reflection in his polished shield. A painter watching his own scream in a mirror, and putting it on a shield, would be a very Caravaggio kind of joke.
