[quietly] In Rome in 1606, Caravaggio killed a man named Ranuccio Tomassoni. He spent the rest of his life on the run, and he died in 1610, aged thirty-eight.

In a career that short, he changed European painting. He took real faces, ordinary and lined, and struck them with a hard light out of deep darkness. That way of painting is called tenebrism, from the Italian word for shadows. Painters all over Europe copied it, and they became known as the Caravaggisti. This room and the three after it follow them.

Start with the Sacrifice of Isaac. God has ordered Abraham to kill his son, and the old man has the boy pinned down. At the last instant an angel grips his wrist, hard, and points to the ram that will die instead. [pause] Look at that hand on the wrist. The whole story turns on it. Behind them stretches a wide, calm landscape of hills and a village. The subject was chosen by a powerful churchman, Maffeo Barberini, who later became Pope Urban the Eighth.

[wry] It came to the Uffizi as a gift from the collector John Fairfax Murray. He had bought it believing it was by another painter entirely, Gherardo delle Notti. You'll meet him two rooms from here.

Guido Reni was in Rome in the same years, and he borrowed Caravaggio's darkness without his violence. His David poses like a dandy in a red feathered hat, calmly considering the giant's head. Artemisia Gentileschi's Judith hangs in this room too.

Stay in Room E4 for Caravaggio's Medusa, the screaming head painted on a round shield.
