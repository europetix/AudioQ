[curious] For a long time, this was where the story began: Cimabue, the grand old master, and then everyone else. The museum's own dating has quietly changed that.

The Uffizi calls Cimabue the most important Florentine painter of the thirteenth century. He painted this for the Vallombrosan monks of Santa Trinita, a church a few streets from here. Today it's dated to about 1290 to 1300, late in his career. That makes it later than Duccio's Madonna in this same room.

[pause] Read it from the bottom up. Under the throne, in four arches, stand four Old Testament prophets: Jeremiah, Abraham, David and Isaiah. Their scrolls carry texts about the Incarnation and Mary's virginity. Above them rises an ivory throne, built up like a piece of architecture. Eight angels with wonderful many-coloured wings lift it gently on either side.

The Virgin's right hand points to her son. It's an old Byzantine formula. She is showing you the way to salvation. The Child is dressed like an ancient philosopher, blessing, with a rolled scroll in his hand. Their robes are traced with fine lines of gold called damascene, and even the gold background is engraved with geometric patterns.

[lightly] Now look again, at the folds of the cloth, the soft shadows, the gentle faces. That is a new naturalism. The museum links it to younger painters, to Duccio, and above all to Giotto, who was Cimabue's pupil.

Vasari tells how Cimabue found a shepherd boy drawing sheep on a stone and took him on as an apprentice. That boy was Giotto. [warmly] Whether or not it happened, the pupil's painting is waiting in this same room.

Stay in Room A4 for Duccio's Rucellai Madonna, the largest of the three, with six kneeling angels holding the throne.
