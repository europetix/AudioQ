[curious] Before it was a corridor, this was a loggia, open to the air above the Piazzale. And that may be where the word "gallery" comes from.

Vasari designed three of these corridors, east, south and west, running round the top of the building. The museum says the word gallery is widely believed to come from this very setting. When Francesco de' Medici began to fill it in the 1580s, this eastern stretch was the whole gallery. It was his private collection, for himself and his court.

He filled it with the family's ancient sculpture. Many of these figures are ancient Roman copies of lost Greek originals, and pairs of statues face each other between the windows. In the eighteenth century this run of marble was one of the great sights of the Grand Tour. Scholars and art lovers came from all over Europe to walk where you are walking now.

[pause] Then look up. The ceiling is covered in grotesques: scrolls, masks, little creatures and garlands. The first bay was painted in 1581 for Francesco, and the date is painted on the vault. The other corridors took the next two centuries. [lightly] The odd name comes from Rome. Renaissance painters climbed down into the buried rooms of Nero's Golden House and copied what they found. Those underground rooms were called grottoes, so the style became grotesque.

Here's a simple rule that will carry you through the whole museum. The windows look over the courtyard. The painting rooms always open on the other side, away from the windows.

Room A3 is the very first doorway, at the start of the corridor, right beside the vestibule you came through, on the side away from the windows. Look for a tall painted cross on gold.
