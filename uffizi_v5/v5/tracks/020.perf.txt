[curious] In 1483, a ship from Flanders unloaded a painting at Pisa that would make every painter in Florence stop and stare. This is the painting.

Tommaso Portinari ran the Medici bank in Bruges, and around 1477 he ordered this huge altarpiece from Hugo van der Goes. It was for his family's chapel in Sant'Egidio, the church of the hospital of Santa Maria Nuova, here in Florence. It came by sea, and the museum says Florentine painters found it extraordinarily new. They had never seen oil paint handled like this, on this scale.

The family kneel on the wings, in Flemish clothes. On the left are Tommaso and his sons, Antonio and Pigello, with Saint Thomas and Saint Anthony looming behind them. On the right are his wife, Maria Baroncelli, and their daughter Margherita, with Saint Margaret and Mary Magdalene. Notice the sizes. The saints tower over the family. Size here means rank, not distance.

[pause] Now the centre. The Child lies on the bare ground in rays of light, the Light of the World. In front of him stands a still life. A white iris and a red lily, for purity and for Christ's blood. Purple columbines, for the Virgin's sorrows. Red carnations, for the Trinity. Every petal is exact.

Then look to the right of the centre, at the shepherds. Rough, open-mouthed working men, hurrying in from the fields. [wry] Florence noticed them. Ghirlandaio's Adoration in the Sassetti Chapel, painted two years later, seems to borrow these very shepherds.

[warmly] A landmark in Florence's encounter with Flemish oil painting, and it began as one family's chapel picture.

Room A14, the Room of the Maps, opening off this room, has a short Extra on maps and mathematics. The next main stop is the Tribune, Room A16, the octagonal room just beyond Room A15: look for the red walls and the mother-of-pearl dome.
