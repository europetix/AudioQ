[amused] On the back of this little canvas, an old inscription gives the painter's nickname: "Michelangelo of the Bambocciate".

Michelangelo Cerquozzi worked in Rome. A bambocciata was a small scene of everyday life, a fashion named after a painter from the north nicknamed Il Bamboccio. Here, in the 1640s, he turns the same eye on himself.

He's at his easel in a bare studio. In front of him an old man, stripped to the waist, kneels at a lectern. He's a model, posing as Saint Jerome. [pause] Now look round the room. A marble bust lies on the floor, there to be copied. A brazier of glowing coals warms the bare room. And on the wall hangs a painting of the Good Thief on his cross. He liked ordinary Romans as models, the way Caravaggio did.

It's a self-portrait that shows you not just a face, but a working day.

The next rooms follow the same thread: C4 is called Showing the Artwork, and C5 reaches the early eighteenth century.

Walk on along C4 and C5, then follow the museum's route straight on through the rooms being rehung, C6 to C12, to Room D1. In D1, go through to Room D4, past D2, for the Madonna with the impossibly long neck. If a barrier stops you, ask a warden for Room D1.
