[quietly] Rembrandt painted this old man in 1665, four years before his own death. By then he had lost a great deal.

His wife Saskia had died in 1642, and in 1656 he went bankrupt. He kept on painting, and his late portraits are among his deepest.

The picture is known as the Old Rabbi. The face has been identified as a rabbi's, and a name has been suggested, though the documents don't confirm it. Rembrandt lived for many years in Amsterdam's Jewish quarter, and he often asked neighbours and acquaintances to sit for him. So this may simply be a man he knew.

[pause] Look at how it's painted. The colour is laid on thick, and you can see the texture of the paint. The old man isn't posing for you. He seems lost in his own thoughts, almost swallowed by the dark around him.

The painting has travelled too. Napoleon's officials noticed how good it was, and it was carried off to Paris. It came back to Florence after his fall.

The room also carries two Flemish names. Peter Paul Rubens was the great painter of Catholic Antwerp, all movement and warm flesh. Anthony van Dyck began as his brilliant assistant and went on to paint the courts of Europe.

[warmly] After so much splendour, it's fitting to end the paintings with a quiet face.
