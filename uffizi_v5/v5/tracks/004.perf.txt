[curious] Dante used two of the painters in this room as a warning about pride.

In his Purgatory, on the terrace where the proud are purged, the illuminator Oderisi tells the poet how quickly fame fades. In Longfellow's translation: "In painting Cimabue thought that he should hold the field, now Giotto has the cry, so that the other's fame is growing dim."

Both men are here. So is a third great painter, Duccio, from Siena. All three painted the same subject: the Virgin and Child enthroned in majesty, a Maestà. All three are gigantic, from about three and a quarter metres to four and a half. On the museum's own dates, they were painted within about twenty years of each other, from the mid twelve-eighties to the early thirteen hundreds. And the earliest is not Cimabue's. It's Duccio's.

[pause] So don't think of an old master followed by his pupils in a neat line. Think of three different approaches, side by side. To compare them, look at three things. First, the throne: is it a flat shape, or can you feel space inside it? Then the angels: are they a pattern, or a crowd? And last, the bodies: do they float, or do they sit with real weight?

Take your time between them. Every painter on this floor learned something from what happened in this room.

[wry] Dante thought fame was fleeting. Seven centuries on, all three names still draw a crowd.

Stay in Room A4. Start with Cimabue's panel: the one with four prophets in arches under the throne.
