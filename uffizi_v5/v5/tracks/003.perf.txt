[reverent] This is the oldest room in the gallery. Its great cross was painted more than eight hundred years ago, and nobody knows who painted it, or which church it came from.

The museum names its maker simply as a Pisan painter, perhaps a master working in Pisa in the middle of the twelfth century. Even that date is very uncertain. Pisa was then a rich sea power, trading right across the Mediterranean.

Look at Christ. His eyes are open and his head is raised. He isn't suffering. He is triumphant over death, which was the usual way to paint him in Italy until the early twelve hundreds. Around him, small scenes tell the story of the Passion and what came after: the washing of the disciples' feet, the arrest, the way to Calvary, the entombment, the descent into Limbo. They read from top to bottom and left to right, like a strip of pictures.

[pause] And everything sits on gold. Here gold isn't decoration. It stands for divine light, for a heaven that never changes, an idea inherited from the Byzantine painting of the Christian East. The figures face you straight on. Nobody has weight. Nobody stands in a space you could step into. These were images for prayer, glowing by candlelight in a dark church.

[warmly] Hold on to three things: flat gold, frontal figures, no weight. In the next room, three enormous Madonnas begin to pull that tradition apart.

Go back out to the corridor and walk back past the vestibule you came in by, to the next doorway on the same side: Room A4, the big room with three giant gold Madonnas.
