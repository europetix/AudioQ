[curious] For years, this painting sat in the Uffizi's storerooms, and nobody knew what it was. Then in 1913 it was found, and the art historian Roberto Longhi gave it back to Caravaggio.

It's an early work, from his first years in Rome, when he lived under the protection of Cardinal del Monte, the man who sent the Medusa to Florence. According to the museum, the cardinal gave this one to Grand Duke Ferdinando for the wedding of his son Cosimo.

So here is Bacchus, god of wine, crowned with vine leaves. He holds out a glass toward you, as if inviting you to drink. [pause] Now look a little closer. The fruit in the bowl is not all perfect. Some of it is already bruised and turning. And look at his hands. The fingernails are dirty. [amused] He is a god. He is also, quite clearly, a real young man posing, with a heavy, slightly drunken look.

The body follows classical models, the ancient portraits of Antinous, the beautiful youth loved by the emperor Hadrian. Some critics read the wine and fruit as an invitation to friendship and simple pleasures. Others see a celebration of the freedom of the senses. And the fruit on the turn may be a quiet reminder that ripeness does not last.

[conspiratorial] There is one more secret, and you won't be able to see it. When the painting was cleaned in 1922, a tiny reflection appeared in the glass carafe on the table. In 2009, infrared imaging showed what it was: a man in the clothes of the time, very probably the painter himself. It cannot be seen with the naked eye, so don't strain for it.

[warmly] Caravaggio offers you the glass. And somewhere in the carafe, he stays to watch you take it.
