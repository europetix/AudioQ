[quietly] Some of the halos in this painting are partly hidden, by other heads and by the throne itself.

That small fact is the revolution. If one thing can hide another, there is space between them. Giotto painted this around 1300 to 1305 for the church of Ognissanti, All Saints, served by the monks of the Humiliati order. Nobody knows exactly where it stood inside the church.

Mary sits like a queen. Around her rises a pointed tabernacle of inlaid, many-coloured marble, a piece of Gothic architecture you could almost walk into. The saints and angels stand inside it, and they overlap. [pause] Now look at her body. Giotto keeps the decoration on her clothes to a minimum, so that light and shade can do the work. You can sense her knees and her lap, and the weight settling into the seat.

The angels have jobs. The two kneeling at the foot of the throne offer vases of roses and lilies, for charity and purity. The angels beside the throne hold out a crown and a pyx, a small lidded vessel that probably points to Christ's Passion. The Child raises his hand in blessing and holds a rolled scroll.

Vasari tells us that Giotto brought painting back to nature. That's a writer looking back two centuries later. The museum's own verdict is just as strong: for over a century, this composition was a model for Florentine painters. You can almost date modern painting from this panel.

[wry] Remember Dante's line, that Giotto now "has the cry". [pause] Standing here, it's hard to argue.
