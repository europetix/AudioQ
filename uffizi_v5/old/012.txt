 The First Renaissance Painters — Masaccio,
 Angelico, Uccello
 WHERE YOU ARE
 The early-Renaissance rooms after the Gothic, where the gold grounds drop away and daylight, weight and
 real space take over.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 Florentine painting of the early 1400s tackling the new problems head-on: Masaccio's gravity, Fra Angelico's
 lit depth, and Paolo Uccello's obsessive perspective in his great battle panel. Smaller predella scenes and
 altarpieces line the walls.
 LISTEN FOR
 What changed the instant the gold came down, who these three painters were, and the painting to walk up to
 first.


 You've crossed a real threshold. The gold grounds are behind you now, and the painters in this room are
 wrestling with the single great problem of the new century: how to set solid bodies into real,
 measurable, daylit space.

 This is the generation that took Giotto's breakthrough and turned it into a method. Masaccio — dead at
 twenty-six, but the most important painter of his moment — gives his figures real gravity, real shadow,
 the feeling of standing on the ground. Fra Angelico, a Dominican friar, keeps the gold and the sweetness
 but builds real architectural depth behind his saints. And Paolo Uccello became genuinely obsessed with
 perspective, the new geometry for making a flat panel look deep — to the point, Vasari says, of staying
 up all night over his vanishing points.

 Look around for Masaccio and Masolino's Madonna and Child with Saint Anne, and for Fra Angelico's
 jewel-bright work, before you settle. One honest note on this corner: the museum has been rehanging it,
 and the next rooms along, numbers ten to twelve, are closed for renovation — so the route jumps ahead
 shortly. But first, the painting that announces the whole century: go and stand in front of Uccello's
 Battle of San Romano, and play the next track.
