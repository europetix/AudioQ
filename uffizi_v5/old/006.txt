 Duccio — Rucellai Madonna
 WHERE YOU ARE
 In front of Duccio's Maestà, on the left-hand wall — the largest of the three panels, with six angels framing
 the throne.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 The Virgin enthroned, the Child raising his hand in blessing, six kneeling angels holding the sides and corners
 of a slender Gothic throne draped in gold-edged cloth. The frame carries small roundels of saints. Tempera
 and gold, dated 1285 — the largest 13th-century panel in the room.
 LISTEN FOR
 How the Sienese answer differs from the Florentine one, the detective story of its authorship, and the detail
 that is pure Duccio.


 This is the Rucellai Madonna, painted in twelve eighty-five by Duccio di Buoninsegna — and it carries
 a small detective story. It was commissioned by a religious confraternity for a chapel in the church of
 Santa Maria Novella, and for centuries everyone assumed it was a Cimabue. Only in the twentieth
 century did scholars settle that it was Duccio's — a reattribution that reshaped our whole understanding
 of both men and of the Sienese school he founded.

 It's the biggest panel in the room, and the most graceful. Where Cimabue is grave and architectural,
 Duccio is a master of line. Look first at the throne: instead of a heavy structure, it's a slender, delicate
 thing, and six angels kneel around it, leaning in to hold its sides and corners as if it weighed almost
 nothing — three down each side, their wings overlapping in a soft rhythm. Then look at the edge of the
 Virgin's deep blue cloak: it's trimmed with a flowing band of gold that ripples and curves down the
 whole length of the figure. Follow that gold hem with your eye — it's drawing as music, a line that
 exists for sheer beauty. No Florentine would have lavished such care on a decorative edge.

 And look at her face: softer than Cimabue's, more inward, almost sad — a real human tenderness inside
 all the formality. Notice, too, the little roundels of saints set into the frame, like a border of jewels
 around the whole image. This is the Gothic line at its most refined: beauty offered as a form of devotion.
 Siena would follow this road of elegance and colour for the next century, while Florence took the other
 one — which is the road the painting in the centre of the room is about to choose, head-on. Turn to the
 Giotto in the centre, and play the next track.
