 Caravaggio — Bacchus
 WHERE YOU ARE
 The adjoining room holding Caravaggio's Bacchus, an early and very different work from the Medusa.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 Caravaggio's Bacchus: a young god of wine reclining, offering a shallow glass of red wine toward the viewer,
 a wreath of vine leaves in his hair and a dish of fruit before him — sensual, ambiguous, startlingly real, with
 the fruit beginning to spoil.
 LISTEN FOR
 How to read this very different Caravaggio, and the track to play in front of it.


 After the violence of the last room, the Bacchus is a quieter shock — early Caravaggio in a sensual,
 teasing, ambiguous mood.

 A young man, crowned with vine leaves, reclines and offers you a shallow glass of red wine, holding it
 out by its stem as though inviting you to drink with him. He's meant to be Bacchus, the god of wine —
 but he's unmistakably a real, slightly flushed Roman youth, a model, not an idealised deity, with grubby
 fingernails and a heavy, languid body. The famous Caravaggio realism is all here. And look at the bowl
 of fruit in front of him: ripe, lush, but with pieces already bruised, spotted, beginning to rot — a quiet
 reminder, even in this scene of pleasure, that beauty and ripeness don't last.
