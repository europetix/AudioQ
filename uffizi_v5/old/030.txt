 Leonardo da Vinci — The Adoration of the Magi
 (Unfinished)
 WHERE YOU ARE
 In front of the large square Adoration — a brown, monochrome, clearly unfinished panel, utterly unlike the
 polished pictures around it.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 A near-square panel left at the underpainting stage: the Virgin and Child at the calm centre, ringed by a
 swirling, agitated crowd of adoring and gesturing figures, with rearing horses, fighting men and a ruined
 classical building behind. All in warm brown underpaint, ghostly and unresolved. Begun 1481, abandoned
 the next year.
 LISTEN FOR
 Why an unfinished painting is one of the most revealing things in the whole museum.


 Don't walk past this brown, ghostly, obviously unfinished panel — it's one of the most revealing objects
 in the entire gallery, precisely because it was never completed.

 Leonardo began it in fourteen eighty-one, commissioned by the monks of San Donato a Scopeto just
 outside Florence, then left for Milan the next year and never came back to finish it. (The monks
 eventually had Filippino Lippi paint them a replacement — the very Adoration you saw a few rooms
 back.) What's left is the underpainting, the stage before the colour goes on, and it lets you see straight
 into how Leonardo's mind worked. At the calm centre sit the Virgin and Child. But around them,
 everything churns: a swirling ring of figures leaning in, reaching, recoiling, shading their eyes, pointing,
 overwhelmed by the miracle in front of them. This is something genuinely new — not a static, orderly
 Adoration like Gentile's gorgeous procession upstairs, but a single crowd caught in one charged
 emotional instant, all of human wonder turned toward one point.

 Then look at the background, which Leonardo never tamed: rearing, battling horses, fighting men, and a
 ruined classical building with empty staircases climbing to nowhere. He worked out its perspective in a
 meticulous separate drawing, then piled in far more than any painting could resolve — which is part of
 why it defeated him. You can actually see him drawing, redrawing, searching, changing his mind in the
 wet ground itself. A finished masterpiece hides all that struggle behind a perfect surface; this one shows
 you the genius mid-thought, mid-doubt, and that is a far rarer thing to witness. Onward to the next room,
 and play the next track there.
