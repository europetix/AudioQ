 Cimabue — Santa Trinita Maestà
 WHERE YOU ARE
 In front of Cimabue's Maestà, on the right-hand wall — a towering panel close to four metres tall, so step
 back and look up.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 The Virgin enthroned with the Child on her knee, against gold. Four angels stack symmetrically up each side
 of an elaborate inlaid throne; beneath it, in arched openings, four Old Testament prophets hold scrolls.
 Tempera and gold on a single great wooden panel, about 1280.
 LISTEN FOR
 What belongs to the old world here, the one thing reaching toward the new, and the story that links it across
 the room.


 Start with Cimabue, painted around twelve eighty for the church of Santa Trinita — and look at it as the
 most traditional of the three, because that's exactly what makes it the right place to begin.

 Almost everything here belongs to the old Byzantine manner. The gold ground. The strict symmetry.
 The angels stacked up the sides in tidy tiers, more like a repeating pattern than a crowd of living beings.
 The Virgin herself is long and slender and weightless, her robe ridged with fine lines of gold — those
 gold striations standing in for the way light would fall, rather than real, observed shadow. Cimabue was
 the most celebrated painter in Florence in his day, the summit of a way of working centuries old.

 But look closer, at three things. First, the faces — there's a softness, a gentle shading around the eyes
 and cheeks, a reach toward something more human than the tradition strictly required. Second, look
 down at the base of the throne, at the four prophets in their arched niches: Cimabue is trying to make
 that architecture turn and recede, to seat them in a real structure with depth. It doesn't fully work — the
 space is more diagram than room — but the ambition is there. Third, look at the throne itself, at how he
 tilts its sides inward to suggest it stands in space; it's a first, halting attempt at the very thing Giotto,
 across this room, will solve completely.

 Cimabue is often called the last great painter of the old world. There's an old story, told by Vasari, that
 he was the teacher who found a shepherd boy sketching sheep on a rock and took him on — and that
 boy was Giotto, whose painting hangs across this room. Whether or not it happened, the room is hung to
 tell exactly that story: the master, and the pupil who would overtake him. Now turn to the left-hand
 wall, to Duccio's Madonna, and play the next track.
