 The Self-Portraits
 WHERE YOU ARE
 Entering the suite of self-portrait rooms — walls painted a warm pink, a marble statue of a seated cardinal
 welcoming you at the threshold.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 Part of the largest collection of artists' self-portraits in the world — over a thousand painters looking back at
 themselves across five centuries, from Renaissance masters to living artists, in a sequence of bright pink
 rooms named for the cardinal who began it.
 LISTEN FOR
 Where this strange, intimate collection came from, why the rooms are pink, and how to take them in.


 This is one of the most unusual and intimate experiences in the whole museum: room after room of
 artists looking back at themselves. The Uffizi owns the largest collection of self-portraits in the world
 — well over a thousand of them — and a rotating selection hangs here.

 It began with one man, the seventeenth-century Cardinal Leopoldo de' Medici, a passionate collector
 who started gathering painters' self-portraits as a kind of gallery of genius — the actual faces of the
 people who made the art. That's his statue greeting you at the entrance, and the walls are painted pink in
 a nod to his cardinal's robes. For a century and a half the self-portraits hung in the famous Vasari
 Corridor over the river, where almost no one could see them; only recently were they brought here, into
 the main route, properly lit and cared for, so that for the first time you can walk among them.

 Take them slowly, because each is a double encounter — you're looking at a painting, and a painter is
 looking back, having chosen exactly how they wished to be seen. The collection runs from the
 Renaissance to artists alive today, and it rotates, so no two visits are quite the same. The next track
 points out some of the faces to find. Walk on, and play it in the next room.
