 LISTEN FOR
 Why the landscape is the real subject, the meaning of the lion, and what Bellini does with light that Florence
 couldn't.


 This is Giovanni Bellini's Saint Jerome in the Desert, from around fourteen eighty — small, but one of
 the finest things in the whole collection, and a lesson in what Venetian painting could do that Florentine
 painting couldn't.

 The nominal subject is Saint Jerome, the scholar-hermit who withdrew to the desert to translate the
 Bible into Latin — you see him seated on the rocks, absorbed in his book, his cardinal's red laid aside on
 the stone. Look for his lion: the legend says Jerome drew a thorn from a wild lion's paw, and the grateful
 beast became his lifelong companion, so the lion is how you recognise him in any painting. But the real
 subject here is the landscape and the light. Look how Bellini opens up an enormous, wholly believable
 world behind the saint — crags, a road winding off into the distance, a little fortified town on its hill,
 ranges dissolving into haze — and how the whole scene glows with a soft, even, late-afternoon light that
 seems to rise from the air itself rather than fall from any single sun.

 That marriage of holy figure and radiant, lovingly observed nature is Bellini's great gift to Venice, and
 through him to his pupils Giorgione and Titian. Where a Florentine might have given you Jerome and a
 token rock, sharply outlined, Bellini gives you a whole luminous universe for the saint to be still inside,
 every edge softened by the warm air. Notice how the stillness is total — no wind, no event, just a man
 reading in the gold of evening, the lion dozing, the town going about its business far below. You can
 almost feel the heat held in the stone. It's a painting about peace, and about light as a kind of grace. The
 next room is just ahead — play the next track when you reach it.
