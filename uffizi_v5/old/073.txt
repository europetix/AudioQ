 The End of the Walk
 WHERE YOU ARE
 Wherever you've come to rest — near the exit, on the rooftop terrace, or back out in the loggia. No painting in
 front of you now; just a moment to close the visit.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 The end of the tour. A short look back over everything you've seen, a word on where you are in the story of
 museums, and a few easy ways to carry the day onward into Florence.
 LISTEN FOR
 What you've actually just done, why this place matters beyond its paintings, and three simple onward steps
 from the door.


 You've reached the end — so take a breath, and let it land, because what you've just walked through is
 not only one of the greatest collections of art on earth, it is very nearly the place where the whole idea of
 an art museum was born.

 Look back over the ground you covered. Giotto, teaching painting to breathe, to carry weight and stand
 in real space. Botticelli's drifting goddesses. Leonardo as a young man, Michelangelo's one finished
 panel, Raphael's effortless calm — the High Renaissance at its very summit. Titian's warm Venetian
 light and Bronzino's cool court splendour. And, at the last, Caravaggio's shadow and Rembrandt's old,
 unflinching face. From a flat gold ground to a single candle in the dark: in about two hours you've
 followed the whole long adventure of how human beings taught themselves to see, and to set down what
 they saw.

 Remember the little octagonal Tribune from earlier — the Medici's private treasure-room, built to be
 shown and admired, and in time opened to everyone. That generous idea, born right here, is the reason
 you were able to walk through all of this today. You've been a guest in the first of the great public
 museums.

 Before you drift back into the city, a few easy things from right here. If you haven't yet, ride up to the
 rooftop terrace for a coffee and a view of the Palazzo Vecchio's tower and the great dome of the Duomo.
 The bookshop by the exit is genuinely good. And three short walks: step onto the Ponte Vecchio just
 downstream; linger in the Piazza della Signoria, an open-air sculpture gallery in its own right beneath
 the Loggia dei Lanzi; or stroll five minutes north to stand under the Duomo itself.

 Whatever you do next, try to carry one picture out with you — the single work that stopped you,
 whichever one it was. That one is yours to keep. Thank you for letting me walk the Uffizi with you, and
 enjoy Florence.
