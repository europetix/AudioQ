 Faces Across Five Centuries
 WHERE YOU ARE
 Among the self-portrait rooms, where the great names hang within arm's reach of one another.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 The highlights of the self-portrait collection: Raphael, Rembrandt, Rubens, Velazquez and Bernini among the
 old masters; pioneering women like Sofonisba Anguissola and Lavinia Fontana; and modern faces —
 Chagall, Morandi, even Yayoi Kusama — bringing the gallery of genius up to the present.
 LISTEN FOR
 Which faces to seek out, and what a self-portrait reveals that no other picture can.


 Now slow down and hunt for faces, because the names hanging here are extraordinary — and a
 self-portrait tells you things no other kind of portrait can.

 Among the old masters, look for Raphael, young and serene; Rembrandt, who painted himself
 obsessively from confident youth to ruined, unflinching old age; the swaggering Rubens; the grave,
 watchful Velazquez; and Bernini, whose marble you may have just seen in the Contini rooms, here
 turned to face you in paint. Look, too, for the women who fought their way into a closed profession and
 recorded their own faces with quiet defiance — Sofonisba Anguissola and Lavinia Fontana among
 them, rare female voices from centuries when women were barred from drawing the nude and from
 most formal training. And because the collection is alive and still growing, you'll find modern faces as
 well: Marc Chagall floating before Notre-Dame, the still, severe Giorgio Morandi, and living artists
 right up to Yayoi Kusama, dotted and mirrored into infinity.

 As you look, ask what each painter chose to show: confident or vulnerable, at work at the easel or
 dressed in costume, flattering or brutally honest. A self-portrait is the one picture in which the artist is at
 once the seeing eye and the thing seen, deciding exactly how they wish to meet the gaze of everyone
 who will ever come after. It's a deeply human room to linger in. When you're ready, move on toward the
 early Renaissance rooms nearby, and play the next track.
