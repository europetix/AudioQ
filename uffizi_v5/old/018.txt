 Van der Goes — The Portinari Triptych
 WHERE YOU ARE
 In front of the open triptych — a very wide three-panelled work; the foreground is at roughly eye level, so
 crouch slightly to study it.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 A central Adoration of the Shepherds, the Child laid bare on the ground while weathered shepherds crowd in;
 the kneeling Portinari family with their towering patron saints on the side wings; and across the foreground, a
 sheaf of wheat and a famous still-life of irises, a red lily and columbine in two vessels.
 LISTEN FOR
 The detail Florentine painters couldn't stop studying, the chain it set off, and the cold you can almost feel.


 This is the Portinari Triptych, and to feel its impact you have to look at the things Florentine painters
 had not been doing.

 Start with the shepherds, just right of the centre. Their faces are weathered, individual, almost rough —
 real working men, open-mouthed, hurrying in from the fields to see the Child, painted with a plain,
 unidealised humanity that was startling in Italy. Then drop your eyes to the foreground, to the still-life
 that everyone remembers: a sheaf of wheat, and two vessels holding irises, a single red lily and pale
 columbine, every petal and leaf rendered in oil with a clarity that seems to hold its own light. Each
 flower carries a meaning — the lily for purity, the columbine, whose name evokes the dove, for the
 sorrows of the Virgin — but it's the sheer optical truth of them that stopped Florentine painters cold.
 They stood where you're standing and studied that single patch of flowers.

 Look, too, at the scale, which is deliberately strange: the kneeling Portinari family on the wings are
 painted small, dwarfed by their giant guardian saints looming behind them, a northern convention that
 measures importance by size rather than distance. And feel the cold in it — the wintry grey light, the
 bare earth, the hush over the whole scene, so different from the warm gold of the Italian rooms. The
 painting arrived in Florence in fourteen eighty-three, in oil, exact and patient and northern, and it was
 hung among the warm Italian tempera on purpose, so the collision was unmissable. Painters like
 Ghirlandaio took the lesson directly, copying its shepherds almost outright — and through Ghirlandaio's
 workshop it reached the young Michelangelo. This is the moment Italy began to take oil painting
 seriously. The next room is just ahead — play the next track when you reach it.
