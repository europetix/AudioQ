 Caravaggio — Medusa
 WHERE YOU ARE
 In front of the Medusa — a round painting on a convex wooden shield, mounted so the surface bulges slightly
 toward you.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 On a circular ceremonial shield: the severed head of Medusa at the instant of decapitation, mouth open in a
 scream, blood jetting from the neck, a writhing crown of green snakes for hair, against a dark green ground.
 The convex surface gives the head a startling three-dimensional bulge. Oil on canvas over a wooden shield,
 around 1597.
 LISTEN FOR
 Why it's painted on a shield, the impossible moment it captures, and whose face it might be.


 This is Caravaggio's Medusa, and the first strange thing about it is what it's painted on: not a flat panel
 but a convex wooden parade shield, curving out toward you. Caravaggio used that curve as part of the
 illusion — the head seems to bulge and lunge into your space, leaping off the surface, so the object that
 should protect you instead attacks.

 In the myth, anyone who looked directly at the Gorgon Medusa was turned to stone, until the hero
 Perseus beheaded her by using his polished shield as a mirror, so he never met her gaze head-on.
 Caravaggio paints the most impossible instant of all: the very moment of decapitation. Look at the face
 — the mouth wrenched open in a full scream, the eyes flung wide with shock, the brow knotted in
 disbelief. Blood jets in thick, dark ropes from the severed neck, painted with a butcher's accuracy. The
 hair is a writhing nest of green snakes, each one alive, twisting, hissing in different directions. And here
 is the unbearable cleverness of it: Caravaggio catches her in the split second when she is already
 beheaded but not yet dead — still conscious, still horrified, watching her own death arrive. It's a scream
 frozen at the very edge of extinction.

 There's a long tradition that the agonised face is Caravaggio's own, that he studied himself in a mirror
 and painted himself as the monster — a private, dark joke entirely in keeping with a man who lived
 violently and died on the run. The shield was a diplomatic gift, sent to the Medici in Florence, a
 dazzling show-off piece designed to astonish a court that loved both art and weapons. It works on you
 exactly as intended: revulsion and wonder in the same instant. Stand close and let the convex surface do
 its trick, watch the head seem to swell toward you, then turn to Artemisia's Judith and play the next
 track.
