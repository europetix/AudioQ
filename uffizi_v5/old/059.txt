 Entering Venice
 WHERE YOU ARE
 A room opening the gallery's great sequence of Venetian sixteenth-century painting, its walls a deep Venetian
 green.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 The threshold to the Venetian rooms — their walls a deep green chosen to set off the school's glowing colour
 — beginning the Uffizi's superb run of Venetian masters: Titian, Tintoretto, Veronese and their circle.
 LISTEN FOR
 Why Venice is almost a different country in paint, the great divide it embodies, and what's coming.


 Notice the walls change colour here, to a deep, saturated green — because you are crossing into Venice,
 and Venetian painting is almost a different art from the Florentine work you've been walking through all
 day.

 Here's the great divide that ran through the whole Italian Renaissance. Florence built its art on disegno
 — on drawing, on line and contour and design, the intellectual skeleton beneath a picture; for
 Florentines, drawing was the very soul of art, the proof of a thinking mind. Venice built its art on colore
 — on colour and light. Venetian painters worked in oil on canvas, layering up glowing, atmospheric
 colour, softening their edges, modelling form out of warm light and shadow rather than firm outline, and
 chasing a sensuous richness of surface that you feel in the body before you ever analyse it in the head. It
 is warmer, more worldly, more openly luxurious — the art of a rich merchant republic that loved
 pleasure and display.

 The rooms ahead hold one of the finest collections of sixteenth-century Venetian painting anywhere on
 earth: Titian above all, then the rushing drama of Tintoretto and the silvery splendour of Veronese. So
 let your eyes relax now, out of line and into colour and light, and prepare to be seduced rather than
 instructed. The next room is just ahead — play the next track when you reach it.
