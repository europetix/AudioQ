 Simone Martini & Lippo Memmi — The
 Annunciation
 WHERE YOU ARE
 In front of the great gold Annunciation — a tall triptych with a pointed Gothic frame: the angel kneeling at
 the left, the Virgin at the right, a vase of lilies between them.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 On a blazing, deeply tooled gold ground: the angel Gabriel just landed, his plaid cloak still settling, an olive
 branch in his hand; the Virgin recoiling on her throne, twisting away, a thumb marking her place in a book; a
 gold vase of white lilies between them; the angel's spoken greeting lettered in raised gold across the air.
 Painted 1333 for Siena Cathedral.
 LISTEN FOR
 The detail no one forgets, the human moment underneath the gold, and the small symbols worth hunting for.


 This is the Annunciation, painted in thirteen thirty-three by Simone Martini, with the two saints in the
 side panels usually given to his brother-in-law and partner Lippo Memmi. It was made for an altar in
 Siena Cathedral, and it is the summit of Sienese Gothic painting.

 Start with the thing nobody ever forgets: the words. The angel's greeting runs from Gabriel's mouth
 straight toward the Virgin, lettered in raised, gilded capitals across the empty air between them — 'Ave
 gratia plena,' hail, full of grace. It's the painting making sound visible, one of the boldest ideas in all of
 medieval art, and it forces your eye to travel the exact path of the angel's voice.

 Now look at the Virgin, because she's the human heart of it. This is not serene acceptance. She pulls
 back into her throne, her body twisting away, drawing her deep-blue cloak across herself almost
 defensively, her face wary and unsettled — one thumb still caught in the book she was reading, as if the
 angel has interrupted her mid-sentence. Simone has caught the instant of alarm, the human flinch before
 the 'yes,' in the most ceremonial subject in Christian art. It is astonishingly real feeling inside a frame of
 pure gold.

 Then hunt for the small things, because Simone has loaded the panel with them. The vase of white lilies
 between the two figures, for the Virgin's purity. The olive branch in Gabriel's hand and the olive crown
 on his head — olive being Siena's own emblem, and a sign of the peace the message brings. His
 patterned cloak, still ruffled and flying from the speed of his arrival, as if he has only this second
 touched down. And the gold itself: every inch of the background is tooled and punched into pattern, so
 the whole surface shifts and glints as you move past it. It's worldly and otherworldly at once,
 fashionable and eternal. When you've taken it in, walk on into the next room and play the next track.
