 Leonardo da Vinci
 WHERE YOU ARE
 The Leonardo room — holding two early works by Leonardo himself and a collaboration with his master
 Verrocchio.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 Early Leonardo: the delicate Annunciation, painted in his twenties; the great unfinished Adoration of the
 Magi; and Verrocchio's Baptism of Christ, in which the young Leonardo painted at least one angel — the
 work where, the story goes, the pupil surpassed the master.
 LISTEN FOR
 What to look at, in what order, in one of the most important rooms in the building.


 This room stops people, and rightly. It holds the work of Leonardo da Vinci as a young man — before
 Milan, before the Last Supper, before the Mona Lisa — right at the start of one of the most
 extraordinary careers in human history. To stand here is to catch a genius in the act of becoming
 himself.

 There are three things to look at, and an order that makes sense of them. Start with Verrocchio's Baptism
 of Christ: this was the workshop where the teenage Leonardo trained, and he painted at least one of the
 kneeling angels in it. Vasari tells the famous story that when Verrocchio saw the angel his young pupil
 had painted — softer, more alive, more subtly lit than anything around it — he put down his brush in
 despair and never painted again, knowing he had been surpassed. Find the angel on the left, in profile,
 and judge for yourself.

 Then turn to Leonardo's own two works, each with its own track: the serene, jewel-precise
 Annunciation, painted when he was barely twenty; and the huge, ghostly, unfinished Adoration of the
 Magi, abandoned when he left Florence for Milan. Together they show you both sides of him — the
 finisher of exquisite detail and the restless searcher who could never stop rethinking. Take them in that
 spirit: you're watching a mind teach itself to do things no one had done before. Start with the
 Annunciation. Play the next track in front of it.
