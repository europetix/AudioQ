TRACK 024 · A25 W-FOCUS 326 WORDS · ~3 MIN



 Piero della Francesca — The Duke and Duchess of
 Urbino
 WHERE YOU ARE
 In front of the two small facing panels — the Duke and Duchess in profile, turned to face each other across
 the gap between the frames.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 A diptych: on the right the Duke, Federico da Montefeltro, in a red robe and cap, seen from his left; on the left
 his wife, Battista Sforza, her hair elaborately dressed and pearled, her skin pale. Behind each, a vast
 continuous landscape of the Urbino countryside seen from above. The reverse sides show the couple on
 triumphal chariots. Oil and tempera, around 1465-72.
 LISTEN FOR
 Why both faces are in strict profile, the detail in the Duke's nose, the strange truth about the Duchess, and the
 world behind them.


 These two small panels are among the most famous portraits of the entire Renaissance, and almost
 everything odd about them turns out to have a reason.

 First, the strict profiles. The couple face each other in pure side view, like the emperors stamped on
 ancient Roman coins — a deliberate echo of antiquity meant to make a duke and duchess look timeless
 and authoritative. But there's a blunt practical reason too. Look at the Duke's face: that famous broken
 nose, with its sharp inward dent, and every wart and mole left honestly in place. Federico had lost his
 right eye and damaged the bridge of his nose in a tournament, so he was always painted from his good
 left side — and Piero records the rest of that rugged soldier's face without a flicker of flattery. Power
 here doesn't need to be pretty; it needs to be unmistakable.

 Now look at Battista, on the left, and notice how pale she is, almost chalk-white against the blue sky.
 She had died young, around twenty-six, probably about the time the portrait was made; that deathly
 pallor is the quiet signal that this is partly a memorial to a wife already gone. Behind both of them
 stretches a single continuous landscape, seen from a high, god-like vantage — the rolling hills, winding
 rivers and tiny hazy towns of the lands they ruled, dissolving into a pale blue distance. That softening of
 the far hills into blue haze is Piero studying exactly how the air itself thickens over distance, the same
 problem Leonardo will take further in the very next rooms.

 And if you can see the backs of the panels, they carry the rest of the message: the Duke and Duchess
 riding in classical triumphal chariots, attended by the Virtues, a complete little program of praise in
 paint. Two faces, two landscapes, one marriage held up forever. Through the doorway, the next track is
 waiting in the room beyond.
