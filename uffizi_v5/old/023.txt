 Domenico Veneziano & Piero della Francesca
 WHERE YOU ARE
 The room of mid-fifteenth-century masters of light and space, holding one of the most famous portrait pairs
 in the world.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 Painting that solved space and light: Domenico Veneziano's serene, daylight-filled Santa Lucia altarpiece,
 and Piero della Francesca's double portrait of the Duke and Duchess of Urbino — calm, geometric, luminous
 work from the generation that perfected perspective.
 LISTEN FOR
 What this room is really about, who these two cool intelligences were, and the pair of profiles to walk straight
 up to.


 This room is about clarity — about light, air and geometry brought under perfect, almost serene control
 by two painters obsessed with getting the visible world exactly right.

 Look first for Domenico Veneziano's altarpiece of Saint Lucy, the Santa Lucia de' Magnoli panel.
 Notice how the whole sacred scene stands in even, rosy daylight, the saints gathered under a loggia of
 pale pink and green marble, the architecture measured and calm, the colours clear as morning.
 Domenico — a Venetian working in Florence — was one of the first to flood a religious painting with
 real, natural light instead of gold, and to set his holy figures in a believable, sunlit space. There's a grim
 old legend, told by Vasari, that he was murdered by a jealous rival; it's certainly false, but it tells you
 how much these painters' rivalries fascinated later Florence.

 Domenico was, by tradition, a teacher of the man whose work dominates this room: Piero della
 Francesca, the great cool intelligence of the early Renaissance — a painter who was also a serious
 mathematician, and wrote treatises on perspective and geometry. His most famous painting hangs here:
 the double portrait of Federico da Montefeltro and his wife Battista Sforza, the Duke and Duchess of
 Urbino. There's a track for it. Go and stand in front of the two facing profiles, and play the next track.
