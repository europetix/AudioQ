 Parmigianino — Madonna of the Long Neck
 WHERE YOU ARE
 In front of the tall, unfinished Madonna of the Long Neck — a strikingly elongated Virgin with a large
 sleeping Child across her lap.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 An elongated Virgin in shimmering blue and gold, an impossibly long neck and tapering fingers; a very large
 Christ Child sprawled asleep across her knees; a crowd of beautiful youths pressing in at the left; and at the
 right, baffling: a single tall column and a tiny prophet far below. Oil on panel, around 1535, left unfinished.
 LISTEN FOR
 Why nothing here is 'wrong,' the unsettling Child, the column that makes no sense, and the strange man who
 painted it.


 This is Parmigianino's Madonna of the Long Neck, painted in the fifteen-thirties and left unfinished at
 his early death — and almost everything about it looks 'wrong' on purpose. That, it turns out, is the
 whole key to enjoying it.

 Start with the Madonna herself. Her neck really is swan-long; her fingers taper to impossible lengths;
 her whole body is stretched and refined far beyond anything natural, wrapped in cool, shimmering,
 pearl-and-blue drapery, her small head poised high above sloping shoulders. This is Mannerism's entire
 creed in a single figure: elegance and artifice prized as the highest values, beauty deliberately pushed
 past nature into something icy, refined and faintly unreal. Set her beside Raphael's calm, natural
 Madonnas upstairs and you feel exactly how far, and how knowingly, painting had travelled in a single
 generation.

 Now look at the Christ Child across her lap, and notice how he unsettles you. He is enormous, almost
 adolescent in size, and he sprawls in a heavy, boneless sleep that looks disturbingly like death — a
 quiet, deliberate foreshadowing of the dead Christ of the Passion, laid across his mother's knees like a
 premonition. To the left, a press of impossibly lovely youths crowds in, one cradling a tall urn or vase
 that catches the light. And then look to the right, where the picture turns genuinely baffling: a single,
 isolated marble column rises out of nowhere into the sky, and far beneath it stands a tiny figure of a
 prophet, wildly out of scale, unrolling a scroll. Some read the lone column as a symbol of the Virgin
 herself, praised in hymns as a column of the faith; but Parmigianino never resolved that strange space,
 and the mismatch only deepens the dreamlike unease.

 Don't try to make it add up the way a Raphael adds up; it isn't meant to. It's meant to be exquisite, cool
 and quietly uncanny. Parmigianino spent his last years increasingly absorbed in alchemy and left this
 painting and others unfinished — and that air of something arrested, not quite completed, suits its
 haunted beauty perfectly. Whenever you're ready, the next track picks up in the room that follows.
