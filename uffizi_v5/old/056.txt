 Bronzino — Eleonora di Toledo with Her Son
 Giovanni
 WHERE YOU ARE
 In front of the portrait of Eleonora di Toledo — the duchess seated, a small boy at her side, against a cool
 blue ground.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 The Duchess Eleonora, wife of Cosimo I, seated and facing us, one hand resting on the shoulder of her young
 son Giovanni; an extraordinary brocaded gown of black, white and gold; pearls at her throat and in a net over
 her hair; a flat, cool blue background. Oil on panel, around 1545.
 LISTEN FOR
 Why the dress almost upstages the face, the meaning of the pearls and the gesture, and the sad coda to the
 story.


 This is Bronzino's portrait of Eleonora di Toledo, the Spanish wife of Duke Cosimo the First, with one
 of her sons, Giovanni, beside her — painted around fifteen forty-five, and one of the great court
 portraits of the Renaissance.

 Look at the dress first, because Bronzino almost wills you to. That gown of black, white and gold
 brocade, patterned with bold pomegranates and scrolling vines, is rendered with such hypnotic,
 thread-by-thread precision that it very nearly becomes the true subject of the painting — a showcase,
 too, for Florence's famous luxury textile industry, the source of much Medici wealth. There's a long-told
 story that Eleonora was buried in this very gown; it is now doubted, but the legend tells you how
 completely the dress and the woman fused in memory. Note the pearls as well — at her throat, looped
 across her bodice, netted over her hair. Pearls signalled purity and immense wealth at once, and
 Bronzino lavishes a jeweller's patience on every single one.

 Now look at her face and her hand. Her expression is cool, composed, almost mask-like — that
 signature Bronzino distance — the very picture of aristocratic self-control, a ruler who never lets the
 surface crack. And one hand rests quietly on her little son's shoulder. That gesture is the real message of
 the painting. This is not a private, tender mother-and-child; it is a dynastic statement. Eleonora had
 given Cosimo many children and so secured the Medici line, and the portrait announces exactly that:
 continuity, wealth, the guaranteed future of a ruling house, fixed forever in enamel-smooth paint. The
 flat blue ground lifts the two of them out of ordinary time, like figures struck on a coin.

 There is a quiet sadness behind the splendour, though. Both Eleonora and this young son Giovanni
 would die within days of each other some years later, struck down by malaria — so this serene image of
 a secure future became, in time, a memorial to a mother and child the dynasty lost too soon. Carry on
 through; the next track will meet you in the next room.
