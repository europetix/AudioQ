 Correggio and Parmigianino
 WHERE YOU ARE
 The room of two great painters from Parma — Correggio and Parmigianino — holding one of the strangest

 WHAT YOU SEE
 Painting from Parma: Correggio's tender, softly lit devotional works, and Parmigianino's elegant, elongated,

 LISTEN FOR
 How these two painters from Parma point the century in two directions, and the painting to walk up to.


 Two painters from the small city of Parma share this room, and between them they point the sixteenth
 century in two very different directions.

 Correggio is all tenderness and soft, caressing light — sweet, intimate Madonnas and holy scenes, with
 a melting delicacy and a gentle illusionism that look ahead, remarkably, to the Baroque and even to the
 dreamy charm of the eighteenth century. In his great dome frescoes back in Parma he made painted
 ceilings seem to dissolve into open sky, and you can feel that same softness in the smaller works here.
 He's a painter of warmth and grace.

 But the one to stop for is Parmigianino, and his famous Madonna of the Long Neck. He is one of the
 supreme stylists of Mannerism — the movement that followed the High Renaissance and, unable to be
 more perfect than Raphael, chose instead to be more elegant, more artificial, more strange. Where the
 High Renaissance prized natural balance, Parmigianino prizes grace pushed past nature into something
 cool and otherworldly: impossibly long limbs, swan necks, tapering fingers, a refinement so extreme it
 tips into the uncanny. His Madonna of the Long Neck is the great example, and there's a track for it. Go
 and stand in front of it, and play the next track.
