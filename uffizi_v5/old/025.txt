 Pietro Perugino
 WHERE YOU ARE
 The room of Pietro Perugino, the great Umbrian master of sweetness and calm.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 Serene, softly modelled painting by Perugino — gentle Madonnas, a Pietà, calm landscapes bathed in clear
 light, and portraits of remarkable directness, including the sharp-eyed Francesco delle Opere. The painter
 who, above all, taught the young Raphael.
 LISTEN FOR
 Why Perugino matters far more than his fame today suggests, and what to look for.


 Perugino is not a household name now, but stand here a moment, because few painters shaped what
 came next as much as he did.

 He was the master of sweetness and serene order: soft, tender-faced Madonnas and saints, set in calm,
 spacious landscapes under clear Umbrian skies, everything balanced, gentle and unhurried. Look at the
 melting modelling of the faces, the slightly tilted heads, the deep airy distances behind. By around
 fifteen hundred Pietro Vannucci, called Perugino after his home region of Perugia, was one of the most
 admired and best-paid painters in all of Italy, his serene formula imitated everywhere.

 But look, too, for his portraits — the keen, watchful Francesco delle Opere is here, proof that the
 sweetness was a choice, not a limit; he could be sharp and exact when he wanted. And hold onto one
 fact above all: Perugino was the teacher of Raphael. Almost everything that makes the young Raphael's
 Madonnas so effortlessly harmonious — the soft faces, the calm pyramids of figures, the gentle
 landscapes — begins right here, in Perugino's workshop. The pupil would soon eclipse the master so
 completely that Perugino fell out of fashion and was mocked, late in life, for repeating himself; but
 without this room there is no Raphael. Look for the family resemblance, then walk on. There's more
 next door; play the next track once you're inside.
