 The Botticelli Room
 WHERE YOU ARE
 The large, luminous room currently holding Botticelli's two great mythologies, with several of his Madonnas
 and tondi around the walls.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 Sandro Botticelli's poetry in paint: the Birth of Venus and the Primavera, hung with the Madonna of the
 Magnificat, the Adoration of the Magi, Pallas and the Centaur, and more — one of the most famous rooms in
 any museum on earth.
 LISTEN FOR
 How to take a room this crowded and this beloved, and which two paintings to give your real time to.


 This is the room most people came to see, and you can feel it the moment you walk in. After all that
 tempera glowing on dark wood, Botticelli's two huge mythologies seem to breathe — pale, graceful,
 painted on canvas, lit from within, floating on the wall like daydreams.

 A word on where things are. The museum has lately been showing the great Botticellis here in room
 A-nine, gathered near their usual home while the surrounding rooms are reworked. Whatever the sign
 over the door says, you'll know them on sight. Around the two famous canvases hang others worth a
 slow look: the circular Madonna of the Magnificat; the Adoration of the Magi, in which Botticelli
 painted portraits of the Medici and, on the far right, very probably himself, looking out at you; and the
 strange, tender Pallas and the Centaur.

 But the two to stand in front of are the Birth of Venus and the Primavera, and there's a track for each.
 They reward patience — this is the densest square of beauty in the building, so don't rush it. Start with
 the Birth of Venus. Play the next track in front of it.
