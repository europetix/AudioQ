 Sassetta and Andrea del Castagno
 WHERE YOU ARE
 The room of early Tuscan painting in the collection, pairing Sienese gold with muscular Florentine form.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 Two poles of early-fifteenth-century Tuscan painting: Sassetta's tender, gold-ground Sienese work, and
 Andrea del Castagno's powerful, sculptural Florentine Virgin and Child with Saints — among the most
 important works in the collection.
 LISTEN FOR
 How this room replays, in miniature, the great Siena-versus-Florence story you saw upstairs.


 This room quietly restages the great argument you walked through on the floor above — Siena's grace
 against Florence's weight — in two outstanding panels, and it shows why curators rate this collection so
 highly.

 On the Sienese side stands Sassetta, one of the loveliest painters of the fifteenth-century Sienese school,
 a direct heir to Simone Martini's world of gold and flowing, lyrical line. His Madonna of the Snow tells
 a charming old legend — the founding of a great Roman church on the very spot where snow
 miraculously fell one August morning — in delicate colour and burnished gold, utterly faithful to
 Siena's centuries-old enchantment with beauty and storytelling.

 Then turn to Andrea del Castagno, and feel the temperature drop and the gravity increase. Castagno was
 one of the toughest, most muscular Florentine painters of the early Renaissance — a follower of
 Masaccio who built his figures with the hard, weighty solidity of carved stone. His Virgin and Child
 with Saints, which the museum's own director has called among the most Florentine things in the entire
 collection, gives you weight, anatomy and gravity exactly where Sassetta gives you grace and gold leaf.
 Stand for a moment between the two and you've got the whole early Renaissance — its two rival souls
 — in a single small room. When you're ready, move on. Head through whenever you like, and the next
 track will be ready next door.
