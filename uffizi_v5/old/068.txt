 Artemisia Gentileschi — Judith Slaying Holofernes
 WHERE YOU ARE
 In front of Artemisia's Judith — a large, dark canvas of two women pinning down and beheading a man,
 blood spilling across white sheets.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 In deep shadow lit by a single hard light: the widow Judith and her maidservant hold down the enemy general
 Holofernes on a bed and saw through his neck with a sword; blood sprays and runs across the white sheets;
 the women's faces are set with grim, physical effort. Oil on canvas, around 1620.
 LISTEN FOR
 Who Artemisia was, why her version is unlike any man's, and what to look at.


 This is Artemisia Gentileschi's Judith Slaying Holofernes — and it is one of the most powerful paintings
 in the entire gallery, by one of the very few women able to build a major artistic career in the
 seventeenth century.

 The biblical story is a heroic one: the widow Judith saves her besieged people by going to the tent of the
 enemy general Holofernes, letting him drink himself into a stupor, and beheading him in his sleep.
 Many male painters had treated it almost decorously — a serene, beautiful Judith holding a tidy sword,
 the deed already politely done. Artemisia paints the act itself, in progress, as brutal physical labour.
 Look at how the two women work together: Judith leans in with her whole body weight, sleeves pushed
 up her strong forearms, both hands driving the blade as she saws through the neck, her face set with
 concentration rather than triumph. The maidservant — young and powerful, not the usual old crone —
 pins the thrashing Holofernes down with both arms across his chest. Blood jets upward in arcs and soaks
 into the white sheets in dark rivers. Nothing is prettified; everything is weight, force and effort.

 Artemisia learned her dramatic light and shadow — the tenebrism — from Caravaggio's circle and from
 her own father, the painter Orazio. But the raw conviction here is entirely her own. Her early life was
 hard: as a young woman she was raped by a colleague of her father's, and endured a public trial —
 recorded in surviving court documents — during which she herself was tortured with thumbscrews to
 test whether her testimony was true. Viewers have long read that ordeal into the unflinching violence of
 her Judiths, two women overpowering a powerful man with their bare hands. Whether or not you read it
 that way, look at the sheer physical conviction of it, the refusal to look away or to make the horror
 beautiful. It is the work of a painter who knew exactly what she was depicting. When you're ready,
