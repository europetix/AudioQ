 Pontormo and Rosso Fiorentino
 WHERE YOU ARE
 The room of the two founders of Florentine Mannerism — Pontormo and Rosso Fiorentino — with Rosso's
 startling, violent masterpiece.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 Work by the two great young radicals of 1520s Florence: Pontormo, with his charged religious scenes, and
 Rosso Fiorentino, whose paintings push the human body and acid colour to shocking extremes. The room's
 showpiece is Rosso's Moses Defending the Daughters of Jethro.
 LISTEN FOR
 Who broke Florentine painting wide open, and the picture to walk straight up to.


 These two painters, both born around fifteen-oh-three, are the founders of Florentine Mannerism — the
 young radicals who, in the decade after Raphael's death, deliberately blew apart the calm perfection
 their elders had spent a lifetime achieving.

 Pontormo you've just met next door: nervous, original, emotionally strange, a painter of charged and
 tender religious scenes. His friend and rival Rosso Fiorentino — the name means 'red-haired Florentine'
 — pushed even further into the extreme. Rosso's art is jagged, theatrical and sometimes genuinely
 shocking; later in life he would carry this restless Italian manner all the way to the French court at
 Fontainebleau, helping to seed the Renaissance in France.

 The painting to stop for here is his Moses Defending the Daughters of Jethro, and there's a track for it. It
 is unlike anything upstairs: a violent tangle of muscular nude bodies, harsh raking light, and sharp,
 almost electric colour, a deliberate assault on the eye and on every rule of decorum the High
 Renaissance held dear. Where Raphael offered serenity, Rosso offers shock. Go and find Rosso's
 Moses, and play the next track in front of it.
