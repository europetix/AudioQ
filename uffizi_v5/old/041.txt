 Bramantino, the Spanish School & European
 Masters
 WHERE YOU ARE
 The rooms holding the collection's Lombard and European strengths — Bramantino, and the Spanish masters
 who are rare elsewhere in the Uffizi.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 The parts of the collection that fill the Uffizi's gaps: Bramantino's grave Lombard altarpiece, a fine Veronese
 portrait, and above all the Spanish school — works by or attributed to Goya, El Greco and Velazquez,
 painters almost absent from the main galleries.
 LISTEN FOR
 Why these rooms matter out of all proportion to their size, and what to seek out.


 These rooms are the real reason curators prize this collection so highly, because they hold precisely
 what the great Uffizi itself lacks — and it's worth slowing down to appreciate that.

 The main galleries you've walked all day are overwhelmingly Italian; here, quietly, the gaps get filled.
 From Lombardy in the north there's Bramantino, with his strange, grave, geometric altarpiece, all cool
 stillness and architectural order. There's a superb Venetian portrait by Veronese of a father with his
 small son, tender and direct. And, rarest of all for Florence, there is the Spanish school: works by, or
 close to, Goya, El Greco and Velazquez — three of the supreme painters of all Europe, of whom the
 Uffizi otherwise owns almost nothing at all. To stand in front of a dark, searching Spanish portrait here
 is to glimpse a whole towering tradition that the rest of this building, for all its Renaissance riches,
 simply cannot show you.

 That's why one private collector's eye, gathered over a lifetime in the twentieth century, was such a gift:
 it didn't just add more masterpieces, it added whole missing chapters of European art. Seek out the
 Spanish faces in particular — they're the collection's quiet triumph. Then move toward the last and most
 dramatic room. Play the next track in front of the Bernini sculpture.
