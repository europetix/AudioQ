 Michelangelo — The Doni Tondo (Holy Family)
 WHERE YOU ARE
 In front of the round panel in its richly carved gilded frame — the Holy Family in the foreground, a low wall
 and nude youths behind.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 A circular painting: Mary kneels in the foreground and twists powerfully to take the Christ Child over her
 shoulder from Joseph behind her; the figures are muscular, sculptural, brilliantly coloured in shot pinks,
 oranges and greens; behind a low wall sits a row of mysterious nude youths, with the young John the Baptist
 between. Tempera on panel, around 1505-07, in its original carved frame.
 LISTEN FOR
 Why this looks like a painted sculpture, the strange nudes behind, the colours that shocked, and the wedding
 it marked.


 This is the Doni Tondo, the only finished panel painting by Michelangelo that you can see in Florence
 — he thought of himself as a sculptor, not a painter, and that conviction is carved into every inch of it. It
 was made around fifteen-oh-six for the wealthy Florentine Agnolo Doni, probably to mark his marriage
 to Maddalena Strozzi, whose portraits by Raphael hang in this same room.

 Look first at the central group and how it moves. Mary kneels in the foreground, but she's twisting —
 turning back and up over her own shoulder to take the Child from Joseph behind her. It's a complicated,
 spiralling pose, the whole family knotted into one continuous twist, and the bodies are heavy, muscular,
 sculptural; Mary's bare arm could belong to an athlete. Michelangelo is painting figures exactly as if he
 were carving them from marble, wringing the maximum of energy out of a compact group — and,
 remember, you saw the seed of this in Signorelli's twisting tondo a few rooms back.

 Then notice two stranger things. The colours: acid pinks, shot orange-and-green drapery, cold blue —
 brilliant, almost harsh hues that startled people, and that the next generation of Mannerist painters would
 seize on; when you reach Pontormo and Rosso downstairs, remember these colours began here. And the
 background: behind a low stone wall sits a row of nude young men, lounging and leaning together, with
 the boy John the Baptist between them and the Holy Family. Nobody is certain what they mean — the
 pagan world before grace, perhaps, with the Holy Family marking the new age in front of them. They
 are beautiful and unexplained, and utterly Michelangelo: he could never resist the naked human body,
 even here. Step back to feel the whole spiralling design inside its golden frame, then turn to the Raphael
 and play the next track.
