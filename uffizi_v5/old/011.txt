 Gentile da Fabriano — Adoration of the Magi
 WHERE YOU ARE
 In front of the great gold Adoration — a huge panel in an elaborate gilded Gothic frame of three pointed
 arches, with a row of small scenes (the predella) along its base at eye level.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 A glittering procession of the three kings, courtiers, horses, hounds, leopards and a falcon, winding from
 distant hills at the top down to the Holy Family at the left, where the eldest king kneels to kiss the Child's feet
 and the youngest waits to have his golden spurs removed. Real gold leaf throughout; signed and dated 1423.
 Below, three small predella scenes including a night Nativity.
 LISTEN FOR
 How to read it like a story, the patron's fingerprints all over it, and the small detail that is really the future
 arriving.


 This is Gentile da Fabriano's Adoration of the Magi, signed and dated fourteen twenty-three. It was
 commissioned by Palla Strozzi, the richest man in Florence at the time, for his family's chapel — and it
 is the most lavish thing the International Gothic ever made.

 Read it like a story, from the top down. Start in the distance, in the three little arched scenes along the
 very top, where the kings first see the star, then ride along the coast, then approach the city. Follow the
 procession as it winds down through the hills, growing larger, until it spills into the foreground on the
 left, at the stable. There the eldest king has already knelt and taken off his crown to kiss the Child's feet;
 the second kneels behind him; and the youngest, in dazzling clothes, stands waiting while a servant
 unbuckles his golden spurs — because you take your spurs off in the presence of a king.

 Everything here is what a great patron paid to be seen spending money on: cloth of gold raised and
 tooled so it catches the light, a menagerie of leopards, monkeys, hounds and a falcon, the very latest
 courtly fashion, and real gold leaf laid on thick — in candlelight the whole wall would have flickered
 and shifted. Look closely and you may even find the patron's own family worked into the crowd.

 But now drop your eyes to the small scenes along the base, the predella, and look at the Nativity on the
 left. It's a night scene, and the light comes from the Christ Child himself, glowing out onto the faces
 around him in the dark. That — painting real light, falling from a real source, modelling real forms — is
 the new thing, the crack through which the whole Renaissance is about to pour. This painting is the most
 beautiful goodbye in the building. From here, the gold starts to fall away and the real world rushes in.
