 Bernini — The Martyrdom of Saint Lawrence
 WHERE YOU ARE
 In front of the marble — a single life-scale figure twisting on a metal gridiron over carved flames.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 The young deacon Lawrence stretched on a gridiron above flames, his body arching and twisting, one arm
 flung up, his face turned upward in a complex expression of pain and rapture; the flames, the metal bars and
 the flesh all rendered in white marble. Carved by Bernini around 1617, in his late teens.
 LISTEN FOR
 What to look for in the marble, the legend of how he caught the expression, and why it announces the
 Baroque.


 This is Bernini's Martyrdom of Saint Lawrence, and the first astonishing fact about it is the sculptor's
 age: he was still in his teens, not yet twenty, when he carved this from a single block of marble. It's a
 young man's audacious announcement that a new genius had arrived in Rome.

 Lawrence, a deacon of the early Church, was martyred by being roasted alive on a gridiron — and
 Bernini gives you the moment in full. Look at how the body twists and arches up off the metal bars, one
 arm thrown back over the head, the muscles of the torso taut and straining, the whole figure caught in
 restless movement rather than calm repose. Renaissance sculptors gave you bodies at rest, balanced and
 serene; Bernini gives you a body in the grip of an instant. Then look at how he has carved the
 impossible out of one cold material: the licking tongues of flame beneath, the hard straight edges of the
 gridiron, and the soft yield of living flesh pressing against the bars — all of it the same white stone, yet
 each reading as a completely different substance.

 And look hardest at the face, turned upward and slightly open. It holds two things at the same instant:
 physical agony, and religious ecstasy — pain transfigured into vision, the saint already half in heaven.
 There's a famous story that to get that expression exactly right, the young Bernini studied his own face
 in a mirror while holding his bare leg against a flame, drawing his own grimace as he burned. True or
 not, it tells you everything about his ambition: to make cold marble feel, to freeze a human being at the
 absolute peak of emotion and hold it there forever. That restless movement and raw, theatrical feeling,
 breaking completely with Renaissance calm, is the Baroque being born — the same new energy you'll
 meet in Caravaggio's rooms shortly. When you've taken it in, you've finished this collection. Make your
 way back toward the main galleries for the self-portraits, and play the next track.
