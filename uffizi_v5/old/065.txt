 The Verone — the Loggia
 WHERE YOU ARE
 The Verone, an open loggia or terrace at the end of the wing, with views out over the city and the river.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 An open loggia — the 'Verone' — at the end of the first-floor wing: a light-filled architectural pause with
 views over Florence, marking the close of the sixteenth-century rooms before the seventeenth century begins.
 LISTEN FOR
 A breath and a view, what you've just completed, and what comes next.


 Step out into the Verone — the open loggia at the end of the wing — and take a real breath. After room
 upon room of painting, this light-filled architectural pause, with its view out over the rooftops and the
 river, is a welcome one. Let your eyes rest on something far away for a moment; museum fatigue is real,
 and the gallery built this opening as a deliberate place to recover.

 It also marks a genuine boundary in your visit. Behind you now lies the entire sixteenth century,
 complete: the soft Lombard wake of Leonardo, the shock of Florentine Mannerism with Pontormo and
 Rosso, the cool dynastic magnificence of Bronzino and the Medici court, and the glowing green rooms
 of Venice — Titian, Tintoretto, Veronese. That is the cinquecento, the century in which Italian painting
 conquered Europe and set the standard for everything that followed.

 Ahead lies the final stretch: the seventeenth century, the Baroque, and a painter who will change
 everything once more, more violently than anyone since Giotto — Caravaggio. The whole gallery has
 been quietly building, century by century, toward this turn from idealised beauty to raw, theatrical,
 shadow-struck reality. Take the view, gather yourself, and walk on into the seventeenth-century rooms.
 Play the next track when you reach the Caravaggio room.
