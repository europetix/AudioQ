 Albrecht Dürer — Adoration of the Magi

 Italian works nearby.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 The Virgin and Child at the left before a crumbling classical ruin; the three kings approaching with their gifts,

 LISTEN FOR
 What Dürer carried home from Italy, the face hidden in the crowd, and how to read his obsessive detail.


 This is Albrecht Dürer's Adoration of the Magi, painted in fifteen-oh-four, just after his second journey
 to Italy — and it's a painting standing on a border, half German, half Italian.

 From Italy, Dürer took the calm, balanced composition and the confident, deep landscape that opens
 behind the figures, with its blue distance and careful recession. But everything else is unmistakably
 northern. Look at the detail: the goldsmith's precision of the kings' goblets and gifts, every gleam and
 engraved pattern; the individual stones of the ruined wall, each weathered differently; the insects, the
 plants sprouting from the cracks, the texture of fur and brocade and hair. Dürer renders all of it with the
 patience of an engraver — which is exactly what he was, the supreme printmaker of his age, trained to
 make every line count. Nothing here is generalised; everything is observed.

 Now find the kneeling king at the centre, the one with long fair hair holding an open gold casket. That's
 Dürer himself — he often slipped his own face into his pictures, and his is the self-confident gaze of a
 man who signed his works with a famous monogram and insisted, against the custom of his day, that the
 painter was no mere craftsman but something close to a creator. Step back and you'll see the harmony
 and balance he learned in Italy; step close and you'll see the German love of the particular, the refusal to
 leave any surface unexamined. Hold both at once and you've understood the northern Renaissance in a
 single frame — a meeting of southern order and northern scrutiny in one ambitious mind. When you're
 ready, walk on through these rooms and play the next track.
