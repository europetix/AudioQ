 The Uffizi — Before You Go In
 WHERE YOU ARE
 In the Piazzale degli Uffizi, the long narrow courtyard between the two wings of the building, before you join
 the entrance queue at Door 1.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 A long, narrow stone courtyard running down toward the Arno, framed by two matching wings of arches and
 columns, with statues of famous Tuscans set in the niches along the piers. The river closes off the far end.
 LISTEN FOR
 What this building actually was, how the gallery came to be, how this guide works, and the shape of the
 Classic route you're about to walk.


 Welcome to Florence, and welcome to the Uffizi. Before you go in, stand here in the courtyard for a
 moment, because the building itself is the first thing worth understanding.

 Look at the name. Uffizi means offices. Cosimo the First de' Medici had this built in the fifteen-sixties,
 to a design by Giorgio Vasari, to house the administrative offices and magistrates of the Tuscan state —
 all under one roof, the two long wings you see on either side of you, joined at the far end by the river. It
 was a government building. The art came later. Cosimo's son, Francesco the First, turned the top floor
 into a private gallery for the Medici treasures, and around fifteen eighty-one he had Buontalenti build a
 small octagonal jewel-box of a room, the Tribune, to show off the very best of them. For two centuries
 this was a private collection you needed permission to see. Then, in seventeen sixty-nine, it opened to
 the public — one of the first museums in the modern sense anywhere in the world. And when the last of
 the Medici died, the family pact signed by Anna Maria Luisa left the entire collection to the city of
 Florence on one condition: that it could never be sold, never broken up, never leave the city. Which is
 why it's all still here.

 How this guide works. It's built from two kinds of tracks. Room tracks orient you the moment you walk
 into a new room — what's there, what to look at first. Work tracks are for standing in front of a single
 painting. Each track tells you what to play next, so you can just walk and listen. Every track is labelled
 with the museum's own room code, so you can match the audio to the signs on the walls.

 The shape of the day. You'll climb to the second floor and walk the Renaissance more or less in the
 order it happened — the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries first, then the early Renaissance and the
 Tribune, then the High Renaissance of Leonardo, Michelangelo and Raphael. Then you come down to
 the first floor for the sixteenth century, and finish with the seventeenth — Caravaggio, Rembrandt. It
 runs a little over two hours if you don't rush.

 One practical thing: book a timed entry slot before you come, or the queue can swallow an hour. When
 you're through the ticket check and you've reached the top of the staircase on the second floor, play the
 next track.
