 Paolo Uccello — The Battle of San Romano
 WHERE YOU ARE
 In front of Uccello's large battle panel — step back far enough to take in the whole shallow stage of horses
 and lances at once.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 A clash of armoured horsemen on a narrow foreground shelf: a thicket of raised and broken lances, a fallen
 soldier laid flat into the ground, rearing horses in pink, white and black, and a high background of tiny hedged
 fields and hunting figures. One of three panels, 1430s-40s; tempera on poplar with gold and silver leaf.
 LISTEN FOR
 Why this 'battle' is really a painting about geometry, the joke in the broken lances, and the detail that breaks
 the rules.


 This is one of three panels Paolo Uccello painted in the fourteen-thirties and forties of the Battle of San
 Romano, a Florentine victory over Siena. The other two hang in London and Paris; this is Florence's,
 and the Medici prized the set enough to take it for themselves and hang it in their palace.

 Don't read it as a war picture — read it as a man falling in love with perspective and showing off. Look
 down at the broken lances scattered on the ground: Uccello has arranged them not where they'd really
 fall, but like the ruled lines of a geometry diagram, all converging toward the same vanishing point,
 turning a battlefield into a demonstration of the new science of depth. Then find the fallen soldier at the
 lower left, lying flat — he's been carefully foreshortened, drawn shrinking away into the picture, one of
 the first real attempts in European painting to do that with a whole human body.

 Now let your eye enjoy how strange and beautiful the result is. The horses look like carved
 rocking-horses; one is bubble-gum pink. The armour, the harnesses and the bridles glint with real gold
 and silver leaf, which would have flashed in candlelight. And look up at the high background, with its
 little hedged fields and tiny figures chasing rabbits over the hills — it ignores the foreground's careful
 perspective completely, as if it belonged to another, older painting. That clash between the rigorous
 geometry below and the fairy-tale landscape above is the whole charm: Uccello cared less about the
 blood than about the grid underneath it. Vasari says he stayed up all night over his vanishing points
 while his wife called him to bed. You're looking at the early Renaissance teaching itself to see in three
 dimensions, out loud. When you're ready, walk on toward the Botticelli room and play the next track.
