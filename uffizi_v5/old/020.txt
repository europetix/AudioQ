 Renaissance Beyond the Alps — the German
 Masters
 WHERE YOU ARE
 The suite of rooms given to northern European painting — German and Flemish work hung together after the
 Italian early Renaissance.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 Painting from north of the Alps around 1500: Albrecht Dürer above all, with Lucas Cranach the Elder, and
 works in the orbit of Holbein and the German and Flemish schools — sharper, stranger, more minutely
 detailed than their Italian neighbours.
 LISTEN FOR
 Why the Uffizi sends you out of Italy here, and the painter to look for.


 For a few rooms the gallery leaves Italy, and it's worth knowing why. The Medici collected across
 borders, and Florentine artists were hungry for what the north could do — so the museum deliberately
 sets the German and Flemish masters here, in dialogue with the Italians you've just seen.

 The towering figure is Albrecht Dürer of Nuremberg, the greatest printmaker and one of the greatest
 painters of the northern Renaissance — a man who travelled to Italy himself to learn its theories of
 proportion, and carried them home. Around him you'll find Lucas Cranach the Elder, the painter of the
 German Reformation, with his cool, elegant nudes and sharp portraits, and other northern hands. What
 unites them is a different temperature from the Italians: a love of hard, minute, almost microscopic
 detail, of strange light, of individual faces rather than ideal ones.

 The painting to seek out is Dürer's, and there's a track for it. Find his Adoration of the Magi, and play
 the next track in front of it.
