 Caravaggio — Bacchus (in front of the work)
 WHERE YOU ARE
 Close in front of the Bacchus — near enough to read the wine, the fruit, and the surface of the glass.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 A bare-shouldered youth as Bacchus, draped in white, vine leaves and grapes in his dark hair, reclining
 behind a stone table; he pinches the stem of a glass of red wine and tilts it toward us; a carafe and a bowl of
 fruit sit before him, some of it overripe. Oil on canvas, around 1597.
 LISTEN FOR
 The details that reward standing close, and the secret found hidden inside the painting.


 Now that you're close, let this strange, seductive picture reveal itself, because it's a painting about
 looking — and about transience — hidden inside a scene of pure pleasure.

 Start with the wine glass he holds out toward you. Look at the surface of the wine: Caravaggio paints
 tiny ripples and concentric reflections in it, as if the boy's hand is faintly trembling, or the wine has only
 just been poured and hasn't settled. It's an invitation — he offers you the glass directly, drawing you into
 the picture as his drinking companion. Then look at the glass carafe on the table to the left: in its
 rounded surface there's a minuscule reflection, and conservators who studied the painting under
 magnification found, tucked into that highlight, a tiny image of a man at an easel — almost certainly
 Caravaggio himself, painting the very scene. He signed his presence into a reflection a few millimetres
 across, a private joke for anyone who looked closely enough.

 Then the fruit. Caravaggio was one of the first Italian painters to treat still-life with complete
 seriousness, and the bowl here is a marvel of observation — but look closely and several pieces are
 blemished, split, wormed, going soft, with a leaf curling and browning at the edge. In a painting all
 about youth, wine and sensual pleasure, that quiet decay is entirely deliberate: a whisper, even here, that
 beauty does not last, that this ripeness, this flushed young god offering you a drink, is already beginning
 to pass. The boy's heavy-lidded, slightly-drunk gaze and his outstretched glass invite you in; the rotting
 fruit reminds you the moment won't keep. That double note — sensual invitation and mortality, pleasure
 and its shadow, held together in one image — is pure Caravaggio, and it's why this teasing picture of a
 boy with a drink is far deeper than it first appears. Onward to the next room, and play the next track
 there.
