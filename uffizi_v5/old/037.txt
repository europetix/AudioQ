 The Contini Bonacossi Collection
 WHERE YOU ARE
 Entering the suite of rooms holding the Contini Bonacossi collection — a self-contained set of galleries on
 the first floor, hung and furnished differently from the rest of the museum.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 A complete private collection within the museum: paintings, sculpture, furniture and ceramics gathered by
 one twentieth-century connoisseur and shown together — gold-ground panels, Tuscan and Venetian masters,
 the Spanish school, and a Bernini, all in eight themed rooms.
 LISTEN FOR
 Where this collection came from, why it feels different from the rest of the Uffizi, and what to look for as you
 move through it.


 You've stepped into something different here — not another stretch of the Uffizi's own collection, but a
 whole private collection set within it, and it has its own flavour.

 It was assembled in the first half of the twentieth century by Alessandro Contini Bonacossi, one of the
 great collectors and art dealers of his age, a man with an extraordinary eye. After his death his family
 gave a core of the collection — around a hundred and forty works — to the Italian state, and after
 decades shown only by appointment in a quiet palace nearby, it was finally installed here, openly, in
 eight rooms, only a few years ago. Curators rate its best pieces as the equal of the Uffizi's own, and it
 deliberately fills gaps in the main collection — the Lombard school, and above all the Spanish masters
 of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, who are otherwise thin on the ground here.

 Because the donor wanted it kept together as he'd lived with it, you'll notice the display is unlike the rest
 of the museum: paintings hang near antique furniture, a whole room is given to majolica ceramics,
 another to sculpture. It feels less like a public gallery and more like walking through a great collector's
 home. This first room sets the tone with shimmering gold-ground panels, among them a jewel-like
 Madonna of the Snow by the Sienese master Sassetta. Take in the change of atmosphere, then move on.
