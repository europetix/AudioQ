 Rosso Fiorentino — Moses Defending the
 Daughters of Jethro
 WHERE YOU ARE
 In front of Rosso's Moses — a panel filled almost edge to edge with violently entangled, brightly lit nude
 bodies.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 A knot of muscular male nudes crashing together in the foreground as Moses beats off the shepherds
 attacking Jethro's daughters at a well; sharp, almost metallic light; vivid, clashing colours; the daughters off to
 the sides; little depth — the bodies are pressed flat against the picture plane. Oil on canvas, around 1523.
 LISTEN FOR
 Why this looks so shockingly modern, what Rosso is deliberately doing to you, and where his rage took him.


 This is Rosso Fiorentino's Moses Defending the Daughters of Jethro, from around fifteen twenty-three
 — one of the most startling, almost violent paintings in the entire gallery. It can look shockingly modern
 even now, and that is no accident.

 The biblical story behind it is small — Moses driving off the shepherds who were harassing the
 daughters of Jethro at a well — but Rosso turns it into a brawl of bodies. Look at how the foreground is
 jammed with muscular male nudes, crashing into one another, limbs tangled and overlapping, one figure
 sprawled head-first toward you, another arched backward in mid-fall. There is almost no depth: Rosso
 presses the whole fight flat up against the surface of the picture, so the bodies seem to spill out of the
 frame and into your own space. The light is hard and metallic, carving each muscle into a sharp ridge,
 and the colours are deliberately harsh — acid pinks, cold blues, sour greens — the exact opposite of
 Andrea del Sarto's warm, smoky harmony.

 This is Mannerism at its most aggressive. Rosso has taken everything the High Renaissance perfected
 — anatomy, composition, colour, the heroic nude — and weaponised it, choosing dissonance over
 beauty and shock over calm. A generation that knew it could never out-perfect Raphael chose instead to
 disturb, and few did it more fiercely than Rosso does here. Notice, too, how he keeps your eye agitated:
 there is no single resting point, no clear hero-and-villain reading, just a churn of straining flesh.

 Hold this picture in mind, because its restless energy had a long afterlife. A few years later Rosso left
 Italy for the French court at Fontainebleau, where he helped invent a whole elegant, elongated French
 version of this style and seeded the Renaissance in the north. The shock you feel standing here travelled
 all the way to the palaces of France. The next track is in the room that follows — start it when you
 arrive.
