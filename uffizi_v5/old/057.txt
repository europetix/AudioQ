 The Room of the Counter-Reformation
 WHERE YOU ARE
 A room of later sixteenth-century religious painting shaped by the Catholic Counter-Reformation.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 Devotional painting from the decades after the Council of Trent, when the Catholic Church laid down new
 rules for religious art — clearer, more decorous, more emotionally direct images designed to move ordinary
 worshippers and answer the Protestant Reformation.
 LISTEN FOR
 How religion reshaped art in the later 1500s, what changed on the wall, and where it leads.


 This room marks a turning point that reshaped European art for more than a century — the Catholic
 Counter-Reformation — and it's worth pausing to understand it, because it quietly explains much of
 what comes next.

 By the middle of the fifteen-hundreds the Catholic Church was fighting back hard against the Protestant
 Reformation, and at the great Council of Trent it laid down firm new rules for religious painting. Sacred
 art was to be clear, correct and decorous: no confusing Mannerist cleverness, no distracting nudity,
 nothing ambiguous or fanciful that might mislead ordinary believers — and, above all, it was to move
 people, to stir genuine devotion in worshippers who often couldn't read a word and learned their faith
 from images. Look at the difference from Rosso's shocking tangle of nudes a few rooms back: these
 later religious works are calmer, more legible, more openly and directly emotional, composed so a
 person kneeling in a side chapel grasps the story and feels it in an instant.

 That new demand — for religious images that are both doctrinally correct and powerfully, sincerely
 moving — leads straight toward the drama of the Baroque and the thunderclap of Caravaggio, waiting
 in the seventeenth-century rooms ahead. Note the new clarity and warmth of feeling here, the turn away
 from artifice toward the heart, then move on. Into the next room next, and play the next track as you step
 in.
