 Rembrandt, Rubens and Van Dyck
 WHERE YOU ARE
 The final room of the tour — the great painters of the Protestant and Catholic north: Rembrandt, Rubens and
 Van Dyck.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 The masters of the seventeenth-century north: Peter Paul Rubens and Anthony van Dyck of Catholic
 Flanders, all swagger and rich colour, and Rembrandt of Protestant Holland — represented here by
 self-portraits painted at opposite ends of his life.
 LISTEN FOR
 Why the gallery ends here, the pair of paintings to seek out, and what they quietly say about a whole life.


 This is the last room of the tour, and a fitting place to end — with the giants of the seventeenth-century
 north, and a quiet, profound meditation on the passage of a single human life.

 From Catholic Flanders come two of the era's titans: Rubens, with his tumbling, full-blooded energy, his
 warm flesh and billowing movement, and his brilliant pupil Anthony van Dyck, the most elegant
 aristocratic portraitist in all of Europe, who would go on to define how kings and courtiers wished to be
 seen. But seek out, above all, the Rembrandts — and notice the extraordinary way the Uffizi shows him
 to you, through self-portraits painted decades apart. In one he is young, confident, prosperous, a rising
 star of Amsterdam at the height of his fame. In another he is old, having outlived his wealth, his wife
 and his fashionable success, bankrupt and bereaved — his face worn, shadowed, and utterly without
 vanity, looking back at you with unbearable honesty.

 Stand between the two faces and you can read an entire lifetime in one artist's unflinching gaze —
 Rembrandt's lifelong, courageous study of his own ageing self, the most honest self-examination in the
 history of painting. It is a deeply human note to finish on, and a fitting close to six centuries of looking.
 Take your time with the young face and the old. Then, when you're ready to leave, make your way
 toward the exit, and play the final track on your way out.
