 Lorenzo Monaco & Gentile da Fabriano —
 International Gothic
 WHERE YOU ARE
 The International Gothic room, dominated by one very large, very golden altarpiece, with bright panels by
 Lorenzo Monaco nearby.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 Sumptuous, courtly painting from around 1400-1425: Lorenzo Monaco's clear, brilliant tempera panels — a
 monk-painter of the Camaldolese order — and the room's showpiece, Gentile da Fabriano's enormous gold
 'Adoration of the Magi.'
 LISTEN FOR
 What 'International Gothic' means in one room, who Lorenzo Monaco was, and the painting to spend real
 time in front of.


 Just before the Renaissance proper cracks open, painting takes one last, deep, glorious breath of the
 Middle Ages — and this is the room where you can stand inside it.

 This is International Gothic: the elegant, courtly style that travelled right across Europe around fourteen
 hundred, the same language being spoken in Paris, Prague, Milan and Florence — all rich fabrics,
 graceful S-curved poses, jewel colour, gold, and a love of exquisite detail for its own sake. Look first at
 Lorenzo Monaco's panels, with their clean, almost luminous colour and their slender, swaying figures.
 He was a monk of the Camaldolese order as well as a painter — Lorenzo 'the Monk' is literally what his
 name means — and he's the great Florentine voice of this style.

 But the room belongs to one painting, and you'll know it the moment you turn around: Gentile da
 Fabriano's Adoration of the Magi, a wall of gold and pageantry, the single most spectacular thing the
 International Gothic ever produced. Go and stand close in front of it, and play the next track.
