 LISTEN FOR
 How a Greek in Venice became one of the strangest painters in history, caught here at the very start.


 A small room, but a remarkable presence: El Greco, one of the most singular painters who ever lived,
 caught here near the very beginning of his long journey.

 He was born on the island of Crete and trained first as a Greek icon painter, in the flat, golden, devout
 Byzantine tradition — a world away from the Renaissance. Then, hungry for more, he came to Venice
 and to Rome in the fifteen-sixties and seventies, and the work in this room belongs to that Italian
 education, when he was hungrily soaking up Titian's colour and, above all, Tintoretto's restless,
 shadow-struck drama. You can already sense something unusual stirring in it: a nervous energy, an
 unexpected light, a willingness to stretch and distort a figure for sheer emotional effect rather than
 correctness.

 Only later, after he settled in the Spanish city of Toledo, would that grow into his unmistakable mature
 style — elongated, flickering, flame-like figures stretched upward toward heaven, lit by a cold, silvery,
 unearthly light, faces rapt with spiritual intensity. It was so strange that for centuries afterward he was
 dismissed as merely eccentric, even mad — and then, in the twentieth century, suddenly hailed as a
 visionary, a forerunner of Expressionism and modern art. Look here for the first seeds of all of that, in
 the Venetian-trained Greek before he became fully himself. Through the doorway, the next track is
 waiting in the room beyond.
