 Cimabue, Duccio, Giotto — the Room of the
 Maestà
 WHERE YOU ARE
 The large room where three monumental altarpieces face each other across the floor — one on each of three
 walls, each well over three metres tall.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 Three versions of the same subject — the Virgin and Child enthroned in majesty, a 'Maestà' — by three
 painters within about a single generation: Cimabue (right), Duccio (left), and Giotto (centre, in the place of
 honour). Gold grounds, angels, prophets beneath the thrones.
 LISTEN FOR
 Why these three are deliberately staged together, and the order to take them in so the change is visible.


 This is one of the most carefully arranged rooms in the whole building, and one of the most rewarding,
 because it isn't just a display — it's an argument, staged in paint.

 Three paintings, all the same subject: the Virgin and Child enthroned in majesty, what's called a Maestà.
 All three are enormous, all three sit on gold, and all three were made within roughly thirty years of one
 another, around twelve eighty to thirteen ten. Hung together like this, they let you watch painting
 change its mind in real time.

 On the right is Cimabue, the great old master, working at the very summit of the Byzantine tradition you
 just saw next door. On the left is Duccio, the founder of the Sienese school — still steeped in gold and
 elegance, but loosening it. And in the centre, in the place of honour, is Giotto, the painter who broke the
 rules: whose Virgin has real bodily weight, whose throne has real depth you feel you could walk into.
 Everything you'll see on this floor for the rest of the morning grows out of the shift that happens
 between these three walls.

 There's a separate track for each painting. Take them in the order that makes the change visible:
 Cimabue first, on the right, then Duccio on the left, then Giotto in the centre last. Go and stand in front
 of the Cimabue, and play the next track.
