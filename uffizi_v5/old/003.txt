 Thirteenth-Century Painting — Where the Story
 Starts
 WHERE YOU ARE
 In the first painting room off the corridor, hung with large gold-ground panels — Madonnas and painted
 crucifixes from the twelve and thirteen hundreds.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 Devotional panels on wood, their backgrounds solid gold leaf: enthroned Madonnas, a large painted Cross or
 two, ranks of small saints. The figures are frontal, elongated, outlined in dark line, with little sense of weight
 or of real space.
 LISTEN FOR
 What this kind of painting was for, why it looks the way it does, and exactly what to carry into the next room.


 You're at the beginning, in every sense, because the gallery is arranged so that you walk forward
 through time — and this is the oldest room of all.

 Look at what these paintings have in common: gold. The backgrounds are solid gold leaf, beaten thin
 and burnished, and the figures stand against it without any landscape, any room, any sky. The Madonnas
 face you straight on, long and narrow, their features drawn in firm dark outlines, their robes folding in
 stylised gold lines rather than real cloth. Nobody here has much weight. Nobody quite stands in a space
 you could enter.

 That isn't failure — it's the whole intention. This is painting inherited from Byzantium, from the
 Greek-speaking Christian East, and its job was never to show you the visible world. It was to open a
 window onto an unchanging heaven: the gold is divine light, not afternoon sun, and the stillness is the
 stillness of eternity. These were objects to pray in front of, lit by candles, glowing in the dark of a
 church.

 Hold three things in your eye as you leave: the flat gold, the frontal pose, the absence of weight.
 Because next door, three enormous paintings of exactly this subject are going to take that inherited
 tradition and start pulling it apart — and you'll only see how radical that is if you remember what it
 looked like here first. The next room is just ahead. Walk in, and play the next track.
