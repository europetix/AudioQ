 Filippo Lippi — Madonna and Child with Two
 Angels (the Lippina)
 WHERE YOU ARE
 In front of the Lippina — a fairly small panel: a young Madonna at prayer, the Child held up by two angels
 before her, a window onto a landscape behind.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 A delicate young Madonna with elaborately dressed hair and a near-transparent halo sits before an open
 window; two angels lift the Christ Child toward her, and the nearest — a cheeky boy — grins and looks
 straight out at us; behind, a window opens onto a pale rocky landscape. Tempera on panel, around 1450-65.
 LISTEN FOR
 Why this Madonna felt revolutionary, the grinning angel, the window behind her, and the scandalous love
 story in her face.


 This is the Lippina, Filippo Lippi's Madonna and Child with Two Angels, from the middle of the
 fifteenth century — and after the solemn, golden Madonnas of the floor above, it can stop you in your
 tracks with its sheer humanity.

 Look at the Madonna first. She isn't a remote queen of heaven on a gold throne; she's a beautiful,
 fashionable young Florentine woman, her hair elaborately braided, looped with pearls and a gauzy veil,
 her halo so fine it's almost invisible, sitting quietly by a window with her hands pressed together in
 prayer. She could be someone Lippi passed in the street that morning. Then look at the two angels
 lifting the Child up toward her — and especially the one in front, a mischievous boy who turns and grins
 broadly straight out at you, breaking the fourth wall five centuries before anyone named it. It's playful,
 warm, completely unprecedented in a sacred image, and it pulls you into the family group as if you were
 standing in the room.

 Behind them, through the window, opens a pale rocky landscape — one of the first times a Florentine
 Madonna is set against a real, deep view of the world rather than flat gold, the air softening as it recedes.
 And there's a human story folded into all this tenderness. Lippi was a Carmelite friar, sworn to celibacy,
 but he fell in love with a young nun, Lucrezia Buti, ran off with her, and had a son — Filippino Lippi,
 who became a fine painter in his own right. Tradition holds that Lucrezia was the model for this very
 Madonna, which would make this serene image of holy motherhood a quiet, tender portrait of
 forbidden, real-life love. You can feel that warmth in it. And you can see the whole future here, too:
 Lippi was Botticelli's teacher, and the grace of the Birth of Venus begins with faces and gestures like
 these. Through the doorway, the next track is waiting in the room beyond.
