 The Eastern Corridor — The Spine of the Gallery
 WHERE YOU ARE
 In the long Eastern Corridor: a tall, straight gallery with windows down one side and the painting rooms
 opening off the other, ancient statues lining the walls.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 A corridor running the full length of one wing — a painted, vaulted ceiling covered end to end in delicate
 'grotesque' decoration, ranks of ancient Roman marble busts and statues along the walls, and tall windows
 looking over the courtyard toward the Arno.
 LISTEN FOR
 What these corridors are, the ceiling worth tipping your head back for, and how to use the corridor as you go.


 Before you dive into the painting rooms, look at where you're walking, because the corridor itself is one
 of the oldest parts of the museum and one of the easiest to march straight through without noticing.

 This is the Eastern Corridor, one of three that Vasari ran around the U of the building in the
 fifteen-sixties. He designed them as long, uninterrupted perspectives, and the Medici quickly filled them
 with their collection of ancient sculpture — the marble emperors, gods and athletes you can see ranked
 along the walls, many of them brought from Rome. For centuries this corridor of antiquities was the
 gallery; the rooms full of paintings opened off it as a kind of bonus.

 Now tip your head back. The entire vaulted ceiling is covered in what's called grotesque decoration —
 painted in the fifteen-eighties, a fantasy of scrolls, masks, little creatures, garlands and medallions,
 named after the ancient Roman wall-paintings that had recently been dug up underground, in 'grottoes.'
 It runs unbroken for the whole length, and most visitors never look up at it once.

 Through the windows on one side you get your first views down into the narrow courtyard and out
 toward the river. Use the corridor as your reset between rooms — step back into it, take a breath, look
 up, then turn into the next painting room. The first one is just off the corridor. Turn into it and play the
 next track.
