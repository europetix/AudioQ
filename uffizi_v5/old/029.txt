 Leonardo da Vinci — The Annunciation
 WHERE YOU ARE
 In front of the Annunciation — a wide, low panel: the angel kneeling at the left in a garden, the Virgin at the
 right at a marble lectern, a misty landscape between them.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 The angel Gabriel kneels in a walled garden, wings raised, a lily in his hand, fresh grass dotted with flowers
 beneath him; the Virgin sits at the right behind an ornate marble lectern, one hand raised, one in her book;
 behind them a row of dark cypress-like trees and, beyond, a hazy harbour and mountains dissolving into pale
 blue. Oil and tempera, early 1470s.
 LISTEN FOR
 What's already unmistakably Leonardo here, the marble lectern's real-world model, and the famous 'mistake'
 worth finding.


 This is Leonardo's Annunciation, painted in his early twenties, around fourteen seventy-two — an early
 work, but with the future already breaking through in every corner.

 Start with the things only Leonardo was doing. Look at the grass beneath the angel: it isn't a flat green
 carpet but a meadow of individual blades and carefully observed flowers, the work of a man who would
 fill notebooks with botanical drawings. Look at the angel's wings — modelled on the wings of real
 birds, not the usual decorative feathers. And look, above all, into the far distance between the two
 figures: trees, then a misty harbour with faint ships, then pale mountains melting into the blue air. That
 softening of everything as it recedes — atmospheric perspective, the truth that distance is hazy and
 bluish — is something Leonardo would spend his whole life perfecting, and it's already here in his
 twenties.

 Look, too, at the elaborately carved marble lectern in front of the Virgin. Leonardo modelled it closely
 on a real object he knew well — a marble tomb his own master Verrocchio had carved for the Medici in
 the church of San Lorenzo — so a real Florentine monument is hidden inside this sacred scene.

 Now the famous puzzle. Look at the Virgin's right arm, resting on the lectern. It seems too long — her
 hand reaches an oddly far distance. Scholars argue endlessly: a young painter's slip, or a deliberate
 adjustment, calculated to look exactly right when the panel was hung high on a church wall and seen
 from below and to the side. Either way, it's a reminder that you're watching an artist still working things
 out, in public. Sit with the calm and the strangeness of it, then turn to the unfinished Adoration and play
 the next track.
