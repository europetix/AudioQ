 Toward Mannerism — and Down to the First Floor
 WHERE YOU ARE
 The last rooms of the second floor, gathering early-sixteenth-century painters who carry the story toward
 Mannerism — and toward the staircase down.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 A mixed room of early-1500s painters: Franciabigio and Granacci of Florence, the Spaniard Alonso
 Berruguete, and the Sienese Domenico Beccafumi — work in which the calm of the High Renaissance begins
 to bend toward the restless elegance of Mannerism.
 LISTEN FOR
 How this room hinges between two ages, and exactly where you go next.


 These last rooms of the second floor are a hinge, and worth a real moment before you go down, because
 you can feel the High Renaissance starting to bend out of true.

 The painters gathered here all worked in the early fifteen-hundreds, in the long shadow of Raphael,
 Michelangelo and Andrea del Sarto. Franciabigio and Francesco Granacci are solid Florentines —
 Granacci was a boyhood friend of Michelangelo, and worked alongside him as a young man. But look at
 the Spaniard Alonso Berruguete and the Sienese Domenico Beccafumi, and you start to see something
 new creeping in: elongated figures, strange acid colours, restless and unstable poses, an elegance that
 prizes artifice and difficulty over calm. This is the beginning of Mannerism — the deliberately
 sophisticated, unsettled style that follows the perfect balance of the High Renaissance, as if a generation
 that knew it could never out-perfect Raphael decided to be strange and brilliant instead. Berruguete
 would carry these ideas home and help launch the style in Spain; Beccafumi became the great Mannerist
 of Siena.

 And that restless new spirit is exactly where the rest of the gallery goes. You've now walked the entire
 second floor — the Renaissance from a flat gold ground all the way to its towering summit. From here
 the route heads down: take the Lanzi staircase to the first floor, where the rest of the collection waits —
 two further galleries the Medici and their heirs gathered, and then the great rooms of the sixteenth and
 seventeenth centuries. Play the next track when you reach the foot of the stairs.
