 Botticelli — The Birth of Venus
 WHERE YOU ARE
 In front of the Birth of Venus — a wide canvas, almost three metres across, so step back to let the whole
 scene open out.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 The goddess stands nude on a giant scallop shell, drifting to shore; at the left, the wind-god Zephyr, entwined
 with a breeze-nymph, blows her in on a shower of roses; at the right, one of the Hours rushes forward to wrap
 her in a flower-strewn cloak. Tempera on canvas, mid-1480s.
 LISTEN FOR
 Why a nude pagan goddess on this scale was almost unthinkable, what Botticelli was really painting, and the
 'flaws' that aren't flaws.


 Here she is, painted in the mid fourteen-eighties — and it's worth pausing on how startling this was. A
 nude female figure, near life-size, at the dead centre of an enormous picture, in the heart of devoutly
 Christian Florence. There had been almost nothing like it since antiquity, a thousand years before.

 Read it left to right, like the breeze itself. On the left, the blue-green wind-god Zephyr, tangled with a
 nymph, blows Venus toward the land on a drift of pink roses — watch how the roses tumble and turn
 through the air, each one outlined. Venus stands on a scallop shell, just born from the sea, fully grown,
 balanced in that famous impossible pose, weight resting on no real foot, her long hair streaming down to
 cover her, utterly serene in the middle of all that motion. On the right, one of the Hours, the spirits of the
 seasons, hurries to clothe her in a mantle scattered with flowers, the cloth lifting in the same wind.

 Now stop looking for realism, because you won't find it, and it isn't the point. Her neck is too long. Her
 left shoulder slopes away impossibly. Her left arm couldn't really hang where it does. She doesn't stand
 so much as hover. None of this is a mistake — Botticelli isn't painting a real body in real light. He's
 painting an idea, drawn from the Neoplatonic philosophy fashionable in the Medici circle: beauty itself
 arriving in the world, earthly loveliness as the first rung on a ladder toward the divine. It's line and grace
 and rhythm raised over weight and muscle — the exact opposite of where Michelangelo will take
 painting at the far end of this floor. Notice, too, the surface: this is one of the first great paintings on
 canvas rather than wood, which is part of why it looks so pale and matte, almost like a fresco or a
 tapestry. For now, just let it be beautiful. When you're ready, turn to the Primavera and play the next
 track.
