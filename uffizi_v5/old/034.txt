 The Niobe Room
 WHERE YOU ARE
 A large, grand neoclassical hall, white and gold, ringed with ancient marble statues of falling and fleeing
 figures.
 WHAT YOU SEE
 A spacious 18th-century hall built to display a group of ancient sculptures: Niobe and her children, shown at
 the moment they are struck down by the gods. White-and-gold stucco walls, a coffered vault, the marble
 figures arranged around the room mid-flight and mid-fall.
 LISTEN FOR
 The myth in the marble, the room's own near-death, and how to read the scene around you.


 Walk into the middle of this grand white-and-gold hall and turn slowly all the way around, because the
 room is telling a single terrible story in marble, all at once, surrounding you.

 The statues are the children of Niobe, an ancient queen who boasted that she had borne more children
 than the goddess Latona — and was punished for that pride when Latona's twins, the archers Apollo and
 Diana, shot down her sons and daughters one by one. The figures around you are frozen at that very
 moment: running, twisting, stumbling, falling, a mother trying to shield her last child under her cloak,
 faces turned up toward the unseen arrows raining from above. They are Roman copies of lost Greek
 originals, dug up in Rome in the fifteen-eighties and brought to Florence, and this elegant neoclassical
 hall was built in the seventeen-eighties expressly to show them as a single, unfolding dramatic group.

 And there's one more thing to know, standing here. In nineteen ninety-three a Mafia car bomb exploded
 in the street just behind the Uffizi, killing five people and badly damaging this very room — its frescoed
 vault, its statues, its decoration. What you're standing in has been painstakingly restored since. So the
 room holds two tragedies at once: the ancient myth in the marble, and the modern wound the building
 itself survived and healed. Take the whole scene in, then move on through the last rooms of this floor.
